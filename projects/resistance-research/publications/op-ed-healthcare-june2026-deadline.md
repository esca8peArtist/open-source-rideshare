## Six Weeks to Save Five Million People's Health Insurance

On June 1, 2026 — six weeks from now — a deadline will arrive that most Americans have never heard of and that the people responsible for it would prefer you not notice.

That is the date by which the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services must issue implementation guidance for the Medicaid work requirements enacted in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law on July 4, 2025. What CMS writes in that guidance — which exemptions it permits, how broadly it defines qualifying activities, how much administrative documentation it requires of states — will determine whether this provision strips health insurance from five million people or from a considerably smaller number.

This is not a hypothetical. This is a decision, being made right now, by a specific agency, with a specific deadline. And there is still time to influence it.

---

The United States entered 2026 already in the middle of a coverage crisis. Before the work requirements take effect, 27.1 million Americans lacked health insurance — a number that began rising sharply after the enhanced premium tax credits enacted under the Inflation Reduction Act expired on December 31, 2025. Those credits had held down marketplace premiums for 22 million people. Without them, premiums have more than doubled for many enrollees. By March 2026, CNBC polling found that 9 percent of 2025 marketplace enrollees had already become uninsured after not renewing. The Urban Institute projects 4.8 million people losing marketplace coverage this year alone.

Into that already-worsening picture, the OBBBA dropped the largest Medicaid rollback in American history. The law requires that Medicaid expansion enrollees — adults aged 19 to 64 — complete 80 hours per month of qualifying "community engagement activities": employment, job training, education, or volunteering. States must implement this by January 1, 2027.

The Congressional Budget Office projects that work requirements alone will cause 5.2 million adults to lose Medicaid coverage by 2034. Of those, 4.8 million will become entirely uninsured. Here is the detail that makes this genuinely brutal: the law specifically bars anyone who loses Medicaid coverage for failing to meet work requirements from receiving marketplace premium tax credits. The administration closed the exit. There is no fallback.

And the uninsured population is already projected to reach 31 million or higher by 2027.

---

Work requirements sound, in the abstract, like a reasonable proposition. In practice, they are an administrative trap.

The people most likely to be caught by them are not people who choose not to work. They are caregivers looking after children or elderly parents, people with intermittent employment in seasonal or gig economies, people in rural areas where the nearest qualifying job training program is 45 miles away, people with health conditions that are not severe enough to qualify as disabilities but that make maintaining 80 hours of documented monthly activity genuinely difficult. A 2019 federal court ruling, Gresham v. Azar, struck down an earlier version of Medicaid work requirements in Arkansas precisely because the evidence showed they were eliminating coverage for people who were already working or were legally exempt — not because of sloth but because of documentation failures and bureaucratic barriers.

Arkansas's work requirement, in the year it operated before courts suspended it, resulted in 18,000 people losing Medicaid coverage. Not one study found evidence that it increased employment.

---

This is where the June 1 guidance matters.

CMS is not powerless in how it writes that guidance. A guidance document that defines exemptions broadly — caregivers, students, people in rural shortage areas, people with irregular employment patterns — can meaningfully reduce coverage losses while technically complying with the law. A guidance document that mandates extensive documentation and narrow exemptions will produce the worst-case outcome the CBO projected.

The administration has every incentive to write narrow guidance. The question is whether the counterpressure is sufficient to widen it.

That pressure can come from three directions that have historically influenced CMS guidance documents. Governors of states that expanded Medicaid — including Republican governors in states like Montana, Indiana, and Ohio, where work requirements will directly affect hundreds of thousands of constituents — carry disproportionate weight when they write to CMS directly. Hospital associations and rural health systems, whose patient volumes and financial viability depend on covered patients, have established standing to comment. And disability rights organizations, whose legal arguments under the ADA and due process doctrine are already being prepared for litigation challenging implementation, have a track record of shifting guidance through formal comment.

CMS accepts public comments through regulations.gov. The agency is required by the Administrative Procedure Act to respond to substantive comments in its final guidance. This is not petitioning — it is the legal mechanism designed to ensure agency accountability. The deadline is six weeks away.

---

The Prior Authorization Reform Act, separately, has 248 House co-sponsors and 64 Senate co-sponsors — a bipartisan supermajority that includes members from across the ideological spectrum, united by the experience of watching insurers delay or deny care for patients and their own families. The only thing standing between that bill and a floor vote is the Senate Finance Committee's scheduling calendar. If you have a senator on that committee, that is the call to make.

But the June 1 deadline is closer and more immediate. It does not require legislative action. It does not require a changed majority. It requires the agency to receive enough substantive input from people with standing that broad exemptions become the more defensible legal and political position.

Five million people losing health insurance is not an abstraction. It is the number the CBO derived from projecting what happens when 80-hour monthly documentation requirements are applied to the actual population of Medicaid expansion enrollees — people with caregiving responsibilities, episodic employment, chronic conditions managed but not disabling, lives that do not fit cleanly into bureaucratic categories. The administrative mechanism that strips their coverage will be set by a document published in the next six weeks.

Comment. Organize. Call the governors' offices. Write the hospitals. Make the guidance document politically expensive to write narrowly.

Six weeks is enough time.

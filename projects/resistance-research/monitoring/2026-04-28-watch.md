## 1. Abrego Garcia / Xinis — April 28 Hearing

### Pre-Hearing State (April 23 baseline)

**Deposition deadline (April 23)**:
Xinis ordered four Trump administration officials — those who signed affidavits in the case — to complete depositions by April 23. As of the preparation of this brief, completion status is unconfirmed. Two scenarios:

- **Scenario A — Depositions completed**: Government officials sat for depositions. This is the least-bad outcome for the administration; it represents compliance, even if the sworn testimony is evasive or incomplete. Xinis may still find the responses inadequate at the hearing, but the administration can argue procedural compliance.
- **Scenario B — Depositions refused or obstructed**: Administration officials declined to sit, asserted privilege, or provided non-answers. This is the higher-escalation scenario. It adds a second layer to the "bad faith" finding already on record and materially accelerates the contempt timeline. A refusal to comply with a deposition order — as opposed to failing to return Abrego Garcia — is a cleaner contempt trigger because it directly defies a specific court order rather than raising foreign-affairs discretion arguments.

**Substantive posture**:
- DOJ's April 20 brief maintained the Liberia deportation demand with no retreat. ICE Director Todd Lyons filed that Liberia is the administration's preferred removal country, calling Costa Rica an alternative that would be "prejudicial" to the United States.
- Xinis's April 8 "fantasy" observation — that the administration cannot simultaneously demand to deport someone and prosecute them criminally — was never addressed in the April 20 brief.
- The Nashville criminal prosecution against Abrego Garcia continues, creating the structural incoherence Xinis identified: he cannot be deported to Liberia and tried in Nashville simultaneously.

**Boasberg circuit precedent**:
On April 14, the DC Circuit (Rao + Walker) terminated Boasberg's criminal contempt inquiry. The administration has a new circuit-level argument: Rao-Walker held that criminal contempt in the executive deportation-defiance context constitutes an "unwarranted impairment" of executive power. However:

1. The Boasberg proceeding was **criminal contempt** in an emergency-order context (AEA deportation flights to El Salvador). Xinis's contempt threat is **civil contempt** in an injunction-enforcement proceeding — procedurally distinct.
2. Civil contempt is coercive, not punitive; it is designed to compel compliance, not to punish past defiance. Courts have historically been more resistant to executive arguments against civil contempt than criminal.
3. The Fourth Circuit (where the Abrego Garcia case sits) is not bound by DC Circuit precedent. The Boasberg ruling is persuasive, not controlling.
4. If the administration files a Fourth Circuit emergency appeal, the Fourth Circuit's receptivity to the Boasberg argument is the key variable.

**Return obligation**:
SCOTUS's unanimous April 10 order held that the government must "facilitate" Abrego Garcia's return — not merely "not oppose" it. The administration's interpretation of "facilitate" has never aligned with Xinis's. At the April 28 hearing, Xinis may ask DOJ to explain specifically what steps were taken to facilitate return, and the distance between what the administration claims and what SCOTUS required will be on the record.

### April 28 Hearing — What to Watch

**1. Deposition outcome admission**
At the opening of the hearing, Xinis will likely ask about deposition completion. The government's answer — completed, refused, partially completed, privilege-asserted — sets the entire tone.

**2. Civil contempt timeline**
If Xinis is moving toward civil contempt, the April 28 hearing may produce:
- A **show cause order** — giving DOJ a short deadline to demonstrate compliance or face contempt
- A direct **civil contempt finding** — ordering sanctions until the administration facilitates return
- A **civil contempt motion filing order** — directing Abrego Garcia's attorneys to formally file, which would initiate a contempt proceeding with full briefing

Civil contempt sanctions in an injunction context typically include: daily fines, imprisonment of officials, or appointment of a special master. The administration would immediately appeal any contempt order to the Fourth Circuit.

**3. Administration's Boasberg argument**
Watch for whether DOJ cites Rao-Walker at the April 28 hearing. If they argue that Xinis's contempt threat is foreclosed by the Boasberg ruling, that is a direct escalation: they are asserting circuit precedent strips Xinis of her enforcement authority. This argument, if made, will define the Fourth Circuit emergency appeal.

**4. Liberia position maintenance or retreat**
Any retreat from the Liberia demand — even a tentative one — is significant. Costa Rica has already signed a removal agreement with the administration. The Liberia insistence, in the absence of a Liberia-specific rationale beyond resource investment, looks like deliberate obstruction in the judicial record.

**5. Fourth Circuit emergency filing**
An immediate administration appeal to the Fourth Circuit — whether of a contempt finding, a show cause order, or even the return obligation itself — would remove the case from Xinis's jurisdiction temporarily. This is the most aggressive post-hearing escalation scenario and would likely trigger a circuit stay application and expedited briefing schedule.

**6. Nashville incoherence**
Any Crenshaw docket activity before April 28 directly affects what Xinis can do. A Crenshaw dismissal of the Nashville prosecution removes the "being tried here" argument that partially explains DOJ's non-return posture. A Crenshaw ruling denying dismissal strengthens DOJ's claim that Nashville requires Abrego Garcia's presence.

### The Structural Linkage

Judge Crenshaw's ruling on the vindictive-prosecution dismissal motion is analytically linked to April 28 regardless of whether it is timed that way. If Crenshaw dismisses the Nashville charges before April 28, it:

1. Removes the "Nashville prosecution requires U.S. presence" argument from DOJ's Xinis brief posture.
2. Eliminates the criminal leverage over Abrego Garcia that the Blanche-public-statement record documents.
3. Creates a gap in DOJ's justification for the Liberia demand — if there is no U.S. prosecution, no "government interest" argument survives beyond the Liberia resource-investment claim.

Nine weeks of silence after a fully-submitted hearing is analytically notable. The April 28 Xinis hearing may be Crenshaw's actual trigger: if Xinis issues contempt and the case escalates, Crenshaw's continued silence becomes untenable either way.

### Watch For

- Any Nashville docket entry published on PACER before or on April 28.
- A dismissal order from Crenshaw — even without prejudice — is an immediate-read development.
- A subpoena order directed at Blanche regarding his public statements linking the civil and criminal cases would be extraordinary and immediately escalatory.
- A denial of the dismissal motion would strengthen DOJ's Xinis posture and likely be cited by DOJ at the April 28 hearing.

## 3. May Day Lead-Up — April 28 (Workers Memorial Day)

April 28 is Workers Memorial Day, the annual day commemorating workers killed or injured on the job. AFL-CIO has toolkit and events live. The symbolic alignment — Workers Memorial Day April 28, May Day Mass Call April 29 (7:30pm), May Day actions May 1 — is intentional organizer framing.

This creates a three-day labor escalation arc: commemoration → national coordination → action.

### What to Watch

No such action has been confirmed as of April 23.

**AFL-CIO national endorsement**: The limiting factor distinguishing this coalition from a classic general strike is the absence of a formal national AFL-CIO federation call. If AFL-CIO announces national participation on or before April 28, the character of May 1 shifts materially toward a general strike rather than a civic/coalition action.

**Scale signals**: Any reports of employer reactions — labor injunctions sought, termination threats, government contractor compliance concerns — indicate the strike is being taken seriously as an economic disruption. Absence of such reactions suggests the economic disruption component remains symbolic.

**Chicago/Teacher precedent**: The Chicago Teachers Union/CPS subsidization of teacher participation (Turley op-ed, April 21) created a test case for publicly-funded May Day participation. Watch for any federal or state response that attempts to claw back public funding or discipline participating teachers.

## Analytical Note: The Enforcement Infrastructure Question

April 28 is the clearest test case to date of whether federal courts retain coercive enforcement authority over executive action in the immigration context.

The structural problem has two layers:

1. **Circuit precedent (Boasberg)**: The DC Circuit Rao-Walker ruling limits criminal contempt of court against executive officials. This precedent now exists and the administration will cite it.

2. **Civil contempt path**: Xinis's civil contempt authority is structurally different. The coercive nature of civil contempt — fines that accumulate until compliance, potential imprisonment of non-complying officials — is harder to frame as an "unwarranted impairment of executive power" when the underlying court order is a SCOTUS-affirmed injunction enforcement proceeding (not an emergency order as in Boasberg).

The test: if Xinis issues a civil contempt order and the Fourth Circuit immediately stays it on Boasberg grounds, the enforcement infrastructure conclusion is clear — courts cannot coerce executive compliance in removal proceedings. If the Fourth Circuit lets civil contempt proceed, the administration faces either compliance or an escalating-fines standoff.

The distinction between "win in court" (SCOTUS April 10 order) and "prevail in fact" (Abrego Garcia actually returned) is the central question of April 28.

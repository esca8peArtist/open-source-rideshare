# Alaska RCV Repeal — November 3, 2026
*Field Operations Research Brief — April 17, 2026*
*The highest-priority defensive ballot measure fight in electoral reform for 2026*

---

## BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT

Alaska's top-four ranked-choice voting system will be put to a repeal vote for the second time in three years on November 3, 2026. The 2024 defense won by 743 votes — 0.23% — the narrowest ballot measure result in Alaska history. The 2026 fight is essentially a replay of 2024 with three material differences: Trump is now publicly backing the repeal, seven other states repealed RCV on the same 2024 ballot while Alaska was the sole defense win, and the repeal campaign has built a more organized volunteer signature-gathering infrastructure. The defense must replicate or improve on 2024's $15 million campaign while closing the 744-vote gap that separated victory from loss. The marginal precinct analysis, the Alaska Native organizing anchor, and the Anchorage blue-shift are the three structural advantages the defense can exploit.

**This is the only field available right now to protect the most prominent real-world RCV model in the United States.** A repeal here removes the national proof-of-concept and provides a template for repeal campaigns in Maine and any other state where RCV survives.

## 1. THE 2024 VOTE — ANATOMY OF THE NEAR-MISS

### Final Certified Results

The November 2024 Ballot Measure 2 (repeal) was certified by Lt. Governor Nancy Dahlstrom following a mandatory recount completed December 9, 2024:

- **Yes (repeal):** 160,230 votes — 49.88%
- **No (keep):** 160,973 votes — 50.12%
- **Margin:** 743 votes
- **Total votes cast on the measure:** ~321,200 out of ~321,000 total ballots

This is the narrowest margin for any ballot measure in Alaska history. By comparison, the original Ballot Measure 2 that created the RCV system passed in November 2020 by 50.6% to 49.4% — also narrow, but more than 2,000 votes.

### What the Night Count Showed

The repeal was actually *leading* on election night, November 5, 2024, and held a lead of more than 4,000 votes for nearly two weeks. The final "no" victory came only as late-arriving ballots — predominantly mail-in, absentee, and rural ballots — were counted through mid-to-late November. This late-count pattern is important for field ops: absentee and mail-ballot programs disproportionately benefit the "no" side, and the victory came from ballots counted weeks after election night.

### Geographic Breakdown

Precinct-level data from the Alaska Division of Elections reveals a near-linear correlation between Republican presidential support and "yes" (repeal) voting. The regional picture:

**Strongly "yes" (repeal) regions:**
- **Matanuska-Susitna Borough (Mat-Su):** Republican registration runs 4:1 over Democrat. Mat-Su is the state's second-largest population center (~115,000 people), Trump country, and the single largest pro-repeal block. Republican presidential margins here exceeded 50 points in 2024. Ballot Measure 2 tracking showed near-identical partisan alignment. Mat-Su gave repeal its largest raw-vote advantage over the defense.
- **Kenai Peninsula Borough:** Republican registration runs 3:1 over Democrat. Another high-turnout, reliably conservative region that voted heavily for repeal.
- **Interior/Fairbanks North Star Borough:** Mixed but leaning repeal. The Fairbanks urban core has a university community (UAF) and some independent/Democratic voting, but the surrounding Interior is conservative rural territory. Net vote probably slight edge to repeal.

**Strongly "no" (keep RCV) regions:**
- **Municipality of Anchorage:** The critical swing block. Anchorage has been drifting Democratic since 2020 — Harris won the municipality over Trump in both 2020 and 2024, the latter representing the first back-to-back Democratic presidential performance in Anchorage in decades. The city's 16 state house members are roughly split, with several Republicans announcing intent to caucus with Democrats. The Anchorage "blue shift" is the single most important structural factor in the defense coalition. High-income South Anchorage precincts (House District 9) delivered 72%+ turnout.
- **Southeast Alaska (Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan, Petersburg):** Juneau is the state capital, heavily public-sector employed, and reliably Democratic. Southeast Alaska overall went "no" on repeal. These are mid-size communities, connected by ferry rather than road, with moderate-to-strong labor and government-sector voting patterns.
- **Alaska Native/Rural regions:** The most complex geography. Bush Alaska (the "Y-K Delta," Interior villages, Northwest Arctic, Kodiak Island outliers) votes heavily Democratic and Alaska Native in federal and statewide races. However, voter turnout in these districts is dramatically lower than urban Alaska — House District 40 (North Slope and Northwest Arctic) saw only 36% turnout in 2024, compared to 72% in South Anchorage. House Districts 37, 38, and 39 (predominantly Alaska Native) also fell below 45% turnout. The untapped "no" votes in rural Alaska are a primary defense target.

### What Drove the Near-Miss

Three factors explain why the 2020 RCV system passed more comfortably than the 2024 defense:

1. **National RCV headwinds:** In November 2024, RCV repeal or defeat-of-RCV-expansion measures passed in Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, and Oregon. Alaska was the only state where voters explicitly chose to keep an existing RCV system that was under repeal threat. The national wave created a "this is going down everywhere" perception that the defense campaign had to fight.

2. **Incumbency disadvantage of complexity:** The more voters used the system through 2022 and 2024, the more some formed negative opinions based on confusion about vote tabulation timing (results took 2+ weeks after election night due to ranked-choice counting of absentee ballots). The perception that results were "delayed" fed into the repeal messaging about transparency and confusion — even though the delay was from Alaska's late absentee-counting law, not RCV itself.

3. **Underfunded repeal, well-funded defense, but still nearly lost:** The defense campaign (No on 2) raised approximately $12 million, dwarfing the repeal campaign's roughly $120,000 in reported 2024 spending. Despite a roughly 100:1 funding advantage, the defense won by 743 votes. This is the most important data point for 2026: money alone is not sufficient. The underlying persuasion challenge is structural, not just financial.

**Key vulnerability identified:** The defense won primarily through late-counted mail and absentee ballots. A well-targeted mail ballot chase program could provide an additional buffer against the "election night lead" dynamic that the repeal side will again try to exploit for narrative purposes.

## 2. ALASKA RCV — SYSTEM DESCRIPTION AND ELECTORAL HISTORY

### What the System Does

Alaska's system, created by 2020 Ballot Measure 2, has two components that operate together:

**Top-Four Open Primary:** All candidates from all parties run in a single primary election, regardless of affiliation. Any registered Alaska voter — including Undeclared and Nonpartisan registered voters — may vote. The top four vote-getters advance to the general election. This is the component that primarily benefits candidates like Lisa Murkowski (R), who would have lost a closed Republican primary in 2022 to Alaska Republican Party-endorsed challenger Kelly Tshibaka. With a top-four open system, Murkowski advanced from the primary on her own voter base.

**Ranked-Choice General Election:** In the general election, voters may rank up to four candidates in order of preference. If no candidate receives more than 50% of first-choice votes, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their voters' second-choice rankings are redistributed, continuing until one candidate holds a majority. This is often described as "instant-runoff voting."

**Campaign Finance Disclosure:** The original 2020 Ballot Measure 2 also enacted enhanced campaign finance disclosure requirements, requiring entities contributing more than $2,000/year derived from donations to disclose the true sources of those contributions. The 2026 repeal initiative would eliminate these disclosure requirements as well — a detail the defense should amplify, because it means the repeal would also roll back dark-money transparency, which polls well with nonpartisan voters.

### 2022 Elections — First RCV Implementation

The 2022 cycle was the first use of the system. Key results:

- **U.S. House Special (June 2022):** Democrat Mary Peltola won, becoming the first Alaska Native to hold federal office in Alaska. She led on first-choice votes with approximately 37% and won in a later round when second-choice transfers from Begich voters split roughly 50% to Palin, 29% to Peltola, with 21% exhausting. The cross-party transfer pattern demonstrated that a significant minority of Republican primary voters preferred a moderate Democrat to Sarah Palin.

- **U.S. Senate (November 2022):** Lisa Murkowski (R) defeated Kelly Tshibaka (R) 53.7% to 46.3% after ranked-choice tabulation. Murkowski's survival was directly attributable to the open primary advancing her to the general alongside Tshibaka, and to second-choice transfers from Democratic candidate Patricia Chesbro's voters. Without RCV, Murkowski almost certainly loses a closed Republican primary.

- **Voter education challenges:** August 2022 initial primary showed some voter confusion, and November 2022 general had slightly elevated ballot error rates compared to pre-RCV elections, though no evidence of systematic disenfranchisement. A 2026 peer-reviewed study in the *Election Law Journal* (Anthony, Manion, Kimball, Kropf) found that voters made fuller use of RCV in areas with greater news coverage, confirming that information and education reduce errors.

### 2024 Elections — Mature System Performance

- **U.S. House (November 2024):** Nick Begich (R) defeated Mary Peltola (D) 51% to 49% in the third-round tabulation, with Begich winning by approximately 8,000 votes in the final count. The result demonstrated that RCV did not systematically advantage one party — a Republican won comfortably in a high-turnout Republican year. This is a critical defense argument: the system produces legitimate results regardless of partisan direction.

- **Cross-party second-choice voting** remained high: in 2022, Anchorage showed roughly 10% of all voters crossing party lines on second-choice rankings. In predominantly Alaska Native districts, the cross-party rate reached 80% in 2022, reflecting the high proportion of Native voters registered as independents or Undeclared.

- **Ballot exhaustion research:** Research by Princeton political scientist Nolan McCarty found approximately 1-in-20 Alaska RCV voters had no vote recorded in the final tabulation round (ballot exhaustion). Exhaustion rates were somewhat lower than New York City RCV elections. Exhaustion correlated with areas of high Alaska Native concentration, particularly in multi-candidate fields without a strong co-ethnic candidate. This is a legitimate concern the defense should engage honestly rather than dismiss — but in context: first-past-the-post also produces "wasted" votes, just of a different kind.

## 3. THE 2026 REPEAL INITIATIVE — WHO IS BEHIND IT AND WHY

### Organizational Structure

**Repeal Now** is the primary campaign organization. Leadership:
- **Judy Eledge** — Chair; president of the Anchorage Republican Women's Club
- **Bethany Marcum** — Treasurer; former director of Americans for Prosperity-Alaska (the Koch network's Alaska operation)
- **Ken McCarty** — former state Rep. (Republican, Eagle River); one of three official petition sponsors
- **Bernadette Wilson** — co-sponsor and gubernatorial candidate; stepped back from operational role to avoid conflict with her 2026 governor's race but remains a vocal repeal supporter

The Marcum connection is significant: Americans for Prosperity has been the national coordinating infrastructure for RCV repeal campaigns in multiple states. The AFP-Alaska pipeline suggests coordination with the national conservative movement's anti-RCV infrastructure, not simply a local grassroots effort despite the "grassroots" framing.

### Funding

The 2026 repeal campaign's financial infrastructure is structurally different from 2024. The 2024 repeal campaign spent approximately $120,000 — nearly all of it on signature gathering. In 2025, when signature gathering began for the 2026 effort, a new entity emerged:

**Aurora Action Network** — the repeal's primary financial backer. Funders:
- **Damien Stella** — Alaska-based engineering consultant
- **Michael Rydin** — Texas political activist with a documented history of large conservative donations

Most of Aurora Action Network's spending went to **Upward LLC**, a Florida-based professional signature-gathering firm. Importantly, Marcum stated that 65% of signatures were gathered by volunteers, with the remainder by paid gatherers sent to rural areas. This volunteer-heavy signature model gives the campaign a grassroots legitimacy narrative that is somewhat accurate.

**Projected 2026 repeal spending:** One Alaska Republican strategist estimated the repeal campaign could raise $3–4 million in 2026 — a dramatic increase from 2024's $120,000, but still likely below the defense's capacity.

### Trump Endorsement — A New and Significant Variable

On approximately April 11, 2026, President Trump posted explicit support for the Alaska RCV repeal, calling the system "disastrous, and very fraudulent" and specifically naming Governor Dunleavy, Senator Dan Sullivan, Congressman Nick Begich, and Alaska GOP Chair Carmela Warfield as "hardworking America First Patriots." This is qualitatively different from 2024.

**Why this matters for the field fight:**
- Trump won Alaska by ~13 points in 2024. His explicit, named endorsement activates the high-loyalty MAGA base in Mat-Su and Kenai in ways the 2024 repeal campaign could not.
- Sullivan (up for reelection in 2026) and Begich (now the state's sole House member) will be on the same ballot. Their voter mobilization operations will be explicitly tied to the RCV repeal cause — meaning every door knocked for Sullivan and Begich generates a potential "yes on repeal" vote.
- The "fraudulent" framing, while factually unsupported, connects to the broader 2020 election skepticism infrastructure, which has high engagement rates among its base.

**The counter-argument the defense must exploit:** Trump's endorsement makes this a MAGA litmus test, which alienates the crossover voters — particularly the significant bloc of Alaskan independents and moderate Republicans — who are structurally pro-RCV because the open primary protects their right to participate. Lisa Murkowski, who did not publicly back the 2024 repeal and who owes her Senate career to the top-four system, is the most important potential endorser for the defense.

### Repeal Messaging Strategy

The repeal campaign's core message cluster:

1. **"Confusion"** — "It's extremely confusing to people, most certainly older people" (Eledge). This is the highest-resonance line because it is partially grounded in real voter experience (delayed results, multi-round tabulation complexity).

2. **"One person, one vote"** — Framing RCV as giving some voters "multiple votes." This is technically inaccurate (every voter's ballot is tabulated the same way), but it tests well among voters unfamiliar with elimination-round mechanics.

3. **"Delayed results / lack of transparency"** — Targeting the fact that 2022 and 2024 results were not known for 2+ weeks after Election Day. This is primarily driven by Alaska's late-arriving absentee ballot law, not by RCV itself, but the connection is opaque to casual observers.

4. **"Imposed by outside money"** — The 2020 passage of Ballot Measure 2 was backed significantly by national electoral reform money. The repeal campaign tries to frame the defense as "out-of-state billionaires." This has limited effect given that the repeal's own funding includes a Texas political activist and a Florida signature firm.

5. **"Fraudulent"** — Trump's explicit "very fraudulent" characterization will be used. No election official or independent body has found fraud in Alaska's RCV administration.

## 4. DEFENSE INFRASTRUCTURE

### Protect Alaska's Elections (PAE)

Protect Alaska's Elections is the primary defense organization registered with Alaska's public offices commission, operating since early 2025. Confirmed support base:
- **Labor unions** — Structured union support provides both organizing infrastructure and financial backing
- **Alaska Native organizations** — Including Alaska Federation of Natives (AFN), which formally endorsed RCV at its 2023 annual convention

PAE's operational model in 2024 (as Alaskans for Better Elections) spent approximately $15 million. The top three donors were Article Four, Unite America, and Action Now Initiative. This concentration matters: the defense is highly dependent on a small number of large national donors from the electoral reform infrastructure. If that funding doesn't materialize at the same scale, the field program will be constrained.

**What PAE did that worked in 2024:**
- High-volume television and digital advertising
- Endorsement coalition including 22 unions and Senator Lisa Murkowski
- Coordination with Get Out the Native Vote (Cook Inlet Tribal Council affiliate)
- Media-friendly earned coverage emphasizing the "Alaska model" — Peltola's 2022 win as proof of concept

**What the 2024 campaign did not sufficiently do:**
- Precinct-level targeting in the marginal Anchorage districts where turnout was low
- Rural Alaska GOTV investment beyond the AFN coalition
- Absentee ballot chase program specifically designed to bank "no" votes early

### FairVote and RepresentUs

Both organizations have confirmed national engagement on Alaska:
- **FairVote** operates as the primary national research and communications infrastructure for RCV defense
- **RepresentUs** has identified Alaska as a national priority defensive campaign

Alaska is the only remaining state-level statewide RCV system in active operation after the 2024 defeats in seven other states. Both organizations have organizational survival reasons to invest heavily here: a second loss would eliminate the national flagship and validate the "RCV is losing everywhere" narrative that has gained momentum since 2024.

### Alaska Federation of Natives (AFN)

AFN's 2023 convention resolution endorsing RCV is operationally significant. AFN connects to the regional Alaska Native corporations (Sealaska, Doyon, Calista, BBNC, etc.) and to tribal governments across the state. This network reaches the Bush Alaska communities where untapped "no" votes are concentrated but where turnout has historically been 36–44%.

## 5. FIELD OPS ANALYSIS — TARGETING AND GEOGRAPHY

### Alaska's Geographic Challenge

Alaska is 663,000 square miles — larger than Texas, California, and Montana combined. Most communities are not road-connected to each other. The Bush Alaska region (the Y-K Delta, Interior villages, Northwest Arctic communities) can only be reached by small plane or boat. Standard canvassing and voter contact models do not apply. This geography determines the entire field strategy:

- **Anchorage (45% of statewide votes):** Canvassable, phone-bankable, mail-reachable. Standard field ops apply. The blue-shift creates persuadable suburban precincts that were not persuadable 10 years ago.
- **Mat-Su and Kenai (18-20% of statewide votes):** Heavily "yes" on repeal. GOTV investment here is limited value for the defense — this is the opponent's base. But depressing the enthusiasm gap matters: if Mat-Su turnout is lower than 2024 because the repeal energy is lower, that helps.
- **Fairbanks (8-9%):** Mixed. The UAF community, downtown Fairbanks, and government workers are reachable. Rural Interior surrounding Fairbanks is repeal territory.
- **Southeast Alaska (5-6%):** Juneau, Sitka, and Ketchikan are accessible by ferry or air. Union density is high. This is a high-value-per-dollar targeting area because the population density for canvassing is reasonable and the persuasion needed is minimal — this is base mobilization territory.
- **Bush Alaska/Rural (12-15%):** The highest-return marginal investment. Turnout in rural majority-Native districts is 20-35 points below Anchorage. Even a 5-point turnout increase in HD37, HD38, HD39, HD40 (combined ~60,000 registered voters at 36-44% turnout) could produce 3,000-5,000 additional "no" votes at a very high conversion rate given Alaska Native communities' structural alignment with open primaries.

### Targetable Precinct Analysis

**Priority 1 — Anchorage marginal precincts:** The Anchorage blue-shift is concentrated in specific Assembly districts and House districts. The 2024 ADN analysis found:
- Anchorage went blue at the presidential level
- Of 16 Anchorage state house members, multiple Republicans are caucusing with Democrats
- House Districts 18, 19, and 22 (North and East Anchorage, high immigrant community populations) had below-45% turnout — these are undervoted "no" precincts

Anchorage targeted canvassing in HD18, HD19, HD22, and midtown/downtown HD17, HD20 represents the highest-density persuadable-to-turnout opportunity.

**Priority 2 — Rural Alaska GOTV (Bush turnout uplift):** House Districts 37, 38, 39 (southwestern Alaska, Y-K Delta, Kuskokwim) and HD40 (North Slope/Northwest Arctic) have the lowest turnout in the state and the highest structural alignment with "no." These districts cannot be canvassed in the traditional sense. The effective tactics are:
- Absentee ballot chase programs — request, return, and cure
- Community radio in Alaska Native languages (Yup'ik, Inupiaq, Athabascan, etc.)
- Coordination with Alaska Native regional corporations and tribal governments for voter education
- Village Council resolutions and community messaging (high-trust-authority sources in these communities)
- Satellite-based voter contact where phone banking is feasible

**Priority 3 — Southeast Alaska mobilization:** Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan — organized labor can do most of the GOTV work here through existing union infrastructure. This is base mobilization, not persuasion. Target: no vote left on the table.

**Priority 4 — Fairbanks/UAF:** Students and faculty at the University of Alaska Fairbanks are persuadable and mobile. Dorm and on-campus registration drives, plus the local progressive and independent voter infrastructure. Low dollar-per-vote cost.

### The Mat-Su Problem

Mat-Su Borough is the defense's most intractable geography. Republican registration is 4:1, Trump won the area by 50+ points, and the repeal campaign's volunteer infrastructure is concentrated here. There are two limited options:

1. **Do not invest in Mat-Su for persuasion** — the ROI is low. Defensive spending here is base-depressing at best.
2. **Target specifically the small Undeclared/Nonpartisan Mat-Su voter who benefits from open primaries** — there is a thin slice of non-MAGA Mat-Su voters who liked the top-four open primary because it let them vote across parties. This voter is potentially reachable on an "open primary" frame rather than an "RCV" frame.

### The Absentee Ballot Strategic Lever

The 2024 defense won on late-counted ballots. The absentee/mail ballot differential was the margin of victory. In Alaska:
- Any registered voter may apply for an absentee ballot
- No-excuse absentee voting is available
- Voters traveling to or working in remote Alaska can request ballots 45 days before the election
- Rural Alaska voters routinely use absentee/mail voting given precinct access challenges

**Operational implication:** A systematic absentee ballot request program targeting HD37-40 (rural Native districts) and the Anchorage undervote precincts, coupled with ballot return tracking and curing, could bank 3,000-8,000 additional "no" votes above the organic absentee rate. Given the 743-vote margin, this alone would have been sufficient to change the trajectory from near-miss to comfortable win.

## 6. MESSAGING THAT WORKS — AND WHAT BACKFIRES

### The Core Research Finding

A 2026 peer-reviewed study in the *Election Law Journal* (Anthony, Manion, Kimball, Kropf) confirms that voter understanding of RCV in Alaska is substantially driven by information environment. Areas with more news coverage showed better RCV ballot utilization and fewer errors. This means:
- **Earned media is not just PR — it directly affects voter competence with the system**
- Education programs that explain the mechanics reduce the "confusion" charge's resonance
- The defense's messaging investment has a direct behavioral effect on the ballot error rate, which in turn affects the exhausted-ballot and "uncounted votes" attack lines

### Messages That Test Well for the Defense

**Highest resonance:**
1. **"Your voice, your choice — any candidate, any party"** — The open primary aspect, where any voter can vote for any candidate regardless of registration, polls strongly with the 40% of Alaskans registered as Undeclared or Nonpartisan. Frame the fight as "keeping your right to vote for the person, not the party."

2. **"Protect your vote from the party bosses"** — Repeal means return to party-controlled primaries. In Alaska's political culture, which is genuinely independent-minded, anti-party-machine messaging works across party lines. Mat-Su voters who supported the open primary for its own sake are reachable on this.

3. **"Alaska Native communities chose this system — it works for them"** — AFN's explicit endorsement, combined with the documented 80% cross-party ranking rate in Native districts, gives this claim credibility. The message: the communities that have the most to lose from winner-take-all politics chose to keep this system.

4. **"Even if you don't love RCV, you should love being able to vote for anyone"** — Separating the ranked-choice component from the open primary component can bring in voters who are ambivalent about the ranking mechanic but strongly support nonpartisan primaries. The two components are bundled in the repeal initiative — repeal eliminates both.

5. **"Dark money repeal"** — The 2026 repeal initiative also eliminates campaign finance disclosure requirements. A funded dark-money framing campaign on this aspect ("they want to repeal transparency about who is spending money on Alaska's elections") is underutilized and could peel off independent voters who oppose Citizens United-style dark money.

**Secondary resonance:**
6. **"Results take time because Alaska's law requires it — not because of RCV"** — Directly addresses the "delayed results" attack. The 2-week count is driven by Alaska's late-arriving absentee ballot period, which applies to all statewide elections whether or not RCV is used. This is a factual correction that needs to be delivered proactively, before the opposition frames the narrative.

7. **"It worked — Republicans and Democrats both won"** — The Murkowski 2022 and Begich 2024 wins demonstrate the system is not systematically partisan. This is counterintuitive to some Democratic voters who see RCV primarily as a way to elect Democrats.

### Messages That Backfire or Should Be Used Carefully

1. **"RCV is more democratic / produces majority winners"** — Too abstract. The word "democratic" has become tribal, and the majority-winner argument requires voter comprehension of the elimination mechanics. It talks about procedure when persuasion requires talking about what voters get.

2. **"Experts say it works"** — Alaska voters are resistant to expert authority claims from outside institutions. The Harvard Journal on Legislation "Alaska Model" article is useful for media and donor communications, not for direct voter persuasion.

3. **"The other states that repealed it were wrong"** — Creates a defensive posture that acknowledges the national headwinds. Don't run away from the 7-state losses; reframe them: "Alaska voters decide for Alaska."

4. **Attacking the repeal funders as "out-of-state"** — The defense's own funding is primarily national (Article Four, Unite America, Action Now Initiative). The out-of-state money attack can easily be used against the defense campaign and will be.

5. **Complexity defense** — Do not defend the complexity of the system mechanically. The "one person, one vote" attack is emotionally resonant even if legally wrong. The counter is not to explain why RCV doesn't actually give some voters more votes — the counter is to appeal to the voter's own experience of ranking their preferences as simple and familiar.

### Alaska Political Culture Context

Alaska's political culture is distinctive in ways that affect messaging:
- **High independent registration:** ~40% of voters are registered Undeclared or Nonpartisan. They value their ability to vote across parties and are hostile to party-machine control of nominations.
- **Subsistence and resource identity:** Many rural and Alaska Native voters organize politically around subsistence rights, land, and resource sovereignty. The RCV/open primary connection to political self-determination resonates with these communities in ways that abstract procedural arguments do not.
- **Skepticism of outside influence:** Both the defense and repeal campaigns have outside funding. The defense needs Alaska-centric voices and messengers — Native leaders, union members, local business owners, former Republican legislators who used the system effectively.
- **Military and veteran community:** Alaska has a large active-duty and veteran population concentrated around JBER (Anchorage) and Fort Wainwright (Fairbanks). This community is often Undeclared, votes split-ticket, and values nonpartisan primaries. The open primary is a specific benefit for this constituency.

**What the defense needs from Murkowski:** A public, prominent, on-camera statement specifically designed for Mat-Su and Kenai Peninsula voters — the moderate Republicans who supported her and who benefit from the open primary. A Murkowski-fronted message on open primary protection could move 1–3% of soft repeal voters in these boroughs. Even a 1% movement in Mat-Su (which alone could represent 700–1,400 votes) changes the outcome.

### The "Anti-Dark-Money" Angle

The 2026 repeal initiative removes not just RCV but the campaign finance disclosure requirements from the original 2020 Ballot Measure 2. This transparency provision requires disclosure of the true sources of large political contributions. Its repeal would reduce Alaska's political transparency.

**This is a significant underused defense argument.** Dark money is unpopular across party lines. Framing the repeal as "eliminating Alaska's transparency requirements" — rather than just "eliminating RCV" — broadens the coalition and creates a wedge issue with moderate Alaskans who are ambivalent about RCV but opposed to political dark money.

## 8. NATIONAL STAKES AND FAILURE CONSEQUENCES

### If Alaska's RCV Is Repealed

Alaska is now the only state with a statewide RCV system actively operating for federal elections after the 2024 defeat of new-adoption measures in seven states. Maine uses RCV for federal elections (congressional and presidential), but Alaska's *top-four plus RCV* combined system is unique and more extensively tested.

A 2026 repeal in Alaska would:
1. **Eliminate the national proof-of-concept** — The "Alaska model" is cited by RCV advocates across the country as evidence that statewide RCV is administrable and produces legitimate results. Its removal hands repeal advocates a "Alaska rejected it" counter-narrative.
2. **Provide a repeal template** — The signature-gathering, messaging, and campaign finance playbook developed in Alaska would be replicated in Maine, which has used RCV for congressional elections since 2018 and where repeal campaigns have been floated.
3. **Damage the open primary movement** — Multiple states are exploring top-four or top-two open primaries. The Alaska repeal, which is framed as an "open primary repeal" as much as an "RCV repeal," would provide momentum against these state-level efforts.
4. **Validate the national anti-RCV infrastructure** — Americans for Prosperity and affiliated organizations have made RCV repeal a national strategic priority since 2022. An Alaska win would accelerate their investment in other targets.

### Murkowski's Long-Term Situation

If RCV is repealed before Murkowski's 2028 reelection, she faces a return to closed Republican primaries in which the Alaska Republican Party infrastructure (already aligned with Trump-endorsed candidates) controls the nomination. This is a significant personal political stake that makes Murkowski a natural defense ally, but her public posture has been cautious — she needs to calculate whether a prominent defense role hurts her with the broader Republican base.

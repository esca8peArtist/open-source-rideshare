## Section 1: The Participation Deficit — Scale, Structure, and Consequences

### 1.1 Trust Data Updated Through 2025

The proposal's "16% in 2023" figure for public trust in government requires updating. Pew Research's December 2025 survey found that **17% of Americans** trust the government in Washington to do what is right "just about always" or "most of the time" — near the lowest point in the nearly 70-year survey history.

The 2025 figure is a *decline* from 22% in 2024, despite a change in administration that historically produces a partisan-realignment bump. That the trust figure declined even as a new president took office (typically a honeymoon period) suggests structural rather than cyclical decay — consistent with the Gilens-Page finding that policy outcomes do not respond to median voter preferences regardless of which party controls government.

The historical trajectory is the meaningful data: 77% (1964) → 54% (1972, post-Vietnam) → 29% (1994, Contract with America) → 54% (2002, post-9/11) → 19% (2011, Tea Party/debt ceiling) → 17% (2025). The arc is long-term decline punctuated by crisis-driven rallies, each rally failing to return to the prior baseline. The structural diagnosis — institutions do not respond to citizens — better explains this pattern than the cyclical diagnosis.

For context: only 39% of Americans say the federal government has a positive impact on "people like me," 56% consider government "wasteful and inefficient," and Congress's trust rating sits at approximately 8% (Gallup 2025). Trust in Congress is so low that it functions as a floor rather than a measurement — it no longer differentiates between "skeptical" and "alienated."

Sources: [Pew Research: Public Trust in Government 1958-2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/) | [Pew: Trust in Government near historic lows](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/public-trust-in-government-near-historic-lows-5/)

### 1.2 How the Participation Gap Translates into Policy Distortion

The participation gap is not merely a civic-health problem. It is a governance-quality problem with measurable policy consequences.

**The primary election concentration effect.** In the approximately 90% of U.S. House districts that are non-competitive in general elections, the primary determines the outcome. Primary turnout in House races averages 5-15% of eligible voters. These voters are systematically older, higher-income, and more ideologically extreme than the general electorate. The result is that a fraction of a fraction of the population selects representatives for all. A member elected in a 5% primary in a safe district faces no accountability from the other 95% until the next primary — and those 95% have no effective mechanism between elections.

**The Gilens-Page measurement.** The proposal correctly cites the Gilens-Page (2014) finding that policy outcomes in the U.S. correlate with elite preferences and show no significant correlation with median voter preferences. This finding has been contested on methodological grounds (Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien, 2017, argued the median-voter correlation appears when outcomes are measured differently), and the debate is genuine. The most defensible synthesis: Gilens and Page establish that when elite and mass preferences diverge, policy follows elites; the contestation establishes that when they align, it's harder to attribute outcomes to either. The finding is not "citizens have no influence" — it is "citizens have no *additional* influence beyond what coincides with what elites want." The functional consequence: participation gaps allow the elite-aligned subset of outcomes to become the entire policy space.

**The local elections crisis.** Turnout in local elections — where the most direct policy impacts on daily life occur — regularly falls below 20%. A 2022 Portland, Oregon city council election drew 11% turnout. A 2023 Los Angeles school board race drew 9%. At these turnout levels, organized minorities of 2-3% of eligible voters can determine outcomes entirely. The practical consequence: school boards, city councils, sheriffs, district attorneys, and local judges — the positions through which authoritarian consolidation is implemented locally, per Part VIII of the crisis analysis — are selected by a minuscule and unrepresentative fraction of affected citizens.

## Section 2: Citizens' Assemblies — Updated Evidence (2024-2025)

### 2.1 The Irish Model: Strengths and Limits

Ireland is the most documented case of successful citizens' assembly deployment, and it has now had enough history to assess both successes and failures. The proposal presents the successes accurately; the failures require documentation because reformers who cannot answer the Irish failure cases will be vulnerable in political advocacy.

**Successes (documented):**
- 2015 assembly: abortion liberalization recommendation → 2018 referendum passed with 66.4%
- 2015 assembly: same-sex marriage recommendation → referendum passed with 62%
- Convention on the Constitution (2012-2014): directly influenced multiple constitutional amendments
- The assembly model enabled politicians to move on issues they could not risk sponsoring directly — providing political cover for reform

**The 2024 failure case:** The assembly on gender equality led to two referendum questions in March 2024 — on expanding the constitutional definition of family and on removing a provision describing women's role in the home. Both referendums failed, with the family referendum defeated by 67% and the care referendum by 74%. The failure was not attributable to the assembly's recommendations being unpopular — the assembly had 99% support for both changes. The failure was attributable to: (1) confusing and legally complex ballot language that differed materially from what the assembly recommended, (2) last-minute No campaigns exploiting the complexity, and (3) insufficient public engagement in the months between assembly recommendation and vote. The lesson: **the assembly can recommend correctly and the political process can still fail the recommendation.** The assembly-to-referendum pipeline is not automatic; it requires dedicated public education, accessible communication, and ballot design that preserves the assembly's intent.

**The "Irish model" constraint:** Ireland's success has been partly attributed to the specific constitutional mechanism (referendum required for constitutional change) that forced politicians to engage with assembly recommendations rather than ignore them. In the U.S. context, without a mandatory referendum mechanism, advisory assemblies could easily be ignored entirely. This is the most powerful counterargument to the Domain 3 proposal and must be addressed: any U.S. Citizens' Assembly design must include mandatory response requirements, public hearings, and eventually binding authority over specific domains (electoral rules, campaign finance, redistricting) to prevent it from becoming a sophisticated form of public consultation theater.

Sources: [Citizens' Assembly Ireland — current reports](https://citizensassembly.ie/reports/) | [UCL Constitution Unit: Citizens' Assemblies](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/social-historical-sciences/constitution-unit/constitution-unit-publications/constitution-unit-briefings/citizens-assemblies-what-are-they-and-how-can-policy-makers-use-them) | [Frontiers: Irish Model for Deliberative Democracy](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.591983/full)

### 2.2 Belgium's Permanent Model at Three Years

Belgium's Ostbelgien (German-speaking Community) permanent citizens' assembly, established in 2019, is the world's first permanent randomly-selected assembly and the strongest precedent for the U.S. proposal's "pathway to binding authority" concept. At three years of operation (through 2022), the evidence is:

- The standing assembly selects its own agenda items — citizens choose what gets deliberated, not politicians
- Issue-specific panels of 25-50 randomly selected citizens deliberate each item
- The regional parliament must formally debate every recommendation and issue a public written response within 6 months
- No recommendation has been fully rejected without public parliamentary explanation
- The model has generated sustained media and academic interest and is being studied for replication at the federal level in Belgium and in other EU countries

The Ostbelgien model directly answers the "advisory assemblies get ignored" criticism by institutionalizing a mandatory parliamentary response requirement. It also demonstrates that permanent random selection is administratively feasible — citizens serve 6-month terms on the standing assembly, with staggered replacement to preserve institutional memory.

**Why this matters for U.S. design:** The mandatory parliamentary response requirement is the single most important procedural innovation. Without it, advisory assemblies produce reports that gather dust. With it, assembly recommendations become a recurring political accountability mechanism. A U.S. Citizens' Assembly statute should specify: within 180 days of any assembly recommendation, the relevant congressional committee must hold a public hearing, and the full chamber must vote on whether to adopt, reject, or refer to further deliberation — with rejection requiring a recorded vote and public explanation.

### 2.3 Netherlands 2025 Climate Assembly

The Netherlands established a National Citizens' Assembly on Climate in 2025 — the latest entry in a growing European pattern. The assembly design incorporated lessons from the French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat (2019-2020), where the government accepted 146 of 149 recommendations but implementation was subsequently inconsistent. The Dutch design addressed the implementation gap by creating a follow-up panel with oversight authority over government compliance with accepted recommendations — a structural innovation that bridges the assembly-to-implementation gap. Results from the Dutch assembly are not yet published as of April 2026, but the design is worth tracking as a model for U.S. implementation pathways.

## Section 3: Digital Deliberation — vTaiwan and pol.is Updated (2024-2025)

### 3.1 vTaiwan's 2024-2025 AI Governance Work

The proposal's vTaiwan reference is accurate but dated. Significant 2024-2025 developments update the evidence base:

**The 80% government response rate** (cited in the proposal as 80% of vTaiwan deliberations resulting in government action since 2015) remains the headline finding. The mechanism has expanded: since 2024, vTaiwan has applied pol.is plus AI summarization tools to AI governance questions — a domain where the traditional regulatory process produces opaque technical rules that citizens cannot meaningfully engage with. In December 2024, vTaiwan hosted a hybrid deliberation event on AI governance with the pol.is tool. In March 2025, findings from this process were presented at a consultation hosted by Taiwan's National Human Rights Commission, and feedback was incorporated into NHRC recommendations for the draft AI Basic Act.

**The key design insight from vTaiwan's recent work:** pol.is surfaces opinion clusters and identifies the most divisive topics for *additional* in-person deliberation — rather than attempting to resolve everything online. The hybrid model (digital survey + in-person workshop for contested items) consistently outperforms purely online deliberation because it uses digital tools where they excel (scale, inclusion, initial mapping of opinion space) and in-person deliberation where it excels (nuance, trust-building, resolution of complex tradeoffs). The U.S. notice-and-comment process, which produces 94% corporate-interest comments on significant rules, is the structural opposite of this model: it is formally open to all but practically captured by organized interests with drafting capacity. A pol.is-style layer added to notice-and-comment would not replace the technical legal comment process but would give regulators a genuine picture of public opinion distribution on regulatory choices — something the current system does not provide.

### 3.2 The Decidim Platform: Scale and Adoption

Barcelona's Decidim platform deserves a more specific evidence base than the proposal provides.

As of 2024:
- Used by over 400 organizations across 50+ countries
- Barcelona: 150,000+ registered participants, 30,000+ proposals submitted, used for strategic planning, participatory budgeting, and policy co-creation
- Helsinki's OmaStadi: 60,000+ residents participated in 2023 cycle, resulting in €8M in public investment decisions
- The city of Madrid has used Decide Madrid (Decidim-based) for consultations on transportation, parks, and city budgets
- Decidim is fully open-source, available for adoption at zero licensing cost, and maintained by a community including the Barcelona city government, several EU research institutes, and independent contributors

**The adoption barrier is not technical — it is political.** Decidim deployments that succeed have strong political commitment from government to actually act on outputs. Deployments that fail produce well-documented citizen input that is then ignored. The most common failure mode: a government deploys Decidim as a public relations exercise without any intention of binding itself to results. The distinction between "consultation" and "participation with consequence" is the critical design variable. Every U.S. Decidim deployment proposal should specify: which decisions are bounded by citizen input, how binding that input is, and what enforcement mechanism exists if government ignores it.

## Section 4: Participatory Budgeting — Evidence Updated to 2024

### 4.1 Porto Alegre: Success and Discontinuity

The Porto Alegre case is the canonical reference for participatory budgeting effectiveness. The proposal presents the outcome data accurately. What requires addition: Porto Alegre discontinued its original participatory budgeting model in 2017 when a new administration chose not to continue it — demonstrating the central vulnerability of participatory budgeting when it depends on political will rather than institutional mandate.

The lesson is not that Porto Alegre's model failed — the documented outcomes (treated water 99% of population, sewer coverage 46% → 86%, 50+ new schools, infant mortality down 19%, truancy 9% → <1%) are genuine. The lesson is that **participatory democracy reforms embedded in administrative practice rather than statutory mandate are reversible when administrations change** — precisely the structural lesson that the democratic renewal proposal's entire architecture addresses. Participatory budgeting requires statutory mandate (not just political will) to be durable. South Korea's mandate of participatory budgeting for all local governments provides the model.

### 4.2 U.S. Cities Updated to 2024

**New York City (2024):** 24 Council Members allocated $24 million in capital funding through participatory budgeting in 2024 — residents voted directly on improvements to schools, parks, and libraries. The NYC program has now allocated over $300 million cumulatively since 2011. Participation rates are consistently highest in lower-income and less-educated districts — the opposite of the pattern in conventional elections, where participation skews wealthy and educated.

**Los Angeles:** Program established 2021, allocated approximately $8.5 million across nine neighborhoods over two cycles. Funded: job training and placement, rental assistance, services for people experiencing homelessness, urban agriculture programs, and medical services for underserved communities. No city-wide expansion as of April 2026.

**The four-conditions synthesis:** A 2024 Tandfonline meta-analysis of participatory budgeting outcomes identified four conditions required for sustained impact: (1) structured participatory arrangements ensuring diverse representation; (2) adequate financial resources; (3) political commitment and flexibility; (4) government commitment to implement results. The Porto Alegre collapse is explained by condition (3) failing. U.S. cities where programs have grown (NYC) satisfy all four. The implication for federal program design: federal matching funds should be conditioned on statutory commitment to implement results — not just conduct the process.

## Section 5: The Resistance-Participation Connection

### 5.1 The Tufekci Variable and Democratic Infrastructure

The Domain 3 proposal and the crisis analysis's Variable 4 (Organizational Depth) address the same structural problem from different directions. Tufekci's finding — that digital-era movements can scale mobilization rapidly but lack the organizational depth to sustain pressure or negotiate outcomes — is structurally equivalent to the participation deficit. Both describe the same gap: between mass sympathy and organized, sustained civic capacity.

The No Kings movement's three-cycle growth pattern (June 2025: 5M → October 2025: 7M → March 2026: 8–9M) is an extraordinary mobilization signal. The organizational depth question is whether this converts into the structures Domain 3 proposes: citizens' assemblies that practice deliberation, participatory budgeting that builds trust through consequential civic action, and civic infrastructure that provides ongoing forums between mobilization events.

**The key insight:** The Eyes on ICE program (200,000+ first training viewers) is itself a form of the "digital deliberation infrastructure" Domain 3 proposes — it converts mass participation into a durable accountability practice. The 3,000 organizing meeting hosts who convened April 3 (post-March 28) are the civic infrastructure formation that Domain 3 describes. The resistance movement is, in a limited way, building the democratic participation infrastructure that the proposal argues must be institutionalized.

The implication: Domain 3 reforms are not just long-term democratic renewal projects. They are a description of what the most effective resistance organizing is already doing, and why institutionalizing it matters for survivability beyond the current crisis.

### 5.2 Why Participation Infrastructure Inoculates Against Authoritarian Capture

The crisis analysis (Section 1.6) documents that 53% of Project 2025 has been implemented in 12 months. The question the participation literature answers is: why did a program for dismantling democratic institutions succeed so quickly?

The answer is not that citizens approved of it. Polling consistently shows majorities opposing specific authoritarian actions. The answer is structural: **between elections, citizens had no consequential institutional forum for their preferences.** Project 2025 was implemented through executive orders, DOGE directives, and administrative actions that operate between elections and outside any forum where organized citizen input has consequence. A federal notice-and-comment rulemaking process, or an advisory agency stakeholder meeting, is theoretically available — but practically captured by organized interests.

Citizens' assemblies, participatory budgeting, and digital deliberation platforms do not prevent authoritarian capture by themselves. But they create practiced civic muscle — the habit and capacity for consequential collective decision-making — that makes societies harder to capture. Denmark's folk high schools are credited as foundational democratic institutions not because they prevented authoritarianism directly, but because they built a citizenry with practiced deliberative capacity that made authoritarian consolidation harder to achieve without visible democratic betrayal. The research on deliberative democracy consistently finds that participation increases not just civic engagement but **civic competence** — citizens who practice deliberation become better at identifying when democratic norms are violated and more willing to act in defense of those norms.

## Section 6: Counterarguments and Responses

These are the strongest objections to Domain 3 reforms, with the evidence-based responses:

**"Citizens' assemblies are unelected and unaccountable."** The objection is technically correct but misses the structural purpose. Citizens' assemblies do not *replace* elected bodies — they *advise* them (in advisory form) or *constrain* them on specific domains where self-interest makes elected bodies structurally incapable of fair decisions (electoral rules, campaign finance, redistricting). The electorate itself is "unelected" in the same sense — citizens who participate in referendums are not accountable to other citizens for their votes. The randomized assembly is a legitimate democratic instrument for exactly the same reason referendums are: it represents a cross-section of the population better than self-selected participation does.

**"Randomly selected citizens lack expertise to decide complex policy."** The evidence from 600+ deliberative processes documented in the OECD's 2020 report contradicts this. Randomly selected citizens, properly supported with expert briefings, professional secretariats, and dedicated research staff, consistently produce well-reasoned recommendations that reflect genuine tradeoffs rather than ideological axioms. Ireland's assembly on abortion produced a more nuanced recommendation than any parliamentary bill would have — recognizing access needs, time limits, and medical judgment in a way that a binary political debate does not allow. The "expertise" objection is often a proxy for "process would not produce the outcome organized interests prefer."

**"Participatory budgeting is a distraction — the amounts are too small to matter."** This objection has empirical weight: NYC's $24M is a fraction of a $100+ billion city budget. The response has two parts. First, the dollar amounts are not the primary mechanism of impact; the primary mechanism is building civic trust and competence through consequential participation. Porto Alegre's outcomes (99% water coverage, infant mortality down 19%) were driven by small-budget PB decisions that changed *priorities* within existing spending, not the total spending level. Second, the scale can grow: the proposal calls for 10-20% of federal community development spending, not a token amount. The pilot/scale logic — begin with consequential but bounded decisions, build trust, expand — is well-documented in the technology adoption and institutional reform literature.

**"Digital platforms exclude the elderly and the poor."** Accurate for current deployment models. The evidence base confirms: 24 million Americans without broadband, 22% of seniors offline, 32 million adults with limited literacy. Domain 3 reform proposals must include, by design: in-person alternatives at all participation points, multilingual support, accessibility accommodation, and proactive outreach to historically excluded communities as conditions for federal matching funds. The Decidim case study evidence shows that inclusive-by-design platforms achieve higher participation in low-income communities than in high-income ones — the equity problem is a design problem, not an inherent technological barrier.

## Section 8: Connection to Other Domains

Domain 3 reforms are uniquely cross-cutting. They depend on and reinforce:

- **Domain 1 (Electoral Reform):** Higher baseline civic engagement reduces primary-election extremism; participatory infrastructure builds the "organizational depth" that Tufekci identifies as the missing variable in digital-era movements
- **Domain 2 (Institutional Integrity):** Citizens' assemblies deliberating on which agencies need constitutional protection would produce more durable political coalitions for Domain 2 reforms than elite advocacy alone
- **Domain 4 (Digital Government Infrastructure):** The deliberation platforms (Decidim, pol.is) share technical architecture with Domain 4's open government APIs; civic infrastructure investment builds the digital literacy required for Domain 4's benefits to reach low-income communities
- **Domain 8 (Media Ecosystem):** Deliberative processes require a minimum common factual baseline; Domain 8's public media and local news investments are prerequisites for participation quality
- **Domain 6 (Judicial Independence):** A citizens' assembly with binding authority over electoral rules would be more resistant to judicial invalidation than ordinary legislation because it is itself a democratic process — harder to characterize as partisan manipulation

The most important feedback loop: **Domain 3 is the mechanism through which citizens build the civic muscle to demand and defend every other domain.** Reforms without participation infrastructure are imposed on a passive citizenry and are therefore reversible whenever political will changes. Reforms accompanied by participation infrastructure create constituencies that understand, value, and defend them.

## Section 9: The Strongest Counterargument — Deliberative Bodies as Organized Minority Capture Vectors

*This section addresses the objection not covered in Section 6: that randomly selected assemblies can be more easily captured by organized minorities than broad elections, which distribute power across millions of individual decisions.*

### 9.1 The Objection Stated Precisely

The conventional critique of deliberative democracy focuses on the "unelected and unaccountable" objection or the expertise question. Those are addressed in Section 6. The stronger structural objection is different: **a small assembly of 150 randomly selected citizens is a softer target for organized influence than a general election of millions of voters.**

The mechanism runs as follows. In a general election, organized minorities must persuade or suppress millions of independent voters — a resource-intensive and imperfect process. In a citizens' assembly of 150 people, the same organized interest needs to influence only a handful of key facilitation decisions: which expert witnesses appear before the assembly, how the problem is framed in the briefing materials, who serves as the professional secretariat, and what deliberative norms are enforced. The "organized minority capture" critique argues that these upstream design choices are more controllable than the upstream design choices of a mass election, making assemblies potentially more, not less, susceptible to elite capture.

This is a serious objection with some empirical basis. It must be answered, not dismissed.

### 9.2 What the 2024-2025 Research Shows

**The Bagg (2024) finding in the American Journal of Political Science** is the most rigorous recent treatment. Bagg's "Sortition as Anti-Corruption: Popular Oversight against Elite Capture" (2024, AJPS) argues that ordinary citizens are vulnerable to subtle elite manipulation through facilitation framing and information selection — and that sortition is best deployed as an oversight check on elite institutions rather than as a primary lawmaking mechanism. The implication: assemblies with binding authority over their own agenda (like the Belgian Ostbelgien model) are more resistant to capture than assemblies whose agendas and expert witnesses are selected by the commissioning government.

**The selection bias problem.** Yale's ISPS Democratic Innovations Program (2025) documented that recruitment for citizens' assemblies systematically oversamples citizens who are already civically engaged — those who respond to invitation letters and accept an 18-month commitment are not representative of the pool even before the random selection occurs. People without flexible work schedules, without reliable childcare, and without confidence in institutional settings are structurally underrepresented before the lottery even happens. The "random sample" is a random sample of those willing and able to participate — a smaller and less representative pool than it appears.

**The invisible power problem.** Research from the Journal of Deliberative Democracy (2024) documents that "invisible power" — the framing of what is considered legitimate versus radical — systematically influences assembly deliberation. Professional facilitators, most trained in consensus-based methodologies that treat conflict as a problem to be managed rather than a signal of genuine disagreement, may suppress heterodox views that would survive in an unmanaged deliberative setting.

**The quota manipulation risk.** A 2026 arXiv paper on temporal panel selection in ongoing citizens' assemblies found that when sortition algorithms use tight demographic quotas, participants may misreport identity characteristics to increase their selection probability. The more precisely targeted the stratification, the more game-able the selection mechanism becomes.

### 9.3 The Counter-Counter: Why Assemblies Are Still More Capture-Resistant Than Current Alternatives

The capture critique is analytically serious but proves too much if taken to its logical conclusion. It would also condemn courts (whose deliberations are more accessible to organized interests than assemblies), regulatory agencies (whose notice-and-comment processes are demonstrably captured), and legislative committees (whose markup sessions are explicitly lobbied). The question is not whether assemblies can be captured — it is whether they are more or less capture-resistant than the alternatives they are meant to supplement or replace.

**The empirical answer is: more resistant, with the right design.** The evidence from 600+ deliberative processes (OECD 2020 report) is that on contentious social policy questions — where organized interests have high stakes and intense preferences — citizens' assemblies consistently produce more moderate, rights-protective, and evidence-responsive recommendations than legislative bodies. The Irish abortion assembly (99% support for access — the organized Catholic hierarchy's preferred outcome was zero) and the French climate assembly (propositions that went further than the government wanted, not further in the direction any organized interest preferred) are the canonical cases.

**The design variables that determine capture resistance:**
1. The assembly selects its own agenda items (Ostbelgien model) — removes the commissioning principal's ability to frame the question
2. Expert witnesses are selected by assembly members, not by the secretariat — removes the information-gatekeeping mechanism
3. The secretariat is staffed by a politically independent body (a university, a non-partisan civic institution) rather than by the government — removes facilitation capture
4. Deliberation norms explicitly protect "minority reports" and dissenting views in the final record — removes consensus-washing
5. The assembly is funded through a statutory appropriation, not through discretionary executive allocation — removes the financial leverage point

When these design features are present, the organized-minority-capture critique loses most of its force. When they are absent, the critique is valid — and the response is not to abandon assemblies but to insist on these design requirements as a condition of any federal authorization.

**The deeper structural point:** A citizens' assembly with the above design features is less capture-resistant than a functioning democratic legislature but more capture-resistant than the alternatives that have replaced legislative function in the current period — judicial review of regulatory action (where corporate interests fund test cases), executive rulemaking (where 94% of significant rule comments come from corporate interests), and the budget reconciliation process (where a handful of swing-vote senators negotiate in private). The relevant comparison is not assemblies versus an idealized legislature; it is assemblies versus the actual institutional alternatives currently performing democratic functions.

## Section 10: Fiscal Estimates — Methodology and Sourcing

*This section replaces and deepens Section 7's fiscal estimates with explicit methodology.*

### 10.1 Citizens' Assembly — Federal Advisory Body

The Section 7 estimate ($35-50M per assembly) is directionally correct but requires methodological grounding.

**Methodological basis for the member compensation figure.** The $75,000 annualized figure for 200 members is derived from two comparators: (1) federal jury service compensation of $50/day (a floor, not a model) and (2) the Welsh Climate Assembly's compensation structure, which paid participants the equivalent of approximately £70/day (roughly $85/day or $31,000 annually). A full-time federal Citizens' Assembly representing a higher-stakes democratic function would warrant compensation in the range of a senior civil service position; $75,000/year for an 18-month term is approximately GS-11 level, the appropriate comparator for a citizen performing a sustained civic function requiring substantial time commitment and expertise development.

**Comparator for secretariat costs.** The Congressional Budget Office employs approximately 235 staff with an annual budget of approximately $60 million — $255,000 per employee. A leaner Citizens' Assembly secretariat of 20 staff would cost approximately $5-8 million annually at equivalent compensation levels, or $7.5-12 million over an 18-month assembly cycle. The $3-5M estimate in Section 7 is below the CBO comparator; $5-10M is more defensible.

**Expert witnesses and research.** The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine charges approximately $1.5-4M per consensus study. A Citizens' Assembly commissioning 10-15 expert reports at comparable cost would spend $15-60M — consistent with the Section 7 range when combined with the facility and translation costs.

**Revised estimate:** $35-65M per assembly (18-month cycle), with an annual carrying cost of $25-45M assuming one assembly in session at any time. The original estimate's low end ($35M) is achievable only with streamlined design; $50M is the median case; $65M is the upper bound for a full-scale model with comprehensive public engagement.

**Context comparator:** The January 6th Select Committee cost approximately $20 million over two years. A permanent Citizens' Assembly costs approximately 2-3x the Select Committee's budget, serving a permanent rather than single-cycle function.

### 10.2 Participatory Budgeting — Federal Matching Fund Program

**Methodological basis.** The New York City figure is the best documented U.S. case: $30 million allocated (FY2026), facilitated by 24 Council Members' offices, with administrative costs estimated at approximately $2-3 million annually based on NYC Council budget allocations. This produces a facilitation-to-allocation ratio of approximately 7-10%.

A federal matching program would have higher per-city startup costs (platform customization, training, initial outreach) and lower ongoing costs as the model stabilizes. A credible federal model:
- Year 1-3 (50-city pilot): $3-5M per city in federal match + $1-2M per city in facilitation grants = $200-350M total program cost per year
- Year 4+ (nationwide, 300 cities over 50,000): $1-2M per city in federal match + $500K in facilitation = $450-750M per year at scale

The Harvard Ash Center's participatory budgeting research (2024) found that programs with federal or state matching funding are 3.5x more likely to reach sustainability than programs funded solely through local discretionary allocations — the matching structure itself changes the durability of the program.

**What PB does not cost:** The funds allocated through PB come from existing budget lines — capital improvement funds, community development block grant equivalents, or discretionary program funds. PB changes the decision-making process, not the total expenditure. The net incremental cost to government is the facilitation and matching fund, not the full value of the projects funded.

Sources: [NYC Council FY2026 PB Results](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2025/05/30/2892/) | [Harvard Ash Center: Participatory Budgeting Paper 2024](https://ash.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/participatory-budgeting-paper.pdf)

### 10.3 Digital Deliberation Infrastructure — Revised Estimate

The Section 7 figures ($50-75M Year 1, $20-50M/year ongoing) are consistent with comparator data. For context:

- The federal government's USAJobs.gov platform cost approximately $30 million to build and costs approximately $5-10M/year to operate, serving approximately 10 million active users
- Healthcare.gov at stable operation (post-2014 rebuild) costs approximately $500 million/year including call centers and enrollment support — the high end of what the entire U.S. government digital deliberation infrastructure would cost at comprehensive scale
- The minimum viable federal version (Decidim deployment for federal agency rulemaking, plus pol.is integration for public comment processes) would cost approximately $30-50M to build and $15-30M/year to operate — below the Section 7 estimates

The Section 7 figures are appropriate for a comprehensive program including extensive multilingual support, accessibility features, and proactive outreach to historically excluded communities. A minimum viable version is achievable at roughly half the cost.

## Section 11: The 2025-2026 Rollback — Democratic Participation Under Attack

*Documenting the specific actions taken against democratic participation infrastructure in the current period — consistent with the cross-domain rollback documentation standard.*

### 11.1 Federal Voting Rights Rollback

**Executive Order 14248 (March 25, 2025):** "Preserving and Protecting the Integrity of American Elections" directed the Election Assistance Commission to add documentary proof of citizenship to the federal voter registration form. The practical consequence: an estimated 21 million Americans — disproportionately women who have changed their names, older Black Americans born during the pre-civil rights era who were never issued birth certificates, and low-income voters — would face barriers to registration that do not exist for voters with matching documentation.

Federal courts have partially blocked implementation. U.S. District Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly (D.D.C.) ruled the proof-of-citizenship directive unconstitutional as a violation of the separation of powers (October 31, 2025 — permanent block). A Massachusetts district court issued additional injunctions on January 30, 2026, blocking Sections 2(d) and 3(d). As of April 2026, litigation continues in multiple circuits.

**The SAVE America Act (passed House 218-213, February 11, 2026):** Goes further than the EO — requires documentary proof of citizenship for all federal election registration, mandates frequent voter purges, and eliminates most current online and mail registration methods. As of April 2026, faces a 60-vote filibuster threshold in the Senate. If enacted: 21+ million Americans face registration barriers; an estimated 69 million women and 4 million men lack birth certificates matching their current legal name.

**EAC destabilization.** The Election Assistance Commission, which provides federal guidance and certification standards for voting systems, has faced administration pressure to rewrite Voluntary Voting System Guidelines and rescind prior certifications — creating uncertainty in the 2026 election infrastructure at the worst possible moment.

### 11.2 State-Level Rollback: 13 States Ban RCV

As of March 2026, **13 states** have enacted laws banning ranked-choice voting statewide. States that enacted bans in the first half of 2025 include Arkansas, Kansas, North Dakota, West Virginia, and Wyoming. The total bans as of March 2026 represent a roughly 2x increase from the 2022 baseline. At the same time, 17 states are considering legislation to adopt RCV in 2025, and Washington D.C.'s voter-passed Initiative 83 (November 2024) will implement RCV in all D.C. elections by 2026. The net trajectory is bifurcation: red-state rollback, blue-state adoption.

### 11.3 Voter Registration: Opposed Trajectories

The Register America to Vote Act (S. 2822, 119th Congress) would implement automatic voter registration at age 18 and through state motor vehicle authorities — the reform that 24 states and D.C. have already enacted. It has no path to enactment in the current Congress. Meanwhile, the SAVE America Act's documentary proof requirement would functionally reverse existing AVR programs in states where they are in place, because online and mail registration — the channels through which AVR operates — would require in-person documentation presentation.

The voting participation battlefield in 2026 is: defending the 24-state AVR gains, defeating the SAVE America Act in the Senate, defending the EO litigation victories through appeals, and keeping 2026 elections from being administered under the destabilized guidance environment the current EO creates.

### 11.4 What This Means for Domain 3 Reform Timelines

The 2025-2026 rollback does not make Domain 3 reforms impossible — state and local Citizens' Assemblies, participatory budgeting, and digital deliberation infrastructure do not require federal legislation. But it does change the sequencing calculus:

- Citizens' assemblies at state level are immediately achievable in states with legislative capacity and political will — Oregon, California, Illinois, Minnesota, Colorado, and Michigan are realistic targets
- Participatory budgeting expansion at the city level is ongoing and not contingent on federal legislative action
- Federal voting rights reform (AVR, RCV) requires Senate filibuster reform or a 60-vote majority — not achievable before 2027 at the earliest

The resistance implication: domain 3 reforms split into two tracks. The state/local infrastructure track (assemblies, PB, digital platforms) can proceed independently of federal legislative outcomes. The federal voting rights track (AVR, VRA restoration, RCV) requires either judicial victories or a changed Senate. The litigation strategy is the near-term lever for the federal track; the state organizing strategy is the near-term lever for the infrastructure track.

## Section 12: Actionable Intelligence

### 12.1 Most Immediately Actionable (April-December 2026)

**VRA Section 2 private right of action — SCOTUS ruling expected by June 2026.** *Louisiana v. Callais* is before the Supreme Court (reargument October 2025). The conservative majority appears ready to impose an "end point" on race-based remedies. If the Court severely limits VRA Section 2 remediation authority, the legislative response is the John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act — which passed the House in August 2021 but died in the Senate. Organizations should be prepared with the legislative vehicle and state-level alternative strategies before the ruling lands.

**Eighth Circuit VRA private right of action.** The SCOTUS emergency stay (July 24, 2025) temporarily preserved private enforcement in the 7-state Eighth Circuit. The underlying ruling — that only the Attorney General can bring Section 2 claims — will be resolved on the merits by June-July 2026. If the stay lifts and the ruling stands, seven states lose private voting rights litigation capacity entirely.

**SAVE America Act Senate vote.** Faces 60-vote threshold. Democratic senators need 40 votes to sustain a filibuster. As of April 2026, the Senate vote has not yet been scheduled. Organizations coordinating constituent pressure on Senate offices: Campaign Legal Center, Fair Elections Center, Brennan Center, League of Women Voters.

**Trump election EO appeals.** The EO litigation victories (permanent block on Section 2(a), January 2026 block on Sections 2(d) and 3(d)) are at the circuit court appellate stage. The D.C. Circuit and First Circuit will likely rule before the 2026 midterm election cycle.

**NYC PB FY2027 cycle (begins October 2026).** The 14th NYC cycle allocated $30M in FY2026 (93,000 voters, 24 districts). The FY2027 cycle will determine whether the program expands (Speaker Adams has indicated interest in additional districts) or holds steady. It is the highest-profile replicable model in the country.

### 12.2 Legislative Vehicles

**Register America to Vote Act — S. 2822 (119th Congress).** Would implement automatic voter registration at age 18 and through state DMV systems nationally — extending to all states the AVR that 24 states and D.C. have already enacted. No current path to Senate floor. Introduces the legislative vehicle for the period when Senate composition changes.

**Ranked Choice Voting Act — S. 3425 / H.R. 6589 (119th Congress).** Would implement ranked choice voting in federal congressional elections. DC's voter-approved Initiative 83 provides immediate proof-of-concept for the federal model.

**Vote at Home Act of 2025 — H.R. 2847 (119th Congress).** Would allow all eligible voters to vote by mail in federal elections. Combined with AVR, would represent the most significant access expansion since the NVRA.

**John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act.** Not currently introduced in the 119th Congress in its prior form, but the substantive legislative vehicle that responds to SCOTUS VRA erosion. Requires a Senate floor majority + filibuster reform or 60 votes.

**State-level Citizens' Assembly legislation.** Oregon, California, and Colorado are the highest-probability targets in 2026-2027 given existing infrastructure and political will. Oregon's Citizens' Initiative Review (statewide since 2011 — the only such model in the U.S.) is the closest existing precedent.

### 12.3 Active Litigation

**Democracy Docket active cases (April 2026):**
- *LULAC v. Executive Office of the President* (D.D.C.) — Trump election EO Section 2(a) permanently blocked October 31, 2025; Sections 2(d)/(3)(d) blocked January 30, 2026. On appeal.
- *State of California v. Trump* (D. Mass.) — Sections 2(a) and 2(d) injunction confirmed. On appeal.
- *League of Women Voters of Ohio v. LaRose* (filed February 13, 2026) — challenging Ohio voter purge program targeting alleged noncitizens using unreliable data
- *Louisiana v. Callais* (SCOTUS, decision expected by June 2026) — VRA Section 2 race-conscious remediation authority; potential to rewrite voting rights litigation landscape
- *Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa Indians v. Howe* (SCOTUS) — private right of action under VRA Section 2; companion to the Eighth Circuit private enforcement ruling

**Brennan Center election EO challenge.** Filed on behalf of a coalition including the League of Women Voters and the NAACP, directly challenging EO 14248's citizenship documentation requirement as beyond executive authority.

### 12.4 Organizations and Their Roles

**Brennan Center for Justice** (brennancenter.org) — primary litigation and policy shop for voting rights defense; maintains the Voting Rights Litigation Tracker (real-time case tracking, usable for monitoring); publishes the definitive legislative analysis of election-related bills.

**Democracy Docket** (democracydocket.com) — Marc Elias's litigation operation, the most active voting rights litigation organization in the country; real-time case database is the best single resource for tracking active lawsuits. Tracks 115+ pages of active cases.

**Campaign Legal Center** (campaignlegal.org) — non-partisan voting rights litigation and policy; specific focus on campaign finance + voting rights intersection; authored the most specific public analysis of the SAVE Act's documentary burden on 21M voters.

**Fair Elections Center** (fairelectionscenter.org) — litigation + advocacy specializing in disenfranchised communities; new leadership (Rebekah Caruthers, summer 2025).

**League of Women Voters** (lwv.org) — oldest voting rights organization in the country; plaintiff in multiple EO challenges; strong state chapter network for state-level AVR and RCV campaigns.

**People Powered** (peoplepowered.org) — the primary global network for participatory democracy infrastructure; tracks Citizens' Assemblies, PB, and digital deliberation; connects U.S. reformers to international practitioners.

**Participatory Budgeting Project** (participatorybudgeting.org) — U.S.-specific PB organizing and support; primary resource for cities looking to launch programs; published the foundational research on U.S. PB program design.

**DemocracyNext** (demnext.org) — tech-enhanced deliberative assemblies; research and practice organization focused on digital participation tools and assembly design; relevant to any U.S. Citizens' Assembly design process.

**Oregon Citizens' Initiative Review Commission** — the only institutionalized Citizens' Review model in the U.S. (since 2011); provides direct precedent for federal Citizens' Assembly design; staff and commissioners available as design consultants.

### 12.5 Near-Term 2026 Windows

**Window 1: SCOTUS VRA ruling (June 2026).** The *Louisiana v. Callais* decision will land during the 2026 midterm campaign season. If it significantly restricts VRA Section 2 remedies, it will accelerate the deterioration of majority-minority districts before November 2026. Organizations must be positioned to respond immediately with the Lewis VRA legislative vehicle and litigation alternatives. The ruling is also the highest-profile opportunity to make voting rights a midterm issue.

**Window 2: SAVE America Act Senate vote (timing TBD, 2026).** A Senate floor vote would be the single most visible participation battleground of 2026. Requires 41 Senate votes to block. The floor debate, whatever its outcome, provides the organizing moment to demonstrate the 21-million-voter harm case to the public.

**Window 3: State-level RCV and AVR campaigns, November 2026.** At least 17 states are considering RCV legislation; Washington D.C.'s Initiative 83 provides the model. Oregon's 2026 legislative session is the highest-priority target for Citizens' Assembly legislation given existing review infrastructure. State ballot initiatives for AVR are viable in Arizona, Nevada, and Michigan — all states where initiative petition processes are accessible.

**Window 4: NYC PB FY2027 expansion (October 2026 cycle launch).** If Speaker Adams expands from 24 to 30+ districts, it would be the largest PB program in U.S. history and the strongest replication model for other cities. The expansion decision will be made in budget negotiations in mid-2026 — an organizing pressure point for participatory democracy advocates.

**Window 5: Federal Citizens' Assembly state-level pilots (2026-2027 state legislative sessions).** Oregon, California, Colorado, and Minnesota are the realistic first-mover states. Oregon's existing Citizens' Initiative Review Commission is the legislative chassis; the ask is expanding its scope and funding. The Oregon 2027 legislative session (following the November 2026 elections) is the most likely first success window if groundwork is laid in 2026.

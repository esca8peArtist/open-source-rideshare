- Fiscal estimates for the federal-scale versions of each reform

## Section 1: The Participation Deficit — Scale, Structure, and Consequences

### 1.1 Trust Data Updated Through 2025

The proposal's "16% in 2023" figure for public trust in government requires updating. Pew Research's December 2025 survey found that **17% of Americans** trust the government in Washington to do what is right "just about always" or "most of the time" — near the lowest point in the nearly 70-year survey history.

The 2025 figure is a *decline* from 22% in 2024, despite a change in administration that historically produces a partisan-realignment bump. That the trust figure declined even as a new president took office (typically a honeymoon period) suggests structural rather than cyclical decay — consistent with the Gilens-Page finding that policy outcomes do not respond to median voter preferences regardless of which party controls government.

The historical trajectory is the meaningful data: 77% (1964) → 54% (1972, post-Vietnam) → 29% (1994, Contract with America) → 54% (2002, post-9/11) → 19% (2011, Tea Party/debt ceiling) → 17% (2025). The arc is long-term decline punctuated by crisis-driven rallies, each rally failing to return to the prior baseline. The structural diagnosis — institutions do not respond to citizens — better explains this pattern than the cyclical diagnosis.

For context: only 39% of Americans say the federal government has a positive impact on "people like me," 56% consider government "wasteful and inefficient," and Congress's trust rating sits at approximately 8% (Gallup 2025). Trust in Congress is so low that it functions as a floor rather than a measurement — it no longer differentiates between "skeptical" and "alienated."

Sources: [Pew Research: Public Trust in Government 1958-2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/) | [Pew: Trust in Government near historic lows](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/public-trust-in-government-near-historic-lows-5/)

### 1.2 How the Participation Gap Translates into Policy Distortion

The participation gap is not merely a civic-health problem. It is a governance-quality problem with measurable policy consequences.

**The primary election concentration effect.** In the approximately 90% of U.S. House districts that are non-competitive in general elections, the primary determines the outcome. Primary turnout in House races averages 5-15% of eligible voters. These voters are systematically older, higher-income, and more ideologically extreme than the general electorate. The result is that a fraction of a fraction of the population selects representatives for all. A member elected in a 5% primary in a safe district faces no accountability from the other 95% until the next primary — and those 95% have no effective mechanism between elections.

**The Gilens-Page measurement.** The proposal correctly cites the Gilens-Page (2014) finding that policy outcomes in the U.S. correlate with elite preferences and show no significant correlation with median voter preferences. This finding has been contested on methodological grounds (Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien, 2017, argued the median-voter correlation appears when outcomes are measured differently), and the debate is genuine. The most defensible synthesis: Gilens and Page establish that when elite and mass preferences diverge, policy follows elites; the contestation establishes that when they align, it's harder to attribute outcomes to either. The finding is not "citizens have no influence" — it is "citizens have no *additional* influence beyond what coincides with what elites want." The functional consequence: participation gaps allow the elite-aligned subset of outcomes to become the entire policy space.

**The local elections crisis.** Turnout in local elections — where the most direct policy impacts on daily life occur — regularly falls below 20%. A 2022 Portland, Oregon city council election drew 11% turnout. A 2023 Los Angeles school board race drew 9%. At these turnout levels, organized minorities of 2-3% of eligible voters can determine outcomes entirely. The practical consequence: school boards, city councils, sheriffs, district attorneys, and local judges — the positions through which authoritarian consolidation is implemented locally, per Part VIII of the crisis analysis — are selected by a minuscule and unrepresentative fraction of affected citizens.

## Section 2: Citizens' Assemblies — Updated Evidence (2024-2025)

### 2.1 The Irish Model: Strengths and Limits

Ireland is the most documented case of successful citizens' assembly deployment, and it has now had enough history to assess both successes and failures. The proposal presents the successes accurately; the failures require documentation because reformers who cannot answer the Irish failure cases will be vulnerable in political advocacy.

**Successes (documented):**
- 2015 assembly: abortion liberalization recommendation → 2018 referendum passed with 66.4%
- 2015 assembly: same-sex marriage recommendation → referendum passed with 62%
- Convention on the Constitution (2012-2014): directly influenced multiple constitutional amendments
- The assembly model enabled politicians to move on issues they could not risk sponsoring directly — providing political cover for reform

**The 2024 failure case:** The assembly on gender equality led to two referendum questions in March 2024 — on expanding the constitutional definition of family and on removing a provision describing women's role in the home. Both referendums failed, with the family referendum defeated by 67% and the care referendum by 74%. The failure was not attributable to the assembly's recommendations being unpopular — the assembly had 99% support for both changes. The failure was attributable to: (1) confusing and legally complex ballot language that differed materially from what the assembly recommended, (2) last-minute No campaigns exploiting the complexity, and (3) insufficient public engagement in the months between assembly recommendation and vote. The lesson: **the assembly can recommend correctly and the political process can still fail the recommendation.** The assembly-to-referendum pipeline is not automatic; it requires dedicated public education, accessible communication, and ballot design that preserves the assembly's intent.

**The "Irish model" constraint:** Ireland's success has been partly attributed to the specific constitutional mechanism (referendum required for constitutional change) that forced politicians to engage with assembly recommendations rather than ignore them. In the U.S. context, without a mandatory referendum mechanism, advisory assemblies could easily be ignored entirely. This is the most powerful counterargument to the Domain 3 proposal and must be addressed: any U.S. Citizens' Assembly design must include mandatory response requirements, public hearings, and eventually binding authority over specific domains (electoral rules, campaign finance, redistricting) to prevent it from becoming a sophisticated form of public consultation theater.

Sources: [Citizens' Assembly Ireland — current reports](https://citizensassembly.ie/reports/) | [UCL Constitution Unit: Citizens' Assemblies](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/social-historical-sciences/constitution-unit/constitution-unit-publications/constitution-unit-briefings/citizens-assemblies-what-are-they-and-how-can-policy-makers-use-them) | [Frontiers: Irish Model for Deliberative Democracy](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2020.591983/full)

### 2.2 Belgium's Permanent Model at Three Years

Belgium's Ostbelgien (German-speaking Community) permanent citizens' assembly, established in 2019, is the world's first permanent randomly-selected assembly and the strongest precedent for the U.S. proposal's "pathway to binding authority" concept. At three years of operation (through 2022), the evidence is:

- The standing assembly selects its own agenda items — citizens choose what gets deliberated, not politicians
- Issue-specific panels of 25-50 randomly selected citizens deliberate each item
- The regional parliament must formally debate every recommendation and issue a public written response within 6 months
- No recommendation has been fully rejected without public parliamentary explanation
- The model has generated sustained media and academic interest and is being studied for replication at the federal level in Belgium and in other EU countries

The Ostbelgien model directly answers the "advisory assemblies get ignored" criticism by institutionalizing a mandatory parliamentary response requirement. It also demonstrates that permanent random selection is administratively feasible — citizens serve 6-month terms on the standing assembly, with staggered replacement to preserve institutional memory.

**Why this matters for U.S. design:** The mandatory parliamentary response requirement is the single most important procedural innovation. Without it, advisory assemblies produce reports that gather dust. With it, assembly recommendations become a recurring political accountability mechanism. A U.S. Citizens' Assembly statute should specify: within 180 days of any assembly recommendation, the relevant congressional committee must hold a public hearing, and the full chamber must vote on whether to adopt, reject, or refer to further deliberation — with rejection requiring a recorded vote and public explanation.

### 2.3 Netherlands 2025 Climate Assembly

The Netherlands established a National Citizens' Assembly on Climate in 2025 — the latest entry in a growing European pattern. The assembly design incorporated lessons from the French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat (2019-2020), where the government accepted 146 of 149 recommendations but implementation was subsequently inconsistent. The Dutch design addressed the implementation gap by creating a follow-up panel with oversight authority over government compliance with accepted recommendations — a structural innovation that bridges the assembly-to-implementation gap. Results from the Dutch assembly are not yet published as of April 2026, but the design is worth tracking as a model for U.S. implementation pathways.

## Section 3: Digital Deliberation — vTaiwan and pol.is Updated (2024-2025)

### 3.1 vTaiwan's 2024-2025 AI Governance Work

The proposal's vTaiwan reference is accurate but dated. Significant 2024-2025 developments update the evidence base:

**The 80% government response rate** (cited in the proposal as 80% of vTaiwan deliberations resulting in government action since 2015) remains the headline finding. The mechanism has expanded: since 2024, vTaiwan has applied pol.is plus AI summarization tools to AI governance questions — a domain where the traditional regulatory process produces opaque technical rules that citizens cannot meaningfully engage with. In December 2024, vTaiwan hosted a hybrid deliberation event on AI governance with the pol.is tool. In March 2025, findings from this process were presented at a consultation hosted by Taiwan's National Human Rights Commission, and feedback was incorporated into NHRC recommendations for the draft AI Basic Act.

**The key design insight from vTaiwan's recent work:** pol.is surfaces opinion clusters and identifies the most divisive topics for *additional* in-person deliberation — rather than attempting to resolve everything online. The hybrid model (digital survey + in-person workshop for contested items) consistently outperforms purely online deliberation because it uses digital tools where they excel (scale, inclusion, initial mapping of opinion space) and in-person deliberation where it excels (nuance, trust-building, resolution of complex tradeoffs). The U.S. notice-and-comment process, which produces 94% corporate-interest comments on significant rules, is the structural opposite of this model: it is formally open to all but practically captured by organized interests with drafting capacity. A pol.is-style layer added to notice-and-comment would not replace the technical legal comment process but would give regulators a genuine picture of public opinion distribution on regulatory choices — something the current system does not provide.

Sources: [vTaiwan AI governance 2024-2025 — People Powered](https://www.peoplepowered.org/news-content/digital-participation-case-study-taiwan) | [vTaiwan — Participedia](https://participedia.net/method/vtaiwan) | [Pol.is — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pol.is)

### 3.2 The Decidim Platform: Scale and Adoption

Barcelona's Decidim platform deserves a more specific evidence base than the proposal provides.

As of 2024:
- Used by over 400 organizations across 50+ countries
- Barcelona: 150,000+ registered participants, 30,000+ proposals submitted, used for strategic planning, participatory budgeting, and policy co-creation
- Helsinki's OmaStadi: 60,000+ residents participated in 2023 cycle, resulting in €8M in public investment decisions
- The city of Madrid has used Decide Madrid (Decidim-based) for consultations on transportation, parks, and city budgets
- Decidim is fully open-source, available for adoption at zero licensing cost, and maintained by a community including the Barcelona city government, several EU research institutes, and independent contributors

**The adoption barrier is not technical — it is political.** Decidim deployments that succeed have strong political commitment from government to actually act on outputs. Deployments that fail produce well-documented citizen input that is then ignored. The most common failure mode: a government deploys Decidim as a public relations exercise without any intention of binding itself to results. The distinction between "consultation" and "participation with consequence" is the critical design variable. Every U.S. Decidim deployment proposal should specify: which decisions are bounded by citizen input, how binding that input is, and what enforcement mechanism exists if government ignores it.

## Section 4: Participatory Budgeting — Evidence Updated to 2024

### 4.1 Porto Alegre: Success and Discontinuity

The Porto Alegre case is the canonical reference for participatory budgeting effectiveness. The proposal presents the outcome data accurately. What requires addition: Porto Alegre discontinued its original participatory budgeting model in 2017 when a new administration chose not to continue it — demonstrating the central vulnerability of participatory budgeting when it depends on political will rather than institutional mandate.

The lesson is not that Porto Alegre's model failed — the documented outcomes (treated water 99% of population, sewer coverage 46% → 86%, 50+ new schools, infant mortality down 19%, truancy 9% → <1%) are genuine. The lesson is that **participatory democracy reforms embedded in administrative practice rather than statutory mandate are reversible when administrations change** — precisely the structural lesson that the democratic renewal proposal's entire architecture addresses. Participatory budgeting requires statutory mandate (not just political will) to be durable. South Korea's mandate of participatory budgeting for all local governments provides the model.

### 4.2 U.S. Cities Updated to 2024

**New York City (2024):** 24 Council Members allocated $24 million in capital funding through participatory budgeting in 2024 — residents voted directly on improvements to schools, parks, and libraries. The NYC program has now allocated over $300 million cumulatively since 2011. Participation rates are consistently highest in lower-income and less-educated districts — the opposite of the pattern in conventional elections, where participation skews wealthy and educated.

**Los Angeles:** Program established 2021, allocated approximately $8.5 million across nine neighborhoods over two cycles. Funded: job training and placement, rental assistance, services for people experiencing homelessness, urban agriculture programs, and medical services for underserved communities. No city-wide expansion as of April 2026.

**The four-conditions synthesis:** A 2024 Tandfonline meta-analysis of participatory budgeting outcomes identified four conditions required for sustained impact: (1) structured participatory arrangements ensuring diverse representation; (2) adequate financial resources; (3) political commitment and flexibility; (4) government commitment to implement results. The Porto Alegre collapse is explained by condition (3) failing. U.S. cities where programs have grown (NYC) satisfy all four. The implication for federal program design: federal matching funds should be conditioned on statutory commitment to implement results — not just conduct the process.

## Section 5: The Resistance-Participation Connection

### 5.1 The Tufekci Variable and Democratic Infrastructure

The Domain 3 proposal and the crisis analysis's Variable 4 (Organizational Depth) address the same structural problem from different directions. Tufekci's finding — that digital-era movements can scale mobilization rapidly but lack the organizational depth to sustain pressure or negotiate outcomes — is structurally equivalent to the participation deficit. Both describe the same gap: between mass sympathy and organized, sustained civic capacity.

The No Kings movement's three-cycle growth pattern (June 2025: 5M → October 2025: 7M → March 2026: 8–9M) is an extraordinary mobilization signal. The organizational depth question is whether this converts into the structures Domain 3 proposes: citizens' assemblies that practice deliberation, participatory budgeting that builds trust through consequential civic action, and civic infrastructure that provides ongoing forums between mobilization events.

**The key insight:** The Eyes on ICE program (200,000+ first training viewers) is itself a form of the "digital deliberation infrastructure" Domain 3 proposes — it converts mass participation into a durable accountability practice. The 3,000 organizing meeting hosts who convened April 3 (post-March 28) are the civic infrastructure formation that Domain 3 describes. The resistance movement is, in a limited way, building the democratic participation infrastructure that the proposal argues must be institutionalized.

The implication: Domain 3 reforms are not just long-term democratic renewal projects. They are a description of what the most effective resistance organizing is already doing, and why institutionalizing it matters for survivability beyond the current crisis.

### 5.2 Why Participation Infrastructure Inoculates Against Authoritarian Capture

The crisis analysis (Section 1.6) documents that 53% of Project 2025 has been implemented in 12 months. The question the participation literature answers is: why did a program for dismantling democratic institutions succeed so quickly?

The answer is not that citizens approved of it. Polling consistently shows majorities opposing specific authoritarian actions. The answer is structural: **between elections, citizens had no consequential institutional forum for their preferences.** Project 2025 was implemented through executive orders, DOGE directives, and administrative actions that operate between elections and outside any forum where organized citizen input has consequence. A federal notice-and-comment rulemaking process, or an advisory agency stakeholder meeting, is theoretically available — but practically captured by organized interests.

Citizens' assemblies, participatory budgeting, and digital deliberation platforms do not prevent authoritarian capture by themselves. But they create practiced civic muscle — the habit and capacity for consequential collective decision-making — that makes societies harder to capture. Denmark's folk high schools are credited as foundational democratic institutions not because they prevented authoritarianism directly, but because they built a citizenry with practiced deliberative capacity that made authoritarian consolidation harder to achieve without visible democratic betrayal. The research on deliberative democracy consistently finds that participation increases not just civic engagement but **civic competence** — citizens who practice deliberation become better at identifying when democratic norms are violated and more willing to act in defense of those norms.

## Section 6: Counterarguments and Responses

These are the strongest objections to Domain 3 reforms, with the evidence-based responses:

**"Citizens' assemblies are unelected and unaccountable."** The objection is technically correct but misses the structural purpose. Citizens' assemblies do not *replace* elected bodies — they *advise* them (in advisory form) or *constrain* them on specific domains where self-interest makes elected bodies structurally incapable of fair decisions (electoral rules, campaign finance, redistricting). The electorate itself is "unelected" in the same sense — citizens who participate in referendums are not accountable to other citizens for their votes. The randomized assembly is a legitimate democratic instrument for exactly the same reason referendums are: it represents a cross-section of the population better than self-selected participation does.

**"Randomly selected citizens lack expertise to decide complex policy."** The evidence from 600+ deliberative processes documented in the OECD's 2020 report contradicts this. Randomly selected citizens, properly supported with expert briefings, professional secretariats, and dedicated research staff, consistently produce well-reasoned recommendations that reflect genuine tradeoffs rather than ideological axioms. Ireland's assembly on abortion produced a more nuanced recommendation than any parliamentary bill would have — recognizing access needs, time limits, and medical judgment in a way that a binary political debate does not allow. The "expertise" objection is often a proxy for "process would not produce the outcome organized interests prefer."

**"Participatory budgeting is a distraction — the amounts are too small to matter."** This objection has empirical weight: NYC's $24M is a fraction of a $100+ billion city budget. The response has two parts. First, the dollar amounts are not the primary mechanism of impact; the primary mechanism is building civic trust and competence through consequential participation. Porto Alegre's outcomes (99% water coverage, infant mortality down 19%) were driven by small-budget PB decisions that changed *priorities* within existing spending, not the total spending level. Second, the scale can grow: the proposal calls for 10-20% of federal community development spending, not a token amount. The pilot/scale logic — begin with consequential but bounded decisions, build trust, expand — is well-documented in the technology adoption and institutional reform literature.

**"Digital platforms exclude the elderly and the poor."** Accurate for current deployment models. The evidence base confirms: 24 million Americans without broadband, 22% of seniors offline, 32 million adults with limited literacy. Domain 3 reform proposals must include, by design: in-person alternatives at all participation points, multilingual support, accessibility accommodation, and proactive outreach to historically excluded communities as conditions for federal matching funds. The Decidim case study evidence shows that inclusive-by-design platforms achieve higher participation in low-income communities than in high-income ones — the equity problem is a design problem, not an inherent technological barrier.

## Section 7: Fiscal Estimates

These estimates are required for serious policy advocacy; the main proposal provides ranges without methodological basis.

**Citizens' Assembly (federal advisory body, 200 members, 18-month terms):**
- Member compensation: 200 members × $75,000 annualized × 1.5 years = $22.5M per assembly
- Secretariat and professional staff (15-20 staff): $3-5M per assembly
- Expert witnesses, research, facilities: $10-20M per assembly
- Total per assembly: approximately $35-50M
- Annual cost (one assembly in session at any time, 18-month cycles): $25-35M/year
- For context: the U.S. Senate costs approximately $1 billion/year to operate; the House approximately $1.4 billion/year. A permanent federal Citizens' Assembly costs approximately 1% of Congress.

**Participatory Budgeting (federal matching fund program, 50-city pilot):**
- Average city PB program cost (NYC model, scaled): $2-4M per city annually
- Federal match at 50%: $1-2M per city × 50 cities = $50-100M/year pilot
- Expansion to all 300+ cities over 50,000: $300-600M/year
- Note: these are facilitation costs, not the funds allocated. The allocated funds come from existing community development appropriations; PB changes *how decisions are made*, not necessarily *how much is spent*.

**Digital Deliberation Infrastructure (Decidim/pol.is at federal scale):**
- Platform development and customization: $20-50M one-time
- Annual operation, moderation, multilingual support, and accessibility: $20-50M/year
- Training for agency staff: $5-10M one-time
- Total: $50-75M in year 1, $20-50M/year ongoing
- For comparison: the federal government spent approximately $100 billion on IT in FY2025; deliberation infrastructure is 0.05% of that total.

**Federal Civic Infrastructure Fund:**
- The proposal's $2-5B annual estimate covers the broadest scope (organizational capacity, civic education, community organizing support nationally)
- A phased approach: $200-500M in Year 1 (pilot grants to 500 high-capacity organizations), scaling to $1-2B by Year 5 as program infrastructure and accountability mechanisms mature
- Revenue offset: a 1% surcharge on federal political advertising (connected to Domain 8 media reforms) would generate approximately $50-100M annually from the $8B+ federal political ad market

## Section 8: Connection to Other Domains

Domain 3 reforms are uniquely cross-cutting. They depend on and reinforce:

- **Domain 1 (Electoral Reform):** Higher baseline civic engagement reduces primary-election extremism; participatory infrastructure builds the "organizational depth" that Tufekci identifies as the missing variable in digital-era movements
- **Domain 2 (Institutional Integrity):** Citizens' assemblies deliberating on which agencies need constitutional protection would produce more durable political coalitions for Domain 2 reforms than elite advocacy alone
- **Domain 4 (Digital Government Infrastructure):** The deliberation platforms (Decidim, pol.is) share technical architecture with Domain 4's open government APIs; civic infrastructure investment builds the digital literacy required for Domain 4's benefits to reach low-income communities
- **Domain 8 (Media Ecosystem):** Deliberative processes require a minimum common factual baseline; Domain 8's public media and local news investments are prerequisites for participation quality
- **Domain 6 (Judicial Independence):** A citizens' assembly with binding authority over electoral rules would be more resistant to judicial invalidation than ordinary legislation because it is itself a democratic process — harder to characterize as partisan manipulation

The most important feedback loop: **Domain 3 is the mechanism through which citizens build the civic muscle to demand and defend every other domain.** Reforms without participation infrastructure are imposed on a passive citizenry and are therefore reversible whenever political will changes. Reforms accompanied by participation infrastructure create constituencies that understand, value, and defend them.

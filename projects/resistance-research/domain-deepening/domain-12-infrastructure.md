# Domain 12: Infrastructure — Evidence Deepening

*Prepared April 17, 2026. Supplements the Infrastructure domain of the Democratic Renewal Proposal. Does not restate the reform architecture — deepens the evidentiary foundation with specific data, current developments, contested findings, and actionable intelligence. Emphasis on the IIJA funding cliff arriving September 30, 2026.*

---

## Overview

Infrastructure is not a neutral technical category — it is the physical substrate of democratic participation. Unequal broadband access produces unequal information access and economic exclusion. Grid failures kill people and concentrate those deaths in low-income and non-white communities. Lead service lines cause irreversible neurological damage predominantly in children of color. Failing transit systems immobilize the car-poor, disproportionately women, elderly, and working class. Every infrastructure failure listed in the ASCE 2025 Report Card (overall grade: **C**, first-ever non-failure grade since 1998, driven largely by IIJA investment) is also a democratic failure — a differential withdrawal of public provision that compounds every other axis of exclusion.

The IIJA cliff arriving on September 30, 2026 is the most urgent infrastructure policy event in a generation. The law authorized $1.2 trillion in infrastructure investment. As of January 31, 2026, $568 billion has been allocated to approximately 68,000 projects — but only $275 billion has been obligated. Authorization expiration does not vaporize obligated funds, but it stops the pipeline of new awards, reverts formula funding toward pre-IIJA baselines, and ends dozens of discretionary programs with no automatic successor. This document tracks that cliff and the Trump administration's systematic dismantling of IIJA programs in advance of it.

## IIJA Funding Cliff — September 2026

### What Expires on September 30, 2026

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (Pub. L. 117-58, November 2021) authorizes federal surface transportation and related infrastructure programs through the end of fiscal year 2026, which ends September 30, 2026. The surface transportation authorization within IIJA — formally the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law — built on and extended the FAST Act (2015), which was itself a five-year bill.

What happens at expiration depends on program type:

**Formula programs** (largest category — highway, bridge, transit): Without reauthorization, these revert to pre-IIJA authorization levels. IIJA significantly elevated formula allocations; the cliff back to FAST Act baselines would be steep. State DOTs plan 3-6 year capital programs; mid-program funding cuts force project cancellation, scope reduction, or emergency state bond issuance. The current annual federal transportation outlay exceeds $102 billion. The gas tax highway trust fund generates approximately $44 billion in 2028. The structural gap — roughly $58 billion annually — cannot be bridged without either new revenue, massive cuts, or deficit appropriations.

**Discretionary grant programs**: Programs including RAISE/BUILD grants, Safe Streets grants, Reconnecting Communities, Carbon Reduction Program, and the Corridor Identification and Development Program (passenger rail) would cease making new awards immediately upon authorization lapse. Existing awarded but unobligated grants remain; new solicitations stop.

**Program-specific appropriations**: Programs with specific multi-year appropriations (NEVI, BEAD, certain water programs) have funding windows that either pre-expired or run through 2026 regardless of surface transportation reauthorization. Their post-cliff fate depends on individual reauthorization language.

As of January 31, 2026: $568 billion allocated, $275 billion obligated — meaning approximately $293 billion in allocated but not yet obligated IIJA funds exists in a zone of political vulnerability even before September 30. [Source: [Funding Landscape, Infrastructure and IIJA Funding in 2026](https://fundinglandscape.com/answers/infrastructure-iija-funding-2026)]

### Congressional Math for Reauthorization

Surface transportation reauthorization has passed on time exactly once since 1991 (the 2005 SAFETEA-LU). Congress operated under short-term extensions for roughly a third of the time since 1991. [Source: [Transportation for America, Five Reasons IIJA Will Expire Without Replacement](https://t4america.org/2025/07/23/five-reasons-why-iija-will-expire-without-a-replacement-in-september-2026/)]

As of April 2026, no reauthorization bill has been introduced. The obstacles are structural:

1. **Budget math**: The July 2025 reconciliation bill added approximately $3 trillion to the federal deficit, depleting Republican appetite for further spending. Maintaining IIJA spending levels would require outlays exceeding $102 billion annually against a gas tax revenue base of ~$44 billion — a $58+ billion annual gap.

2. **Demolished coalition**: The 2021 IIJA passed on a bipartisan 69-30 Senate vote in part because Democrats saw the law as a vehicle for clean energy transition programs. The Trump administration's systematic termination of NEVI, Reconnecting Communities, and "green" transportation programs — and the $2.3 billion in IIJA rescissions through early 2026 — has fractured Democratic willingness to negotiate a successor bill that would legitimize what the administration kept while abandoning what it gutted.

3. **Advance appropriations mechanism exhausted**: The 2021 IIJA used novel "advance appropriations" to budget multi-year spending outside the standard annual appropriations process. This fiscal engineering is not infinitely replicable; the current Congress has already rescinded portions of that advance appropriated money.

4. **Republican program preferences**: Republican leadership has indicated priority for highway and bridge programs while opposing transit, passenger rail, EV charging, and climate-linked transportation spending. A Republican-only bill would likely represent a significant retrenchment from IIJA breadth. A bipartisan deal requires Democratic buy-in that the political conditions no longer support.

The most likely scenario: a short-term continuing resolution extension of surface transportation programs at existing authorization levels, avoiding the cliff technically but also preventing any new multi-year project commitments. This is the infrastructure version of governing by stopgap — precisely the failure mode the Democratic Renewal Proposal is designed to structurally prevent.

### The $2.3 Billion Rescission — What Was Cut

Before the cliff arrives, the Trump administration has already rescinded over $2.3 billion in IIJA-authorized funds through the FY2026 appropriations and executive action. The largest single target:

**National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) program**: $879 million rescinded via FY2026 transportation spending bill. Breakdown: $503.8 million in formula grants, $300 million in competitive grants, $75 million for the Joint Office of Energy and Transportation. The rescission was proportional to each state's unobligated NEVI balance — states that had moved slowly lost more. [Source: [Funding Landscape](https://fundinglandscape.com/answers/infrastructure-iija-funding-2026)]

The NEVI rescission also triggered multi-state litigation. A U.S. District Court judge ruled in January 2026 that the Trump administration had unlawfully suspended the EV charger program, ordering restoration of funds. The administration had argued the January 20, 2025 "Unleashing American Energy" executive order authorized the suspension; the court rejected this interpretation. As of April 2026, litigation continues. [Source: [CNBC, Trump administration unlawfully suspended EV charger program](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/24/us-judge-trump-admin-unlawfully-suspended-ev-charger-program.html)]

### Trump Administration Transition — IIJA Implementation Disrupted

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed EO "Unleashing American Energy," directing all federal agencies to immediately pause disbursement of IIJA and IRA funds. The OMB's implementing memorandum restricted the pause to programs "implicating" the EO's "Terminating the Green New Deal" provisions — but the definitional ambiguity was not resolved for months, during which approximately $125 billion in unobligated DOT funds sat in limbo. [Source: [Crowell & Moring, Navigating the Trump Administration's Pause](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/navigating-the-trump-administrations-pause-on-iija-and-ira-funding-key-implications-for-infrastructure-stakeholders)]

By June 2025, most transportation formula funds had been released from the pause following court orders and OMB clarification. However, programs with climate linkages — carbon reduction, low-carbon transit, EV charging, active transportation — remained contested. [Source: [Georgetown Climate Center, Understanding Recent Federal Actions](https://www.georgetownclimate.org/articles/explainer-dot-funding-low-carbon-transportation.html)]

The transition disruption created real-world consequences: states that had based capital program planning on IIJA-level funding allocations faced multi-month periods of uncertainty. Local transportation agencies that had begun procurement processes for IIJA-funded projects saw timelines slip. The legal overhead of navigating funding freeze litigation consumed capacity at state DOTs and municipal agencies least equipped to absorb it.

### What Happens to States Mid-Project if Authorization Lapses

**Obligated funds are protected**: Once the federal government has executed a grant agreement obligating funds to a specific project, those funds do not disappear at authorization expiration. Ongoing construction on obligated projects continues to draw federal reimbursement.

**Unobligated allocations are vulnerable**: Formula allocations that have been announced but not yet converted into specific grant agreements are subject to congressional action. A continuing resolution at pre-IIJA levels would strand these allocations.

**New solicitations stop**: Discretionary grant programs cease accepting applications. States and cities that have been preparing competitive grant applications — often months or years of staff time — lose the funding opportunity with no successor mechanism.

**Planning investments become stranded**: States have funded planning studies, environmental reviews, and preliminary engineering on the assumption of future IIJA-funded construction grants. Without reauthorization, these planning investments may not lead to construction funding, representing sunk public cost with no project delivery.

The worst-case political economy: Congress passes a continuing resolution at reduced authorization levels. States are forced to scale back or cancel projects mid-development. Political credit for visible construction (already built) accrues to the IIJA; political blame for cancellations (invisible non-delivery) accrues to ambiguous federal dysfunction. The communities harmed are disproportionately those whose projects were approved later in the IIJA cycle — which, given how competitive discretionary grants work, skews toward smaller, lower-capacity, and more rural jurisdictions that took longer to assemble applications.

## 12a: Broadband — BEAD, ACP, and Digital Equity

### ACP Death: 23 Million Households, May 2024

The Affordable Connectivity Program (ACP) formally ended May 2024 after Congress declined to appropriate the $6 billion in additional funding the FCC requested to continue it. At peak enrollment, the program served 23 million households — primarily low-income, rural, tribal, elderly, and disabled populations — with a $30/month subsidy (or $75/month on qualifying tribal lands). [Source: [StateScoop, 23 million households lose internet discounts](https://statescoop.com/affordable-connectivity-program-ends-2024/)]

The aftermath, documented at one year post-expiration: approximately 5 million of the 23 million enrolled households cut internet service entirely rather than pay full market rates. Most remaining households downgraded to slower, cheaper tiers. The Brookings Institution had warned pre-expiration that ACP termination would "widen the digital divide"; the data bears this out. [Source: [Broadband Breakfast, One Year Without the Affordable Connectivity Program](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/one-year-without-the-affordable-connectivity-program/)]

The Michigan case study is instructive: approximately one-third of Michigan households with internet access relied on ACP. In BEAD-targeted buildout areas, an estimated one-quarter to one-third of households served by new BEAD infrastructure may not be able to afford subscription without ACP-equivalent subsidy — meaning BEAD builds infrastructure that low-income households still cannot access. [Source: [Pew Charitable Trusts, States Reckon With Lapse of ACP](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2024/09/20/states-reckon-with-lapse-of-the-broadband-affordable-connectivity-program)]

This is the gap the Democratic Renewal Proposal's 12a reforms directly address: physical infrastructure (BEAD-funded fiber and fixed wireless) is necessary but not sufficient. Without a successor affordability program, the 5 million households who went offline and the millions more who downgraded remain on the wrong side of the divide regardless of what broadband is built nearby.

### BEAD Rollout Status — April 2026

The Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) Program was authorized at $42.45 billion, the largest single federal broadband investment in history. Despite the 2021 authorization, as of August 2025, **zero BEAD funding had been distributed for any eligible broadband deployment project**. [Source: [Light Reading, 2025 in Review: The BEAD Goes On](https://www.lightreading.com/broadband/2025-in-review-the-bead-goes-on-)]

The rollout sequence — formula allocations to states, state initial proposals, NTIA review, final proposals, approval, subgrantee selection, contract execution, construction — takes years by design for accountability purposes. All 56 states and territories submitted Final Proposals; as of February 2026, NTIA had approved 26 state plans. [Source: [Broadband Breakfast, NTIA Approves 9 BEAD Plans](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/ntia-approves-9-bead-plans-promises-early-2026-guidance-on-non-deployment-funds/)]

**Trump administration BEAD intervention**: In 2025, NTIA issued a Policy Notice directing states to revise approved plans within 90 days to comply with new technology-neutral requirements that removed preferences for fiber-optic deployment and opened eligibility to fixed wireless and satellite providers. The Biden-era fiber preference was justified on longevity and performance grounds; the Trump administration characterized it as anti-competitive. States with approved fiber-centric plans were required to restart subgrantee solicitations, adding months to deployment timelines. [Source: [NTIA, Trump Administration Announces Benefit of the Bargain BEAD Program](https://www.ntia.gov/press-release/2025/trump-administration-announces-benefit-bargain-bead-program-removes-regulatory-burdens-lowers-costs)]

The practical consequence: as of April 2026, no American household has received BEAD-funded broadband service. The program authorized in 2021 to close the digital divide by 2030 is, five years in, still in planning and procurement stages — with construction potentially not beginning in most states until 2027, and completion extending toward 2030 or beyond.

Meanwhile, tribal broadband grants totaling nearly $1 billion remain frozen or delayed pending Trump administration review. Senate Commerce Committee Democrats demanded answers from NTIA in early 2026 about the delays. [Source: [U.S. Senate Commerce Committee, Cantwell, Schatz Demand Answers](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/dem/release/cantwell-schatz-demand-answers-on-trump-administrations-delay-of-nearly-1-billion-in-tribal-broadband-grants/)]

### The Affordability-Infrastructure Gap

BEAD rules technically permit use of funds for affordability programs but only after a state demonstrates it can reach all unserved and underserved locations with deployment projects. Given no state has yet completed deployment planning to that threshold, affordability spending from BEAD is effectively unavailable for years. The program as designed requires ACP or a successor to make the infrastructure it funds actually accessible to the households it is intended to serve.

The Democratic Renewal Proposal's treatment of broadband as a regulated utility with universal service obligation directly addresses this structural failure: utility regulation carries both infrastructure obligation and affordability requirement within a single legal framework, eliminating the gap between what is built and what can be afforded.

## 12b: Energy Grid

### FERC Order 1920 — Status as of April 2026

FERC Order 1920, adopted May 13, 2024, is the most significant federal transmission planning reform in decades. It requires transmission providers to conduct long-term regional planning over a 20-year time horizon, anticipating future generation mix (including renewable integration) rather than merely reacting to interconnection requests. The rule also addresses cost allocation for long-range transmission facilities. [Source: [FERC, Explainer on the Transmission Planning and Cost Allocation Final Rule](https://www.ferc.gov/explainer-transmission-planning-and-cost-allocation-final-rule)]

Order 1920 has been through two subsequent proceedings:
- **Order 1920-A (November 2024)**: Clarified state involvement in regional planning processes and cost allocation mechanisms, strengthening state agency participation rights.
- **Order 1920-B (April 2025)**: Affirmed the core rule and its requirements on rehearing.

The rule faces judicial challenges from utilities, fossil fuel interests, and some state regulators. Multiple petitions for judicial review have been consolidated before the Fourth Circuit. The rules remain in effect while challenges proceed — a meaningful distinction from situations where courts stay rules pending review. Compliance obligations are actively running. [Source: [Harvard EELP, Regional Transmission Planning Rule Tracker](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/regional-transmission-planning-rule/)]

The democratic significance: long-range transmission planning determines where and when renewable energy can be built. Without transmission planned in advance, renewable generation connects to an insufficient grid, forcing curtailment (wasted clean energy) or blocking new projects entirely. The 2024-2025 interconnection queue data makes the scale of this problem explicit.

### Interconnection Queue Crisis — Scale

As of end of 2024, approximately **2,300 gigawatts** of total generation and storage capacity actively sought grid interconnection — a number equal to roughly twice the existing U.S. generating capacity. The queue includes 956 GW of solar, 890 GW of battery storage, and 271 GW of wind. [Source: [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up](https://emp.lbl.gov/queues)]

The average wait for interconnection approval is five years. Projects that enter the queue routinely withdraw before completion — often after years of study costs — because the grid cannot accommodate them on current timelines. This queue is the direct mechanism by which the transmission deficit blocks renewable energy development regardless of how much generation capacity is willing to invest.

FERC Order 2023 (2023) introduced cluster-based "first-ready, first-served" processing intended to clear the backlog faster. The 2024 data shows the first year-over-year decline in total queue capacity (from 2,600 GW in 2023 to 2,300 GW in 2024), which some analysts attribute to queue discipline improvements under Order 2023 and others attribute to increased project withdrawals. The structural backlog has not cleared. [Source: [Zero Emission Grid, Clean Energy Interconnection Backlog — 2025 Trends](https://www.zeroemissiongrid.com/insights-press-zeg-blog/interconnection-backlog/)]

The combined Orders 1920 (long-range planning) and 2023 (interconnection reform) represent the most aggressive FERC action on transmission in memory. Whether they are sufficient — and whether they survive court challenge — determines the pace of renewable integration for the balance of the decade.

### Texas/ERCOT — Five Years Post-Uri

Winter Storm Uri (February 2021) killed at least 246 Texans, caused $195 billion in economic damage, and exposed ERCOT's isolation from interstate interconnections and the grid's lack of weatherization requirements. Five years later:

**What improved**: The Public Utility Commission conducted over 7,400 weatherization inspections in fiscal year 2025. Generators are now required to weatherize equipment. ERCOT's governing board was restructured to add independent members. Texas solar capacity more than doubled between 2023 and 2025; battery storage more than tripled. These additions provided meaningful stability during subsequent winter events. [Source: [ERCOT, Weatherization Update December 2025](https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2025/12/02/16.2-System-Planning-and-Weatherization-Update_Revised.pdf)]

**January 2026 stress test**: A winter storm in January 2026 — on the fifth anniversary of Uri — was navigated without reliability failures. ERCOT attributed performance improvement to weatherization reforms and new generation. Texas Tribune and ERCOT confirmed the grid held. [Source: [Texas Tribune, Texas grid weathers weekend storm](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/01/29/texas-winter-storm-uri-anniversary-power-grid-ercot/)]

**What remains inadequate**: A State Auditor's Office report from August 2025 found the Railroad Commission — which oversees natural gas production and delivery — was inadequately verifying that gas systems had properly hardened against cold weather. Natural gas supply failure was the primary cause of Uri; the regulatory gap in the upstream gas supply chain remains partially unaddressed. [Source: [Community Impact, 5 years post-Uri, experts say challenges still remain](https://communityimpact.com/austin/south-central-austin/texas-legislature/2026/02/19/5-years-post-uri-experts-say-challenges-still-remain-for-texas-power-grid/)]

Critically, ERCOT remains deliberately isolated from the Eastern and Western Interconnections — a political choice by Texas to avoid federal FERC jurisdiction. This isolation limits the ability to import emergency power during extreme events. Uri exposed this constraint fatally; the political economy has not changed it.

**Democratic implication**: Texas's grid governance structure — deregulated, isolated, and reluctant to accept federal standards — is a case study in how fragmented infrastructure governance produces catastrophic failures that are then absorbed as individual suffering rather than political accountability. The 246 deaths from Uri have not been assigned legal responsibility at the utility level. The Democratic Renewal Proposal's treatment of grid resilience as a public good requiring federal minimum standards, not as a private market offering with optional weatherization, is directly responsive to this governance failure.

## 12c: Water Infrastructure

### Lead Service Line Replacement — National Progress

The IIJA invested $15 billion toward lead service line replacement (LSLR) through the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund (DWSRF), distributed as $3 billion annually across five fiscal years (2022-2026). As of the FY2025 allocation round, EPA distributed the fourth of five annual tranches, now using inventory data rather than survey estimates to allocate among states — a methodological improvement that directs more funding to states with documented high lead burdens. [Source: [Policy Innovation Center, 2025 Lead Pipe Funding](https://www.policyinnovation.org/insights/2025-lead-pipe-funding-the-good-the-bad-and-the-big-opportunities-ahead)]

States receiving largest FY2025 increases: Michigan (+$87.5M), Illinois (+$68.7M), Indiana (+$68.1M), New York (+$62M). States with reductions: Florida (-$194.5M, largest reduction). The inventory-based reallotment reflects that the Great Lakes corridor — where industrial history and aging housing stock concentrate lead exposure — has more documented lead service lines than the Sunbelt states whose populations drove earlier survey-based formula allocations. [Source: [FFIS, States See Sweeping Funding Shifts in Grants for Replacing Lead Drinking Water Service Lines](https://ffis.org/issue-brief/states-see-sweeping-funding-shifts-in-grants-for-replacing-lead-drinking-water-service-lines/)]

**The nationwide inventory gap**: The EPA's October 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI) — issued in the final months of the Biden administration — requires water systems to submit complete service line inventories and replace all lead service lines within 10 years, with a compliance date of November 1, 2027. An estimated **9.2 million lead service lines** remain in service nationally. At current replacement rates under the IIJA program, the gap between $15 billion authorized and the true cost of replacing all 9.2 million lines (estimated at $45-90 billion by various analyses) is enormous. [Source: [EPA, Service-Line Inventory and Replacement Requirements, LCRI Fact Sheet](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-10/final_lcri_fact-sheet_service-line-inventory.pdf)]

**Trump administration and the LCRI**: The EPA under the Trump administration initially requested a 60-day pause on the LCRI in court, allowing new leadership time to review. Congressional Republicans introduced a resolution under the Congressional Review Act to repeal the rule. As of May 2025, grassroots pressure defeated the CRA repeal attempt. As of August 2025, the EPA announced it would defend the Biden-era LCRI against industry lawsuits — a significant policy commitment, though one that could be reversed. [Source: [Inside Climate News, An EPA Rule Will Reduce Lead in Drinking Water](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/13022025/epa-rule-to-reduce-drinking-water-lead-potential-reversal/)]

### Flint, Michigan — Milestone and Caution

In July 2025, the City of Flint and the NRDC/Concerned Pastors Settlement Agreement confirmed the replacement of all lead service lines covered by the agreement — approximately 11,000 lines in total — a historic milestone reached 11 years after the water crisis began in 2014. [Source: [NRDC, Flint Finishes Lead Pipe Replacement](https://www.nrdc.org/press-releases/flint-finishes-lead-pipe-replacement-historic-milestone)]

Current water quality data through December 2025: 90th percentile lead level of 6 parts per billion, within both the federal 15 ppb action level and Michigan's stricter 12 ppb standard (effective 2025). [Source: [Michigan Public Radio, Latest tests show lead levels in Flint's drinking water remain within compliance](https://www.michiganpublic.org/health/2026-02-06/latest-tests-show-lead-levels-in-flints-drinking-water-remain-within-compliance)]

The Flint completion is not cause for systemic optimism — it required a decade of crisis visibility, sustained legal pressure, state and federal intervention, and $600+ million in settlement and remediation funding for a city of 95,000 people. There are thousands of communities nationwide with similar infrastructure profiles and no equivalent political salience. The democratic lesson: environmental justice outcomes depend on the ability to generate sustained political pressure, which itself depends on resources that affected communities frequently lack.

### Jackson, Mississippi — Ongoing Crisis

Jackson's water system crisis — inadequate treatment, distribution failures, and a history of boil-water advisories serving a majority-Black city that the state legislature had systematically underfunded — attracted EPA intervention in 2022. As of 2024-2026, a third-party manager (Jacobs Engineering, on a 10-year contract) is operating the water treatment plants. Approximately $600 million in federal funding has been directed to the system; the mayor has stated the full need is $2 billion. [Source: [Jacobs, Restoring Trust, One Drop at a Time](https://www.jacobs.com/projects/restoring-trust-one-drop-time-jackson-mississippis-water-turnaround)]

A governance dispute continues: In February 2026, the Mississippi House passed a bill to transfer control of Jackson's water and sewer systems to an authority separate from city government — a move Jackson advocates characterize as state takeover of a majority-Black city's infrastructure. [Source: [Mississippi Today, House passes Jackson water authority bill](https://mississippitoday.org/2026/02/11/jackson-water-authority-bill-with-more-say-from-city-leaders/)]

Jackson's situation is a case study in the compounding structure of infrastructure injustice: historical underinvestment + fiscal austerity + state unwillingness to fund majority-Black municipal needs + emergency federal intervention that bypasses city government = a pattern that simultaneously addresses the immediate crisis and further erodes democratic self-governance.

## 12d: Transit and Rail

### FTA Capital Investment Grants — Demand vs. Supply

The Federal Transit Administration's Capital Investment Grants (CIG) program funds major transit capital projects: heavy rail, commuter rail, light rail, streetcar, and bus rapid transit. For FY2025, Congress appropriated $3.805 billion for CIG.

The gap between supply and demand: communities have requested more than **$45.1 billion** in CIG funding for 66 projects in 24 states for FY2024 and subsequent years. The $3.8 billion appropriation covers approximately 8% of the stated need. [Source: [GAO, Capital Investment Grants Program: DOT Should Tailor Technical Assistance](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107672)]

This $41+ billion annual gap is structural, not cyclical. It reflects both the underinvestment accumulation across decades and the genuine pent-up demand that IIJA-era planning generated. Cities that completed preliminary engineering and environmental review expecting competitive CIG awards are now waiting in a multi-year queue that the appropriations level cannot clear.

For FY2026, the appropriations compromise provides $1.7 billion for CIG — a significant cut from FY2025's $3.8 billion, representing the Trump administration's downward pressure on transit spending. Combined IIJA and annual appropriations provide $15.9 billion for total passenger and freight rail in FY2026. [Source: [Rail Passengers Association, Analysis of FY 2026 THUD Rail Funding](https://www.railpassengers.org/happening-now/news/blog/analysis-of-fy-2026-thud-rail-funding-and-provisions/)]

### Amtrak Corridor ID Program

The IIJA authorized a $1.8 billion Corridor Identification and Development (Corridor ID) Program, establishing a structured federal process for planning new and expanded intercity passenger rail corridors. Formally established in May 2022 by the Federal Railroad Administration (FRA), the program identifies corridors, funds initial planning, and creates a pipeline toward capital investment grants.

As of December 2023, 69 corridors were accepted into the program: 7 proposed high-speed rail routes, 34 new conventional rail routes, 13 extensions of existing routes, and 15 upgrades to existing routes. As of July 2024, FRA had obligated over 80% of Step 1 planning grant agreements (each Step 1 grant provides $500,000 for preliminary planning activities). [Source: [FRA, Corridor Identification and Development Program](https://railroads.dot.gov/corridor-ID-program)]

The Corridor ID program creates a planning pipeline but not a construction pipeline — Step 1 planning is years removed from shovels in the ground. The IIJA also authorized a separate long-distance rail study (Amtrak Daily Long-Distance Service Study, completed as a Final Report) examining restoration of daily long-distance service on routes where Amtrak operates less frequently.

**Trump administration and rail**: The FY2026 CIG cut from $3.8 billion to $1.7 billion represents the single most consequential transit funding reduction. The administration's posture toward intercity rail has been skeptical; the Corridor ID program's 69 planning corridors are unlikely to advance to construction grants under current discretionary priorities. Projects with existing full funding grant agreements (signed contracts) are protected; projects still in planning or awaiting New Starts grants are vulnerable.

### Transit as Democratic Infrastructure

The ASCE 2025 Report Card grades transit at **D** — improved from D- in 2021, but still the lowest grade of any infrastructure category alongside stormwater. The ASCE estimates a $176 billion cumulative transit funding gap through 2029. [Source: [ASCE, ASCE Report Card Gives U.S. Infrastructure Highest-Ever C Grade](https://www.asce.org/publications-and-news/civil-engineering-source/society-news/article/2025/03/25/asce-report-card-gives-us-infrastructure-highest-ever-c-grade/)]

Transit failure is democratic failure in specific ways: transit access determines whether people without cars can reach jobs, medical appointments, polling places, and civic institutions. Car-free populations skew heavily toward lower-income households, elderly residents, people with disabilities, and urban communities of color. When transit service degrades, these populations face a forced choice between paying unaffordable car ownership costs or accepting immobility. Immobility is not a private inconvenience — it is a form of civic exclusion.

## 12e: Maintenance Gap

### The Backlog by Sector

The accumulated deferred maintenance liability across American infrastructure is enormous and growing, despite IIJA investment:

**Roads and bridges**: State and local governments face an estimated **$105 billion** in deferred maintenance for roads and bridges as of 2023, with a combined annual shortfall of at least $8.6 billion. [Source: [Pew Charitable Trusts, State and Local Governments Face $105 Billion in Deferred Maintenance](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/issue-briefs/2025/05/state-and-local-governments-face-105-billion-in-deferred-maintenance-for-roads-and-bridges)]

**Federal real property**: The Department of Defense and civilian agencies' combined building deferred maintenance and repair backlog more than doubled from $171 billion (FY2017) to $370 billion (FY2024). The GSA's own deferred maintenance backlog exceeds $17 billion. GAO added federal building condition as a high-risk area in 2025. [Source: [GAO, Federal Real Property: Disposing of Unneeded Facilities Could Help Reduce Maintenance Backlog](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-108400)]

**National Park Service**: As of end of FY2024, an estimated $22.986 billion in deferred maintenance exists across NPS roads, buildings, utility systems, and structures. [Source: [NPS, By the Numbers — Infrastructure](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/infrastructure/deferred-maintenance.htm)]

**Overall system estimate**: Research estimates total accumulated deferred maintenance cost across U.S. public infrastructure at approximately **$1 trillion**. [Source: [Transportation for America, The country's civil engineers agree: $1.5 trillion didn't produce good infrastructure](https://t4america.org/2025/03/25/the-countrys-civil-engineers-agree-1-5-trillion-didnt-produce-good-infrastructure/)]

### Why IIJA Did Not Solve the Maintenance Problem

The IIJA was primarily structured as a capital investment bill — new projects, capacity expansion, modernization. The maintenance backlog is structurally different: it is ongoing, unglamorous, and lacks the political visibility of ribbon-cutting. Several IIJA programs (Bridge Formula Program, PROTECT for resilience) directed funding toward maintenance categories, but the overall structure prioritized new capability over sustaining existing capability.

Transportation for America's assessment of the IIJA at four years: civil engineers' own 2025 Report Card demonstrates that the $1.5 trillion invested since 2021 (IIJA plus prior authorizations plus state/local spending) produced the first overall grade of C in 27 years — progress, but insufficient to address what decades of underinvestment created. The grading methodology means that sustaining a C requires continued investment; withdrawal would resume grade deterioration quickly. [Source: [Transportation for America, The country's civil engineers agree](https://t4america.org/2025/03/25/the-countrys-civil-engineers-agree-1-5-trillion-didnt-produce-good-infrastructure/)]

The Democratic Renewal Proposal's 12e reform — a maintenance-first federal funding structure — addresses a structural bias in how federal infrastructure money flows. Capital grants are politically easier to appropriate (they create new things) and easier to account for (you can measure what was built). Maintenance grants for existing infrastructure are harder to narrate and harder to evaluate, so they consistently lose political competition to capital projects. Reforming the federal aid structure to require maintenance adequacy before capital expansion would correct this bias.

## International Benchmarks

### South Korea: Universal Broadband as Utility

South Korea achieved near-universal high-speed broadband decades before the United States began BEAD. The mechanism: treating telecommunications infrastructure as regulated utility infrastructure, with government mandate for national coverage and regulated access pricing. South Korea's broadband penetration at gigabit speeds as of 2024 exceeds 95% of households at prices well below U.S. averages. The policy instrument — utility regulation with universal service requirement — is precisely what Domain 12a proposes.

### Germany: Maintenance-First Approach Under Stress

Germany's Autobahn network — long considered the global standard for road quality — has deteriorated significantly due to deferred maintenance after the post-reunification investment boom. The German experience demonstrates that even strong infrastructure systems face the maintenance cliff if operating funding does not keep pace with capital depreciation. The policy lesson for the U.S.: IIJA-era capital investment must be followed by adequate operating and maintenance funding or the investment depreciates faster than it was built.

### Netherlands: Water Infrastructure as Democratic Governance

The Netherlands manages water infrastructure through democratically accountable water boards (waterschappen) — the oldest democratic institutions in the Netherlands, predating the national government. Water boards levy their own taxes, conduct their own elections, and are accountable specifically for water management outcomes. The structural insight: separating water infrastructure governance from general municipal politics, while maintaining democratic accountability, produces more consistent long-term investment. This model is relevant to the Domain 12c proposal for federal-state water infrastructure partnerships with enforceable accountability standards.

### Japan: Seismic Resilience Investment as Grid Model

Japan's post-Fukushima grid reformation included massive investment in regional interconnection and distributed energy resources to reduce dependence on any single generation source. The resulting grid is more resilient to both seismic events and extreme weather. Texas's deliberate ERCOT isolation — rejecting the interstate interconnection model — is the inverse of this approach. The democratic argument: the public bears the mortality risk of isolated grids; the public should have democratic authority over interconnection decisions, not just market authority over electricity purchases.

## Democratic Harm Synthesis

Infrastructure failure is not politically neutral. The data across all five sub-domains shows consistent patterns:

**Geographic concentration in excluded communities**: Lead service lines are concentrated in cities with older housing stock and lower-income populations — Detroit, Flint, Newark, Jackson. Transit failures are worst in cities where car-free populations are highest. Broadband gaps are deepest in rural areas and tribal nations. The communities least politically powerful receive the worst infrastructure.

**Compounding exclusion**: Digital exclusion compounds economic exclusion (remote work access, online services), civic exclusion (e-government services, online participation), and educational exclusion (homework gap). The 5 million households who disconnected after ACP expiration lost access across all these dimensions simultaneously.

**Mortality as democratic outcome**: The 246 deaths in Texas from Winter Storm Uri are not abstractions — they are the measurable mortal consequence of a grid governance model that externalizes reliability costs onto the public while privatizing reliability benefits. The ASCE estimates that inadequate infrastructure costs the average American household $3,300 per year in lost productivity, vehicle damage, water contamination costs, and transit delays.

**Fiscal extraction model**: Many infrastructure-poor communities are simultaneously subject to high user fees — high water rates in systems with deferred maintenance, high broadband prices in low-competition markets, high auto-dependency costs in transit deserts. The communities bearing the worst infrastructure also often pay the most as a percentage of income for what infrastructure they do access. This is a form of regressive infrastructure taxation with no democratic authorization.

**The maintenance gap as slow violence**: Deferred maintenance does not produce visible crises in the short term — it produces invisible degradation that eventually becomes catastrophic failure. Bridge collapses, water main breaks, and blackouts are the visible endpoints of years of invisible neglect. The political economy of maintenance underfunding is precisely that voters cannot see what has not yet failed; politicians therefore underfund maintenance relative to capital (which has ribbon cuttings) and relative to what the physical system requires.

## Actionable Intelligence for 2026

The IIJA cliff at September 30, 2026 is 5.5 months away. The following actions are time-sensitive:

**For state DOTs and transportation agencies (now through September)**:
- Obligate as much IIJA formula funding as possible before September 30. Obligated funds survive authorization lapse. Unobligated formula allocations are vulnerable to a reauthorization that reduces baseline levels.
- Prioritize executing grant agreements on competitive discretionary grants already awarded. Convert awards to obligations.
- Prepare detailed documentation of mid-project status for every federally funded project — the case for protecting ongoing project funding in reauthorization negotiations depends on visible project portfolios.

**For advocates and civic organizations**:
- The reauthorization fight will occur in a compressed window. No bill has been introduced as of April 2026 with 5.5 months until expiration. Surface transportation reauthorization typically takes 2+ years. The likely outcome is a continuing resolution or multi-month extension — not a new comprehensive bill.
- The $2.3 billion in IIJA rescissions and the NEVI litigation establish that obligated-but-unspent funds are legally protected against executive action but politically vulnerable to legislative rescission. Protecting the remaining $293 billion in allocated-but-unobligated funds requires legislative action before September.
- ACP successor legislation has been introduced in multiple Congresses without passing. The argument for a successor program is now backed by 18 months of post-ACP data: 5 million households offline, documented economic harm, and the irony of BEAD funding infrastructure that low-income households cannot afford to use.

**For water justice advocates**:
- The EPA's commitment to defend the LCRI is not legally binding and can be reversed. The October 2024 LCRI requires community water systems to complete full service line inventories by November 1, 2027. Monitoring compliance with the inventory requirement — and publishing which water systems have not filed — is an accountability mechanism available to advocacy organizations right now.
- FY2025 was the fourth of five annual IIJA lead service line funding tranches. FY2026 will be the final tranche. After FY2026, no automatic replacement funding mechanism exists unless Congress reauthorizes a successor program. States that have moved slowly on LSLR are at risk of having unspent FY2022-2025 allocations reallocated to faster-moving states — or simply not deploying funds before the program ends.

**For grid and energy advocates**:
- FERC Orders 1920 and 2023 are in effect and transmission providers are obligated to comply. Monitoring compliance filings at regional transmission organizations (RTOs) is publicly available through FERC's docket system. Non-compliance or slow compliance should be documented and reported.
- The Texas natural gas supply chain weatherization gap identified by the State Auditor in August 2025 is an unaddressed regulatory failure. The next Uri-scale event will test whether Railroad Commission enforcement has meaningfully improved; advocates should be prepared to document the gap between regulatory requirements and actual generator compliance.

**The broader political frame**:
The IIJA cliff is an opportunity as well as a risk. Reauthorization forces a congressional vote on infrastructure investment levels — the first such vote since 2021. The political conditions of 2026 (election year, budget pressure, fragmented coalition) are not favorable to an ambitious successor bill, but they create a pressure point at which organized state and local advocacy can extract commitments. The states with the most to lose from reversion to pre-IIJA baselines — and the most ongoing projects at risk — are not exclusively blue states. Infrastructure is one of the few policy domains where Republican-represented rural states and suburban counties have concrete material stakes in continued federal investment.

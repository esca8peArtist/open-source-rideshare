## Section 1: The US in International Context — A Low-Tax, High-Inequality Outlier

### Total Tax Revenue as a Share of GDP

The United States is among the lowest-taxed wealthy nations in the OECD. The claim that the US is overtaxed relative to peer countries is empirically false.

| Country | Tax-to-GDP Ratio (2024) |
|---|---|
| Denmark | 45.2% |
| France | 43.5% |
| Austria | 43.4% |
| Germany | ~39% |
| United Kingdom | ~35% |
| Canada | ~34% |
| OECD average | 34.1% |
| Australia | ~30% |
| United States | 25.6% |
| Mexico | 18.3% |

The US at 25.6% of GDP sits approximately 8.5 percentage points below the OECD average — a gap worth roughly $2.3 trillion annually at current GDP levels. Only six OECD countries collect less tax as a share of GDP than the US: Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ireland, Mexico, and Türkiye. Among G7 nations alone, the US has the lowest tax-to-GDP ratio.

The US tax-to-GDP ratio has remained near 25–26% for decades, while peer nations at 33–45% fund universal healthcare, subsidized childcare, paid parental leave, active labor market programs, robust pension systems, and free or heavily subsidized public higher education. When out-of-pocket healthcare costs, higher education tuition, and other "private" expenditures for social goods are added to the US tax burden, the total household obligation of a median US family is comparable to or exceeds that of equivalent families in most peer nations — with inferior outcomes.

Sources: [OECD Revenue Statistics 2024 — United States](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/topics/policy-sub-issues/global-tax-revenues/revenue-statistics-united-states.pdf); [OECD Revenue Statistics 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025_07ca0a8e.html); [OECD: Average Tax Revenues Remain Steady as Spending Pressures Grow (2024)](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2024/11/average-tax-revenues-in-the-oecd-remain-steady-as-spending-pressures-grow.html); [Tax Policy Center: How Do US Taxes Compare Internationally?](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-do-us-taxes-compare-internationally)

### Corporate Tax Revenue: A Wider Divergence

The corporate tax comparison is even more stark than the personal income comparison, and it has steepened over time.

| Metric | United States | OECD Average (ex-US) |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate income tax revenue as % of GDP (2021) | 1.6% | 3.2% |
| Statutory corporate rate (post-TCJA 2017) | 21% | ~23% weighted avg |
| OECD average effective tax rate (2024) | ~9–12% for large firms | ~20–22% |

Corporate income tax revenue as a share of US GDP has declined from approximately 4% in the 1960s to 1.6% in 2021 — a halving in relative terms over sixty years. The Tax Policy Center confirms the US raises less corporate tax revenue as a share of GDP than any other G7 country and almost all other OECD members. A 2024 Government Accountability Office analysis found that profitable large US corporations paid an average effective federal tax rate of approximately 9% — less than half the 21% statutory rate. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy found that 55 of the largest US corporations paid zero federal income tax on approximately $40 billion in combined profits in at least one recent year.

**The OECD global minimum tax (Pillar Two)**: The OECD Pillar Two framework — a 15% global minimum corporate tax on multinationals with revenues above €750 million — began taking effect in 2024. The US has not implemented the GloBE rules into domestic law. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, signed July 4, 2025) replaced GILTI with "net CFC tested income" (NCTI) at an effective rate of approximately 12.6% — below the Pillar Two 15% floor. In January 2026, the OECD agreed to a "side-by-side" arrangement effectively exempting US-headquartered multinationals from foreign minimum top-up taxes, in exchange for the US removing Section 899 retaliatory measures. The result: US companies remain below-compliant with the global minimum while other countries' firms operating in the US face effective minimum taxation.

Sources: [Tax Policy Center: How Do US Corporate Income Tax Rates Compare?](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-do-us-corporate-income-tax-rates-and-revenues-compare-other-countries); [OECD Corporate Tax Statistics 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/corporate-tax-statistics-2025_6a945941-en.html); [CBPP: Lessons from the 2017 Tax Law](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/lessons-from-the-2017-tax-law-for-the-future-of-us-corporate-taxation); [Tax Policy Center: OECD Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 Reforms](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/what-are-oecd-pillar-1-and-pillar-2-international-taxation-reforms); [FACT Coalition: OECD Side-by-Side Allowing Tax Abuse](https://thefactcoalition.org/oecd-side-by-side-agreement/)

### The Compliance Burden as a Regressive Tax

One underappreciated dimension of US tax policy: the compliance burden itself functions as a regressive impost. The US tax code extends to 6,871 pages plus approximately 70,000 pages of regulations, rulings, and guidance. Americans spend an estimated 6.5 billion hours and $400 billion per year on tax compliance (National Taxpayers Union Foundation, 2024) — more than the GDP of 80% of the world's economies. This expenditure produces no goods, no services, and no public benefit.

The burden is regressive in structure: wealthy individuals and large corporations hire specialists to exploit complexity and reduce their effective tax rates; middle-income taxpayers bear the highest compliance costs relative to income; low-income filers entitled to refundable credits (EITC, CTC) face the most complex filing requirements and among the highest audit rates relative to the general population.

**The pre-populated return comparison**: Sweden's Skatteverket pre-fills most returns using employer and bank data — 74% of Swedes file by SMS or app, average completion time: 3 minutes. Estonia achieves 98% digital filing averaging 3–5 minutes. Denmark's pre-filled returns have a 99% acceptance rate. The IRS's 2024 Direct File pilot — 140,000 taxpayers in 12 states — demonstrated that the technical capability exists for the US to replicate this, expanding to 296,531 returns in 25 states in 2025 before the Trump administration terminated the program in November 2025, citing "low participation and relatively high costs" ($41 million, or $138 per return). The real barrier is the tax preparation industry's lobbying — Intuit and H&R Block spent approximately $39 million lobbying against IRS free filing between 2006 and 2023. Democrats introduced legislation in February 2026 to restore Direct File; no vote has been scheduled.

Sources: [NTUF: Americans Spend 6.5 Billion Hours on Federal Taxes](https://www.ntu.org/foundation/detail/americans-spend-6-5-billion-hours-on-federal-taxes); [Federal News Network: IRS Direct File Will Not Be Available in 2026](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/it-modernization/2025/11/irs-direct-file-will-not-be-available-in-2026-agency-tells-states/); [CBPP: Trump Plan to End Direct File Is a Mistake](https://www.cbpp.org/blog/trump-plan-to-end-free-direct-file-program-and-rely-on-for-profit-tax-preparers-is-a-mistake)

## Section 2: The Saez/Zucman Billionaire Rate Finding — The Evidence and the Dispute

### The Core Finding

Economists Emmanuel Saez (UC Berkeley) and Gabriel Zucman (UC Berkeley) published an analysis showing that in 2018 — the first full year after TCJA — the 400 wealthiest American families paid a combined effective all-in tax rate of approximately 23%, including federal income taxes, state/local taxes, and corporate taxes attributable to their ownership. The bottom half of US households paid approximately 24.2% — a thin margin higher. This was the first time in the data (going back to 1960) that the ultra-wealthy paid a lower effective rate than the working class.

The longer trend line: the effective rate on the top 400 families fell from approximately 56% in 1960, to 47% in 1980, to 30% in 2017, to 23% in 2018. The post-TCJA inflection reflects the corporate rate cut from 35% to 21% and its concentrated benefit to wealthy equity owners.

**The ProPublica IRS data confirmation**: ProPublica's June 2021 analysis using leaked IRS data for the 25 wealthiest Americans calculated a "true tax rate" — taxes paid as a fraction of wealth increase — of 3.4% over 2014–2018 ($13.6 billion in taxes on $401 billion in wealth growth). Elon Musk: 3.27% true tax rate. Jeff Bezos: 0.98% in two years. Carl Icahn: paid zero federal income tax in 2016 and 2017 despite reporting $544 million in adjusted gross income — using loans of approximately $1.2 billion secured against his appreciated assets (the buy-borrow-die strategy in direct form).

**Wealth concentration as the underlying structural problem**: Federal Reserve distributional data as of Q1 2024 shows the top 1% hold 30.9% of total household wealth (up from 23% in 1989), while the bottom 50% hold 2.5% (down from 3.4%). The Piketty/Saez/Zucman framework documents that top 1% income share rose from 10% in 1979 to 17.6% in 2019 (excluding capital gains) and to 21.1% including capital gains. Gilens and Page's 2014 study found that policy outcomes in the US correlate strongly with economic elite preferences and show no statistically significant correlation with median voter preferences — establishing the political economy mechanism by which fiscal policy tracks concentrated wealth.

Sources: [Saez/Zucman: The Triumph of Injustice (2019 BPEA)](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/SaezZucman2019BPEA.pdf); [ProPublica: The Secret IRS Files](https://www.propublica.org/article/the-secret-irs-files-trove-of-never-before-seen-records-reveal-how-the-wealthiest-avoid-income-tax); [Federal Reserve: Distribution of Household Wealth](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/dataviz/dfa/distribute/chart/); [Saez: Striking It Richer (2022 update)](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-UStopincomes-2022.pdf)

### The Methodological Dispute

The Saez/Zucman finding is contested on specific grounds worth stating plainly:

**The EITC/CTC omission**: Harvard economist Jason Furman and others note that Saez/Zucman's analysis of lower-income households does not include refundable tax credits — the EITC and Child Tax Credit — which represent real income and real tax relief for lower-income working families. Including these would reduce the effective rate of lower-income households and widen the gap in their analysis. This is a genuine methodological point, though it strengthens rather than weakens the finding that the wealthy pay less.

**The Auten/Splinter counter-analysis**: Gerald Auten (Treasury) and David Splinter (JCT) published a 2024 paper arguing that properly accounting for government transfers and adjusting for changing household composition, US income inequality has not risen as dramatically as Piketty, Saez, and Zucman find. Their analysis uses different assumptions about how to attribute undistributed corporate profits and government transfers. The debate is unresolved in the academic literature; most analysts accept that the directional PSZ findings are correct even if precise magnitudes are contested.

**The corporate tax incidence issue**: The 23% effective rate includes corporate income taxes attributed to wealthy families' ownership stakes. This requires assumptions about who bears the corporate tax (see Section 5). Under different incidence assumptions, the calculation changes — but the directional result (effective rates fall sharply at the top) is robust across incidence assumptions.

**Bottom line**: The specific numbers are contested; the directional finding is not. No serious analyst disputes that the tax system is less progressive in practice than its nominal rate structure suggests, that unrealized gains constitute the dominant form of elite wealth accumulation and go untaxed indefinitely, or that the buy-borrow-die strategy eliminates tax on a substantial fraction of billionaire fortunes.

Sources: [Tax Policy Center: Are Billionaire Taxes Falling?](https://taxpolicycenter.org/taxvox/are-billionaire-taxes-falling-depends-who-you-think-pays-corporate-taxes); [Saez/Zucman 2024 IFS Lecture](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/SZ2024IFS.pdf); [Auten/Splinter Reply to PSZ (2024)](https://www.davidsplinter.com/AutenSplinter-2024-ReplyToPSZ.pdf); [Equitable Growth: New Research Doesn't Overturn Consensus on Rising Inequality](https://equitablegrowth.org/new-research-doesnt-overturn-consensus-on-rising-u-s-income-inequality/)

## Section 3: The Buy-Borrow-Die Strategy and Mark-to-Market Reform

*For the full mechanics of the buy-borrow-die strategy, see `domain-05-fiscal-reform.md`, Section 1.2. Summary here for cross-reference.*

The buy-borrow-die mechanism eliminates capital gains tax on a large fraction of ultra-high-net-worth wealth accumulation through three code provisions: (1) indefinite deferral of unrealized appreciation; (2) ability to borrow against appreciated assets with no tax event; (3) stepped-up basis at death erasing the embedded gain permanently. The stepped-up basis costs approximately $40–50 billion per year in foregone federal revenue (Congressional Research Service estimate). Over 75% of this benefit accrues to the largest estates.

### Mark-to-Market and Billionaire Minimum Tax Options

**Mark-to-market taxation**: Treat unrealized appreciation in publicly traded securities as taxable income in the year it accrues, as is already the treatment for futures contracts, certain dealer inventory, and foreign currency positions. For publicly traded assets — which constitute the majority of ultra-high-net-worth portfolios by value — mark-to-market is administratively trivial. Prices are public, tracked daily, and already reported to the IRS on 1099-B forms. No new reporting infrastructure is required.

The harder case — closely held businesses, private equity, real estate — requires valuation. Options: (1) carryover of accruals with interest until sale or death; (2) apply mark-to-market only to publicly traded holdings and liquid assets above a threshold; (3) a deemed-realization trigger at death with an installment payment option for illiquid assets.

**The Wyden/Cohen/Beyer Billionaires Income Tax Act (2025, 119th Congress)**: Senate Finance Committee Ranking Member Ron Wyden and House Members Steve Cohen and Don Beyer reintroduced the Billionaires Income Tax Act in September 2025 — the first time this bill has been a bicameral proposal. It applies a mark-to-market tax on total income (including unrealized gains) for taxpayers with more than $100 million in annual income or more than $1 billion in assets for three consecutive years. The bill would affect fewer than 1,000 taxpayers and is estimated to raise more than $500 billion.

**The Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act of 2026 (H.R.8085)**: Led by Rep. Pramila Jayapal in the House (39 Democratic co-sponsors) and Sen. Chris Van Hollen and Sen. Elizabeth Warren in the Senate (10 Democratic co-sponsors). Imposes a 2% annual wealth tax on net worth above $50 million and an additional 1% on billionaire wealth. Revenue estimate: $200–300 billion annually (Saez/Zucman methodology).

**Constitutional challenge**: The Supreme Court's 2024 decision in *Moore v. United States* upheld the TCJA's mandatory repatriation tax on previously accumulated foreign earnings but preserved the realization requirement as a constitutional matter for most income taxes. The long-term constitutional resolution for mark-to-market remains contested. A constitutional amendment clarifying Congress's power to tax accretion of wealth without realization and without apportionment would permanently resolve the question — a long-term objective, not a near-term strategy.

Sources: [CRS: Mark-to-Market Taxation of Capital Gains](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11957); [Senate Finance Committee: Wyden/Cohen/Beyer Billionaires Income Tax Act (Sept. 2025)](https://www.finance.senate.gov/ranking-members-news/wyden-cohen-beyer-introduce-the-billionaires-income-tax-act); [Congress.gov: H.R.8085 Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act of 2026](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8085/text); [ITEP: Biden Billionaires' Minimum Income Tax](https://itep.org/president-bidens-proposed-billionaires-minimum-income-tax-would-ensure-the-wealthiest-pay-a-reasonable-amount-of-income-tax/)

## Section 4: The Wealth Tax Debate — European Evidence Assessed

*For the full international wealth tax survey, see `domain-05-fiscal-reform.md`, Section 3.3. This section focuses on the design lessons — what a well-structured US proposal must do differently.*

### The Revenue Case

The Saez/Zucman wealth tax proposal — 2% on net wealth above $50 million, 3% above $1 billion — was originally estimated at $200–220 billion annually; their 2021 updated estimate raised this to $300 billion annually for the Warren iteration. Competing estimates from Smith, Zidar, and Zwick range to $117 billion — driven by different assumptions about valuation difficulty and avoidance behavior. Zucman's 2024 advocacy before the G20 for a coordinated 2% tax on approximately 3,000 billionaires globally projected $200–250 billion annually worldwide, with the US share estimated at approximately $100 billion.

A billionaire who earns zero taxable income in a year by living off borrowed money secured against a $10 billion equity stake is completely outside the income tax base. A wealth tax addresses this gap structurally.

### The European Counter-Evidence and Why It Doesn't Transfer

**France's ISF** (*Impôt de Solidarité sur la Fortune*, 1988–2017): Applied to net wealth above approximately €1.3 million at rates of 0.5–1.5%. By 2015, approximately 10,000 millionaires were leaving France annually. The tax was generating approximately €4 billion annually but losing substantial income, corporate, and consumption tax revenue from departing taxpayers. Repealed in 2017 by Macron.

**Norway's 2022 experience**: Norway raised its wealth tax rate from 0.85% to 1.1% in 2022 (applied starting at approximately $150,000 in net worth). In the subsequent 12 months, approximately 30 large taxpayers — representing an estimated $54 billion in combined net worth — emigrated, primarily to Switzerland. The associated revenue loss was estimated at $594 million — exceeding the incremental revenue from the rate increase. Norway subsequently closed the exit loophole in 2024, requiring emigrants to pay an immediate exit tax on unrealized gains. Norway's 2025 wealth tax collections: 34 billion kroner, up from 27 billion in 2022, because the broad base grows faster than high-end emigration depletes it.

**Why the US context differs structurally**:
- **Worldwide taxation**: US citizens and permanent residents pay US tax on worldwide income regardless of residency — legally unique among major economies. Moving to Switzerland does not exempt a US citizen from US taxes.
- **Exit tax (Section 877A)**: Covered expatriates — those renouncing citizenship or long-term residency with net worth above $2 million — are subject to a mark-to-market deemed-disposition tax at the time of expatriation. This provides a foundation for extending deemed disposition to wealth tax avoidance.
- **Threshold design**: The Saez/Zucman $50 million threshold captures a few thousand families — not millions of professionals and small business owners. Norway's 1.1% rate applied starting at approximately $150,000, capturing a fundamentally different population.
- **FATCA infrastructure**: The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act already requires foreign financial institutions to report US account holders — an enforcement infrastructure Europe lacked.

The honest assessment: European evidence proves that bad designs fail. High thresholds, exit taxes on unrealized gains at expatriation, coordination with FATCA/CRS reporting, and anti-avoidance rules for entity structuring are all achievable features of a US wealth tax design. None of this is insurmountable.

Sources: [Saez/Zucman: Wealth Tax Revenue Estimates](https://www.warren.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Wealth%20Tax%20Revenue%20Estimates%20by%20Saez%20and%20Zucman%20-%20Feb%2024%2020211.pdf); [Tax Foundation: Wealth Taxes in Europe 2025](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/eu/wealth-taxes-europe/); [ICIJ: Zucman G20 Billionaire Tax Pitch](https://www.icij.org/investigations/pandora-papers/top-economist-pitches-global-billionaire-tax-to-g20-finance-leaders/); [Canadian Affairs: Norway's Wealth Tax Drove Out the Rich Without Breaking the Bank](https://www.canadianaffairs.news/2025/10/24/norways-wealth-tax-drove-out-the-rich-without-breaking-the-bank/)

## Section 5: Corporate Tax Incidence — Who Bears the Burden?

### The Official Assumptions vs. the Empirical Literature

The question of who ultimately bears the economic burden of the corporate income tax — capital owners or workers — is technically contested and politically consequential. It determines how distributional analyses assign TCJA's benefits and whether the $4,000 wage claim had any theoretical foundation.

**Official agency assumptions**:
- Congressional Budget Office: ~75% on capital (shareholders), ~25% on labor (workers through lower wages)
- Treasury Department: 82% on capital, 18% on labor
- Joint Committee on Taxation: 75% capital, 25% labor

These assumptions derive largely from the closed-economy Harberger (1962) model, where capital cannot relocate internationally.

**The open-economy empirical challenge**: More recent empirical work using state- and country-level variation finds that labor may bear substantially more of the corporate tax burden. In an open economy where capital is internationally mobile, a corporate tax in one jurisdiction reduces investment there, reducing labor demand and wages. Estimates from open-economy models range from 50% to 73%+ of the corporate tax burden falling on labor.

**The resolution**: The debate turns on how mobile capital actually is. For very large multinationals with complex profit-shifting capabilities, capital is highly mobile and labor bears more of the burden. For domestic corporations without offshore options, capital mobility is lower and the traditional CBO assumption holds better. A 50/50 split is probably as defensible as 75/25 for the aggregate corporate sector.

Sources: [CBO: How CBO Projects Corporate Income Tax Revenues (October 2023)](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2023-10/59436-Corporate-Tax-Revenues.pdf); [Tax Policy Center: Who Bears the Burden of Corporate Income Tax?](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/who-bears-burden-corporate-income-tax); [CRS: Overview of the Corporate Income Tax System (2023)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R47519)

---

### The $4,000 Wage Promise vs. the Evidence

The Trump Council of Economic Advisers projected in October 2017 that TCJA's corporate provisions would raise average household wages by a "conservative" $4,000–9,000 annually.

**What happened**: In 2018, US corporations spent approximately $800 billion–$1 trillion on stock buybacks — roughly double the pre-TCJA historical average. Between 2018 and 2022, 280 large US corporations spent a combined $2.7 trillion on buybacks — 98% more than in the comparable pre-TCJA period. On wages: a 2022 study by Joint Committee on Taxation and Federal Reserve researchers found that earnings for workers in the bottom 90% of the within-firm income distribution did not change following TCJA. Wage increases concentrated in the top 10% of earners within firms, and particularly sharply among executives and managers. A 2024 study by Patrick J. Kennedy et al. (JCT) reached the same conclusion.

**The honest middle ground**: TCJA's immediate expensing provision did accelerate business equipment investment in 2018–2019, consistent with standard economic predictions. But the broad-based $4,000 wage gain for working families did not materialize, consistent with the CBO/Treasury assumption that capital, not labor, captures most of the incidence.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) makes the core TCJA individual provisions permanent, adding approximately $4.1 trillion to the deficit over a decade relative to current law, while extending §199A's pass-through deduction (61% of whose benefit accrues to the top 1%) and adopting NCTI at approximately 12.6% effective rate for international income — below the OECD Pillar Two 15% floor. The Tax Policy Center estimates the OBBBA reduces taxes on average by $2,900 in 2026, with nearly 60% of benefits going to the top income quintile.

Sources: [Center for American Progress: TCJA 2 Years Later](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/tcja-2-years-later-corporations-not-workers-big-winners/); [EPI: TCJA Overwhelmingly Benefited the Rich](https://www.epi.org/press/the-tcja-overwhelmingly-benefited-the-rich-and-corporations-while-overlooking-working-families/); [Tax Policy Center: Distributional Effects of 2025 Budget Reconciliation Act](https://taxpolicycenter.org/tax-model-analysis/distributional-effects-tax-provisions-2025-budget-reconciliation-act); [Tax Foundation: One Big Beautiful Bill Tax Changes](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-tax-changes/)

## Section 6: The Carried Interest Loophole — An Unresolved Structural Failure

### What the Loophole Is and What It Costs

Carried interest is the share of profits that hedge fund managers and private equity executives receive as compensation for managing investor capital — typically 20% of investment gains. Under current law, this compensation is taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rate (20% plus 3.8% net investment income tax = 23.8%) rather than as ordinary income (up to 37% plus 3.8% NII = 40.8%). The difference: a fund manager earning $10 million in carried interest pays approximately $1.7 million less in federal taxes than a salaried employee earning the same income.

The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates that closing the loophole would raise $63.1 billion over 10 years — approximately $6 billion annually. While not the largest single revenue item, the carried interest loophole is symbolically significant: it is the clearest case in which compensation paid for work is taxed at the preferential rate reserved for investment income, with no policy justification beyond lobbying power.

### Current Legislative Status

Three separate bills in the 119th Congress (2025-2026) target carried interest:

- **Carried Interest Fairness Act of 2025** (S.445 / H.R.1091): Introduced in the Senate by Senator Tammy Baldwin and colleagues; identical House version introduced by Reps. Gluesenkamp Perez and Beyer. Would tax carried interest as ordinary income.
- **Ending the Carried Interest Loophole Act**: Introduced by Senators Wyden, Whitehouse, and King on April 16, 2026 — the most recent introduction, filed just one day before this document's preparation date.

**The Trump paradox on carried interest**: On February 6, 2025, President Trump met with Republican leadership and included elimination of carried interest's preferential treatment in his stated legislative priorities. This represented a significant and unexpected bipartisan opening. Despite this, the final One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed July 4, 2025 made no changes to carried interest — the private equity and hedge fund industry's lobbying power overcame the president's stated preference. This outcome is a textbook illustration of the Gilens/Page concentrated-interest capture mechanism operating in real time.

Sources: [Congress.gov: S.445 Carried Interest Fairness Act of 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/445); [Senate Finance Committee: Wyden/Whitehouse/King Carried Interest Bill (April 2026)](https://www.finance.senate.gov/ranking-members-news/wyden-whitehouse-king-lead-introduction-of-bill-closing-carried-interest-tax-loophole); [Fortune: GOP Tax Bill Preserves Carried Interest Loophole](https://fortune.com/2025/05/23/president-trump-tax-bill-carried-interest-private-equity-hedge-funds-venture-capital-house-senate/); [CEPR: Private Equity Wins in Trump's Big Beautiful Bill](https://cepr.net/publications/private-equity-wins/)

## Section 7: Tax Expenditures — The Hidden Budget

### Scope and Scale

Tax expenditures — revenue forgone through deductions, exclusions, credits, and preferential rates — are economically equivalent to direct spending but escape annual appropriations scrutiny almost entirely. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates federal tax expenditures for fiscal year 2024 in the range of $1.8 trillion annually — exceeding combined domestic discretionary spending and approaching the entire defense budget ($860 billion discretionary).

The largest categories (approximate FY2024):

| Category | Approximate Annual Cost |
|---|---|
| Employer-provided health insurance exclusion | $370B |
| Retirement savings (401k, IRA, pensions, Keogh) | $397B |
| Preferential capital gains and dividend rates | $185B |
| Child Tax Credit (partially refundable) | $120B+ |
| Earned Income Tax Credit (refundable) | $64B |
| Section 199A pass-through deduction | $58B |
| Mortgage interest deduction | $25B |
| Charitable contribution deduction | $50–60B |

The retirement savings figure alone ($397B) exceeds the entire SNAP food assistance program and rivals Medicaid federal expenditure. It is the single largest tax expenditure in the federal budget.

Sources: [JCT: Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures 2024 (JCX-48-24)](https://www.jct.gov/publications/2024/jcx-48-24/); [Tax Policy Center: What Are the Largest Tax Expenditures?](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/what-are-largest-tax-expenditures)

---

### Distributional Reality: Who Gets What

**Retirement savings ($397B)**: CBO analysis found that 77% of individuals in the highest income quintile benefit from retirement-related tax expenditures, compared to only 19% of those in the bottom quintile. Of $201.6 billion in retirement tax expenditures analyzed, the top quintile received 63% of the benefit; the bottom quintile received 1%. The mechanism is structural: higher earners are more likely to have employers offering plans, more likely to reach contribution limits, and face higher marginal rates that increase the deduction's value.

**> Evidence callout**: The 401k/IRA tax expenditure is the single largest item in the federal tax code at $397B/year. Its benefits are concentrated at the top: the wealthiest fifth of earners receives 63% of the subsidy. The bottom fifth receives 1%. This is not a middle-class program — it is a savings subsidy for those already able to save substantially, structured in a way that amplifies advantage for those with higher marginal rates and more disposable income.

**Mortgage interest deduction ($25B)**: Taxpayers earning above $200,000 in AGI represent 49% of all claimants but capture approximately 71% of total benefit. Most lower-income households take the standard deduction and receive no benefit from the mortgage deduction. Research consistently finds little effect on homeownership rates from the deduction — its primary effect is to raise house prices and purchase amounts for buyers who would buy regardless.

**Section 199A pass-through deduction ($58B)**: The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates that 61% of §199A's benefit in 2024 accrues to the top 1% of income earners. The OBBBA made §199A permanent; CBO estimates that this extension alone reduces federal revenues by approximately $700 billion over ten years. Penn Wharton Budget Model's analysis found that eliminating the §199A deduction for high-income earners while preserving it for genuinely small businesses would reduce its revenue cost by approximately $350 billion over a decade.

**SALT deduction**: The OBBBA raises the SALT cap from $10,000 to $40,000 for households with income below $500,000, primarily benefiting households earning $200,000–$500,000 in high-tax states. This is presented as middle-class relief; the primary beneficiaries are upper-income professional households, not median earners.

**The EITC and CTC as contrast**: The Earned Income Tax Credit ($64B) and Child Tax Credit are strongly progressive — cash transfers to working and lower-middle-income families. In 2024, the EITC lifted approximately 4.4 million people above the poverty line, including 2.3 million children. Together, the EITC and CTC lifted approximately 6.4 million people out of poverty in 2023. This is a genuinely different category of tax expenditure: redistributive in design and effect.

Sources: [CBPP: Retirement Tax Incentives Are Ripe for Reform](https://www.cbpp.org/research/retirement-tax-incentives-are-ripe-for-reform); [CRS: Mortgage Interest Deduction (2024)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12789); [Penn Wharton: Eliminating Excess 199A Benefits](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/2025/1/27/eliminating-excess-benefits-from-section-199a-deduction); [CBPP: The Earned Income Tax Credit](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/the-earned-income-tax-credit)

## Section 8: The "Starve the Beast" Theory — Empirical Failure

### What the Theory Claims

The "starve the beast" argument — prominent in conservative fiscal policy since Reagan — holds that cutting taxes reduces government revenue, which creates fiscal pressure that eventually forces Congress to cut spending as deficits become politically untenable. This theory has justified major tax cuts without offsetting spending reductions: the Reagan cuts (1981), the Bush cuts (2001 and 2003), and TCJA (2017).

### What the Evidence Shows

The empirical literature is unambiguous in rejecting the prediction.

William Niskanen's 2006 Cato Institute paper — a critic of the theory from within the libertarian tradition — found the opposite of the predicted relationship. Controlling for the unemployment rate, federal spending from 1981 to 2000 increased by approximately 0.5% of GDP for every 1 percentage point decline in tax revenue as a share of GDP. Tax cuts were followed by higher spending, not lower. Niskanen identified the mechanism: when taxes are cut without reducing spending, the perceived cost of government falls — voters experience the benefit of programs but attribute less of the cost to current taxation, reducing political pressure to cut spending.

Romer and Romer's Berkeley analysis (*Do Tax Cuts Starve the Beast?*, examining tax changes 1945–2007) found "no evidence that tax cuts lead to reductions in government spending."

**The post-TCJA confirmation**: Federal spending as a share of GDP in FY2019 — the last pre-COVID year — was higher than in any year of the Obama administration. The TCJA added approximately $1.5–1.9 trillion to the national debt over its first decade without producing commensurate spending reductions. The OBBBA now layers an additional $5.2 trillion in net revenue reduction over ten years on top of this baseline.

**The long-run US record**: Since 1981, top marginal income tax rates have fallen from 70% to 37%, the corporate rate from 48% to 21%, and capital gains rates from 28% (1986) to 15–20%. Over this same period, federal debt held by the public grew from roughly 25% of GDP to over 95% of GDP. The supply-side prediction — that lower taxes would generate growth sufficient to reduce deficits — has not materialized at the aggregate level.

**The OBBBA as the current test**: The One Big Beautiful Bill Act's tax provisions are projected to reduce revenues by nearly $5.2 trillion over 2025–2034 on a conventional basis (Tax Foundation). If the starve-the-beast theory held, these cuts should produce spending discipline. Instead, the same legislation included significant increases in defense spending and targeted spending on immigration enforcement, with deficit expansion addressed through cuts to Medicaid and SNAP — a pattern of cutting benefits to low-income populations while expanding deficits, not a pattern of overall spending discipline.

Sources: [Niskanen (2006): Limiting Government — The Failure of Starve the Beast (Cato)](https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/serials/files/cato-journal/2006/11/cj26n3-8.pdf); [Berkeley (Romer): Do Tax Cuts Starve the Beast?](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/draft509.pdf); [Tax Foundation: One Big Beautiful Bill Act Tax Policies](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/big-beautiful-bill-senate-gop-tax-plan/)

## Section 9: The Elasticity of Taxable Income and Revenue-Maximizing Rates

### The Technical Framework

Supply-side objections to high marginal income taxes have a technically respectable form in the elasticity of taxable income (ETI). ETI measures how responsive reported taxable income is to changes in marginal rates: if ETI is 0.5, a 1% increase in the net-of-tax rate raises reported income by 0.5%. High ETI means raising rates reduces the tax base substantially; low ETI means raising rates captures most of the mechanical revenue.

Saez's 2001 paper (*Using Elasticities to Derive Optimal Income Tax Rates*, Review of Economic Studies) established the key formula for the revenue-maximizing top rate: t* = 1 / (1 + a*e), where a describes the shape of the top-income distribution and e is the ETI. For typical top-income distribution parameters (a ≈ 2) and ETI = 0.25: t* = 1 / (1 + 2×0.25) = 73%. At ETI = 0.5: t* ≈ 56%.

**The ETI literature**: The comprehensive review by Saez, Slemrod, and Giertz (2012, *Journal of Economic Literature*) found ETI estimates ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, with a central estimate for broad income of approximately 0.25 for higher-income taxpayers. Critically, the authors distinguish between real behavioral responses (working fewer hours, reducing effort) and avoidance responses (income shifting, tax sheltering). Most of the measured ETI at high incomes reflects avoidance behavior — behavior that base-broadening reform can largely eliminate, not fundamental changes in labor supply.

**The policy implication**: An income tax reform that simultaneously broadens the base (eliminates sheltering opportunities) and raises rates can achieve higher revenue with a lower behavioral cost than the ETI literature suggests under current law. Current US top marginal rate: 37%. This places current policy well below the estimated revenue-maximizing rate even under supply-side-friendly ETI assumptions. The supply-side claim that US tax rates are near or above the revenue-maximizing level has no empirical support in the ETI literature.

Sources: [Saez (2001): Using Elasticities to Derive Optimal Income Tax Rates](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/derive.pdf); [Saez, Slemrod, Giertz (2012): ETI — A Critical Review (JEL)](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-slemrod-giertzJEL12.pdf); [NBER Working Paper 17616: Optimal Taxation of Top Labor Incomes](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17616/w17616.pdf)

---

### Historical Evidence: High Rates and Growth

The top US marginal income tax rate was 91% from 1951 to 1963, fell to 70% from 1965 to 1981, was cut to 50% in 1981 (Reagan), and to 28% in 1986 (Tax Reform Act), before rising to 39.6% in 1993. Current top rate: 37%.

Key facts on the 1950s evidence:

- Real GDP growth averaged 4.2% annually in the 1950s, alongside a 91% top marginal rate.
- The top 1% of taxpayers paid approximately 42% of their income in effective federal income taxes in the 1950s — not 91%, because the statutory rate applied only to income above approximately $200,000 (roughly $2 million in 2024 dollars), and substantial deduction and sheltering opportunities existed.
- A Congressional Research Service analysis found that the statistical association between top marginal income tax rates and real GDP growth rates is not statistically significant across the postwar period.
- Countries with significantly higher total tax burdens — Sweden (42%), Denmark (45%), Germany (39%) — have maintained comparable or stronger productivity growth, human development indices, and median living standards relative to the US in recent decades.

The honest reading: the 1950s don't prove that 91% rates cause growth; they prove such rates are compatible with strong growth. The ETI literature, correctly applied, suggests revenue-maximizing rates well above current US levels (56–73%), with base-broadening as the complementary tool that lowers behavioral costs.

**The Reagan cut: what actually explains the 1980s expansion**: The 1981 Economic Recovery Tax Act is frequently cited as proof that cutting top rates from 70% to 50% produces growth. The academic evidence does not support monocausal attribution: the 1983–1989 expansion was driven primarily by Federal Reserve monetary easing after the Volcker disinflation, a recovery from the 1981–82 recession (the deepest since WWII), and increased defense spending. The tax cuts were partially reversed in 1982 (TEFRA — one of the largest peacetime tax increases in history) as deficits widened, suggesting supply-side revenue feedback did not materialize as claimed.

Sources: [Roosevelt Institute: Effective Progressive Tax Rates in the 1950s](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/blog/effective-progressive-tax-rates-in-the-1950s/); [CRS: Comparison of Historical Top Tax Rates With GDP Growth](https://www.taxnotes.com/research/federal/legislative-documents/congressional-research-service-reports/crs-updates-comparison-of-historical-top-tax-rates-with-rate/vpl8); [AEI: Why We Can't Go Back to 1950s Tax Rates](https://www.aei.org/economics/public-economics/why-we-cant-go-back-to-sky-high-1950s-tax-rates/)

## Section 10: The EITC as Evidence for Refundable Credits

### What the EITC Demonstrates

The Earned Income Tax Credit is the most evidence-supported anti-poverty intervention in the US federal toolkit. It approximates Milton Friedman's proposed negative income tax: a refundable credit that phases in with earned income (creating work incentives at the low end), reaches a maximum, and then phases out. Key 2024 metrics:

- 23 million filers claimed $64 billion in EITC benefits in the 2023 tax year. Average credit: $2,743.
- The EITC lifted approximately 4.4 million people above the poverty line in 2024 (2.3 million children) using the Supplemental Poverty Measure.
- Together with the Child Tax Credit, the EITC lifted approximately 6.4 million people out of poverty in 2023.
- Studies link $1,000 increases in EITC to a 7.3 percentage point increase in employment and a 9.4 percentage point reduction in poverty rates for eligible families.
- Downstream outcomes: children in EITC-eligible families show improved health outcomes, higher educational attainment, and higher adult earnings — persistent effects across the life course.

The EITC has been expanded under every president since Carter (including Reagan, Bush, and Clinton) and enjoys cross-partisan support that almost no other poverty-reduction program achieves. Its political durability reflects the work-incentive framing: benefits increase with employment and wages, creating a constituency that frames recipients as workers rather than beneficiaries.

**The negative income tax lineage**: Milton Friedman proposed a negative income tax (NIT) in 1962 as a replacement for the entire welfare system — a universal floor below which no citizen's income could fall, delivered through the tax system. The EITC is a partial implementation of this idea for working families with children. The contrast between the EITC's bipartisan success and the broader NIT's political failure illustrates the work-conditionality constraint in US policy.

**Limitations**: The EITC phase-out creates high marginal effective tax rates for earners moving from the peak to the phase-out range (effectively 21–36% above the normal rate). Adults without children receive a substantially smaller credit ($649 maximum in 2024 for childless workers). The American Rescue Plan's 2021 temporary expansion of the childless worker credit was not made permanent. The OBBBA makes the $2,000 per-child CTC permanent but does not expand the childless EITC. The OBBBA's Medicaid and SNAP cuts effectively claw back through spending reductions what the permanent CTC provides through the tax code.

Sources: [CBPP: The Earned Income Tax Credit](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/the-earned-income-tax-credit); [CRS: The EITC — An Economic Analysis](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44057); [ITEP: State EITCs 2024](https://itep.org/state-earned-income-tax-credits-2024/)

## Section 11: Financial Transaction Taxes — Design Determines Outcome

### The Revenue Potential and the Design Problem

A financial transaction tax (FTT) — a small percentage levy on trades of stocks, bonds, or derivatives — has been proposed as both a revenue source ($50–200B+ annually at 0.1% on equities) and a mechanism for reducing high-frequency trading (HFT) externalities. The design question is dispositive: the same tax at the same rate can either raise billions annually with minimal market impact, or cause most taxable trading to migrate offshore.

### Sweden's Failure vs. the UK's Success

**Sweden (1984–1991)**: Applied a 0.5% tax on purchases and sales of equity securities, payable only on transactions executed through registered Swedish brokerage houses. Within two years, more than 50% of Swedish equity trading had migrated to London. By 1990, approximately 60% of Swedish equity volume was executed offshore. Revenue: approximately 50 million Swedish kronor annually — less than 4% of the 1,500 million projected. The tax was repealed in 1990.

**UK stamp duty (1986–present)**: Levied at 0.5% on equity purchases. The critical design difference: the UK tax exempts intermediaries — market makers and dealers — from the tax on their purchasing transactions. Only the final investor's purchase from an intermediary is taxed. The UK stamp duty has raised approximately £3–4 billion annually with reasonable consistency and has not produced significant trading migration.

**Design principles from the comparison**: (1) Apply the tax to the investor, not to every transaction in the chain — this preserves market-making liquidity; (2) include non-equity instruments (derivatives, futures, options) proportionally to prevent substitution; (3) implement as a residence-based tax on US investors regardless of execution venue, to prevent offshore migration. A US FTT modeled on the UK structure, extended to derivatives at lower rates (0.005–0.01% on notional value), is estimated by Urban Institute analysts to raise $50–100 billion annually with manageable market impact.

**The HFT externality argument**: High-frequency trading firms execute millions of trades per second in strategies designed to front-run institutional orders (latency arbitrage) and capture small price improvements across millions of microsecond transactions. These strategies consume market infrastructure, reduce market quality for long-term investors, and serve no economic function beyond redistributing gains from other market participants. A financial transaction tax creates a per-trade cost that is negligible for long-horizon investors (who trade infrequently) but prohibitive for HFT strategies dependent on microsecond price movements. This is a feature, not a bug: the tax selectively burdens a market-destabilizing activity while leaving ordinary investor behavior unaffected.

The Political barrier: the financial services industry spends approximately $500 million annually on federal lobbying. Every major FTT proposal in Congress has died in committee. The Inclusive Prosperity Act (Jayapal, multiple Congresses) remains the principal vehicle; it has never received a committee vote.

Sources: [Urban Institute: Financial Transaction Taxes in Theory and Practice](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/78181/2000634-financial-transaction-taxes-in-theory-and-practice.pdf); [NBER: International Experiences with Securities Transaction Taxes](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c6276/c6276.pdf)

## Section 12: The Estate Tax — Concentrated Wealth and the "Death Tax" Framing

### Scale and Erosion

The federal estate tax now affects fewer than 1 in 1,000 estates. The OBBBA extended the $13.61 million per-individual exemption ($27.22 million per married couple) — making permanent the highest exemption in the estate tax's history. The top rate is 40%. The Tax Policy Center estimates approximately 3,800 estates were taxable in 2023 — roughly 0.12% of all deaths. Revenue: approximately $31 billion in FY2023.

**The "death tax" framing**: The estate tax was successfully rebranded as a "death tax" in the 1990s, with particular focus on family farms and small businesses. The Tax Policy Center's data shows that fewer than 80 estates per year nationwide face estate tax liability while also holding closely held farms or small businesses insufficient to cover the tax liability without forcing a sale. The vast majority of estate tax liability falls on large investment portfolios, real estate holdings, and business interests.

**Revenue potential at reform parameters**: Restoring the estate tax to 2009 parameters ($3.5 million exemption, 45% top rate) would affect approximately 0.3% of estates and raise an estimated $85–100 billion over a decade. The Sanders "For the 99.8%" graduated structure (45% above $3.5M, rising to 77% above $1B) would raise substantially more while affecting fewer than 0.2% of estates.

**The stepped-up basis connection**: The estate tax and the stepped-up basis interact: with a $13.61 million exemption, most of the wealth that benefits from stepped-up basis (estates between $1 million and $13 million in appreciated assets) faces no estate tax at all. Eliminating the stepped-up basis would capture the tax that the eroded estate tax no longer reaches, for approximately $40–50 billion annually in additional revenue.

**The gift tax as estate tax complement**: The estate tax interacts with the gift tax (unified credit) in a way that matters for reform design. Wealthy families use annual gift exclusions ($18,000 per recipient per year, 2024) and dynasty trusts (multi-generational trusts that avoid estate tax at each generational transfer) to reduce estate tax liability over long periods. The grantor retained annuity trust (GRAT) — a strategy allowing wealthy individuals to transfer future asset appreciation to heirs with minimal gift tax — is estimated to cost $4–8 billion annually in foregone transfer tax revenue (CBPP estimate). The IRS has limited capacity to value and challenge these complex transfer strategies; the DOGE-driven loss of senior revenue agents has further reduced that capacity. Estate tax reform must include anti-avoidance rules for dynasty trusts and GRATs to prevent the statutory exemption threshold from being circumvented through sophisticated planning.

Sources: [Tax Policy Center: Estate Tax Overview](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-many-people-pay-estate-tax); [CRS: Selected Issues in Tax Reform — The Estate and Gift Tax](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12846); [CRFB: Closing the Stepped-Up Basis Loophole](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/closing-stepped-basis-loophole); [CBPP: The Estate Tax — An Equitable and Adequate Revenue Source](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/the-estate-tax-an-equitable-and-adequate-revenue-source)

## Section 14: International Profit Shifting — Corporate Tax Avoidance

*The mechanisms and Apple/Ireland case study are covered in `domain-05-fiscal-reform.md`, Section 1.3. This section focuses on enforcement tools and the CTA rollback.*

The Tax Justice Network estimates US companies shift approximately $900 billion in profits offshore annually, representing $200–250 billion in annual revenue loss to the US Treasury.

**FATCA's documented success**: The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (2010) required foreign financial institutions to report US account holders to the IRS. In the five years after FATCA implementation, over 100,000 US taxpayers disclosed previously unreported offshore accounts through associated amnesty programs. Before FATCA, UBS alone facilitated approximately 52,000 secret accounts for wealthy US clients — a practice the bank paid $780 million to resolve.

**The Corporate Transparency Act rollback**: The CTA (2021) required domestic companies to report beneficial ownership information to FinCEN — identifying the ultimate human owners of US-registered shell companies used for tax avoidance and evasion. The Trump administration suspended CTA enforcement in 2025 and moved to terminate the beneficial ownership reporting requirement. This reversal directly undermines one of the primary anti-avoidance tools enacted to complement FATCA — restoring the shell company opacity that enables both profit shifting and money laundering.

**BEPS and GILTI's structural weakness**: The OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project, finalized in 2021, established the Pillar Two 15% global minimum corporate tax. TCJA's GILTI was a partial US implementation that fell short structurally — an 80% foreign tax credit effectively set the minimum at 10.5%. The OBBBA replaced GILTI with NCTI at approximately 12.6%, further widening the gap from the 15% floor. US firms can now shift profits to jurisdictions taxing between 12.6% and 15% without facing US top-up taxes or (under the 2026 OECD side-by-side arrangement) foreign jurisdiction top-up taxes — a substantial restoration of pre-TCJA incentive structures.

## Section 15: Counterarguments — Substantive Engagement

### The Supply-Side / Laffer Curve Claim

**The claim**: High marginal tax rates reduce economic activity, investment, and ultimately tax revenue. The Laffer curve's logic — that revenue-maximizing rates are below confiscatory levels — is correct in principle. The supply-side policy conclusion is that current US rates are near or above the revenue-maximizing level.

**The evidence-based response**: The ETI-based revenue-maximizing rate formula (Saez 2001, Saez/Slemrod/Giertz 2012) places the revenue-maximizing top rate at 56–73% for the US top-income distribution, using central ETI estimates from the academic literature. Current top rate: 37%. Even at ETI = 0.5 (the highest credible estimate for the US), the revenue-maximizing rate is 56% — substantially above current policy. The supply-side claim that the US is operating near the top of the Laffer curve has no empirical support in the ETI literature.

The critical qualifier: most of the measured ETI at high incomes reflects avoidance behavior — income shifting, sheltering — not real behavioral changes in labor supply. Base-broadening reform (eliminating shelters) lowers the effective ETI and raises the revenue-maximizing rate further, which is exactly what the 1986 Tax Reform Act demonstrated. The supply-side argument has strongest support in two specific contexts: (1) specific capital flight from wealth taxes with easy exit and no deemed-disposition rules; (2) corporate taxes in highly open economies where capital is genuinely internationally mobile. Neither of these applies cleanly to the proposed reforms under the conditions described in this document.

**The long-run growth record**: A Congressional Research Service analysis found no statistically significant association between top marginal income tax rates and real GDP growth rates across the postwar period. Countries with significantly higher total tax burdens — Sweden, Denmark, Germany — have maintained comparable or stronger productivity growth and median living standards. Post-TCJA corporate investment growth was modest despite the largest corporate tax cut in decades; the $2.7 trillion in buybacks from 2018–2022 flowed to shareholders, not capital formation.

---

### The "Double Taxation" Objection to Wealth Taxes

**The claim**: Income is already taxed when earned. A wealth tax taxes it again, constituting "double taxation" that is fundamentally unfair and discourages saving.

**The evidence-based response**: The double-taxation argument has three weaknesses. First, the income that generates wealth held by the ultra-wealthy is frequently not taxed at all when earned — unrealized capital gains on founding-equity stakes, inherited appreciated assets, and offshore profits routed through low-tax jurisdictions represent wealth accumulation that never passed through the income tax base in full. The ProPublica data shows Bezos paid a 0.98% "true rate" on wealth growth over two years; Musk 3.27%. The underlying income was not taxed when it accrued.

Second, "double taxation" is pervasive in the US tax code and accepted by consensus: corporate income is taxed at the corporate level and again when distributed as dividends; estates are taxed at death despite the underlying wealth having been taxed in income; property is taxed annually by local governments regardless of when the underlying purchase was taxed. A wealth tax is not uniquely or distinctively "double taxation."

Third, the wealth tax is not a second tax on the same income — it is a tax on the stock of accumulated wealth, a different base that captures the social and political power of extreme wealth concentration not captured by income taxes. This is the analytical core of the case for the tax as a structural intervention in democratic governance, not only as a revenue instrument.

---

### IRS Targeting Concerns

**The claim**: IRS enforcement is weaponized against political opponents and religious groups. Expanding IRS power and budget creates political risk of discriminatory enforcement.

**The evidence-based response**: The 2013 IRS targeting controversy — in which IRS personnel applied extra scrutiny to applications for 501(c)(4) status from groups with "Tea Party" or "Patriots" in their names — is the primary historical basis for this concern. The IRS Inspector General confirmed that liberal-sounding groups were also subject to heightened scrutiny, suggesting the issue was poorly designed screening criteria rather than ideological targeting. The practice was discontinued and resulted in disciplinary action.

The targeting concern argues for transparent audit selection criteria, Inspector General oversight, and due process protections — not for reducing enforcement capacity. The populations most harmed by reduced IRS enforcement are not the wealthy: they are ordinary taxpayers who cannot hire specialists to negotiate with understaffed IRS personnel. The audit rate for EITC recipients remains higher than for top-1% filers when properly controlled for complexity of return — a regressive enforcement pattern that more funding would help correct, not entrench. The 2024 IRA-funded IRS improvements specifically reduced EITC correspondence audits by 53% while increasing planned audits of taxpayers earning above $400,000 by 2.5 times — demonstrating that the enforcement equity concern is addressable through funding and policy design.

---

### The Capital Flight Objection to Wealth Taxes

**The claim**: Wealth taxes drive wealthy individuals abroad. The French and Norwegian evidence proves this. The US will face the same.

**The evidence-based response**: The French and Norwegian wealth tax failures share design features that a well-structured US proposal can avoid:
1. Very low thresholds (€1–2 million; NOK 1.7 million) capturing upper-middle-class professionals alongside the ultra-wealthy
2. EU/Schengen portability — emigration requires no visa, no language change, and Switzerland provides instant sanctuary with bilateral agreements
3. No effective exit tax on unrealized gains at the time of departure

The US context differs structurally on all three dimensions:
- **Worldwide taxation**: The US is one of only two countries (with Eritrea) that taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence. Moving abroad does not exempt a US citizen from US tax.
- **Section 877A exit tax**: Covered expatriates renouncing citizenship or long-term residency with net worth above $2 million face a mark-to-market deemed-disposition tax on all assets at the time of expatriation. The financial cost of renouncing US citizenship to evade a wealth tax at the $50 million threshold is substantial.
- **Threshold design**: The Saez/Zucman $50 million threshold captures a few thousand families, not the broad upper-middle class that fled France and Norway.

Norway's experience is instructive even on its own terms: 2025 wealth tax collections of 34 billion kroner exceeded 2022 levels of 27 billion kroner, because the broad base grows faster than high-end emigration depletes it. Norway also closed its exit loophole in 2024. The Norwegian experience demonstrates that a badly designed wealth tax can suffer capital flight; it does not demonstrate that a well-designed tax must.

The Summers objection — that avoidance through entity structuring, not flight, is the real concern — has more merit for a US wealth tax than the flight objection. This argues for strong third-party reporting, anti-abuse rules, and FATCA-integrated compliance infrastructure, not for abandoning the instrument.

---

### "Taxing Unrealized Gains Is Unworkable"

**The claim**: It is impossible to tax gains that have not been realized. Illiquid assets cannot be valued annually. Small business owners would be forced to sell to pay taxes on paper gains.

**The evidence-based response**: This objection has more merit for closely held businesses and illiquid assets than for publicly traded securities. Billionaire wealth is overwhelmingly concentrated in publicly traded securities — Amazon shares, Tesla stock, Apple equity — which are valued every second of every trading day and already reported to the IRS on 1099-B forms. Mark-to-market on liquid assets above $100 million in net worth is administratively trivial. The US already applies mark-to-market to futures contracts, certain dealer inventory, and foreign currency positions — with no insurmountable administrative difficulty.

For illiquid assets — private company stakes, real estate, art — the objection is more valid. The policy design addresses this directly: installment payment options for illiquid asset holders, safe-harbor actuarial valuation methods, and the option to confine mark-to-market to liquid assets while using carryover-with-interest provisions for illiquid assets. The estate tax system already requires annual valuation of similarly complex assets for large estates. The objection proves that mark-to-market on illiquid assets is harder than on liquid assets — not that it is impossible. The sequence priority is to start with liquid assets and build administrative capacity, consistent with the reform sequencing in domain-05-fiscal-reform.md.

### "Corporate Taxes Kill Jobs and Drive Away Investment"

**The claim**: Corporate income taxes reduce the return on capital investment, raise the cost of capital, and ultimately reduce employment and wages. Cutting corporate rates attracts foreign investment and stimulates domestic job creation. The TCJA experience is cited as confirming this: the rate cut from 35% to 21% produced an economic expansion.

**The evidence-based response**: The investment argument has more theoretical credibility than the wage argument (discussed in Section 5), but the evidence from TCJA does not support the large investment response predicted by cost-of-capital models. 280 large US corporations spent $2.7 trillion on stock buybacks from 2018–2022 — 98% more than in the comparable pre-TCJA period. The JCT/Federal Reserve 2022 study found no wage gains for bottom-90% workers. Capital investment growth in 2018 was modest; the equipment investment response was real but small relative to the size of the rate cut.

Three structural responses:

First, the cost-of-capital argument is most credible for small, credit-constrained firms that invest from retained earnings and face the full statutory rate. Large publicly traded corporations — the primary beneficiaries of TCJA's corporate cut — fund most investment from low-cost debt (interest-deductible, effectively subsidized) and retained earnings, and face far lower effective rates than the statutory rate due to depreciation, credits, and profit-shifting. For these firms, the statutory rate has limited effect on marginal investment decisions.

Second, accelerated depreciation and investment tax credits directly offset cost-of-capital effects from higher statutory rates — allowing policy to raise statutory rates while protecting investment incentives through targeted credits. This is what most OECD countries do (high statutory rates with investment-oriented deductions) and what the 1986 Tax Reform Act partially demonstrated.

Third, the competitive dynamic under OECD Pillar Two has fundamentally changed the corporate tax competition calculus. Under the 15% global minimum, if the US raises its corporate rate to 25%, the revenue stays in the US. If the US rate remains at 21% (below the minimum that most countries are implementing), other countries collect top-up taxes and the competitive "benefit" of the lower US rate is shared with foreign treasuries rather than US workers or domestic investment. The OBBBA's NCTI at 12.6% effective rate means foreign governments can collect the gap between 12.6% and their domestic minimums — a transfer of US tax revenue to foreign governments, not a competitive advantage.

### "The Tax Code Is Already Too Complex to Add New Instruments"

**The claim**: Wealth taxes, mark-to-market regimes, and financial transaction taxes would add unmanageable complexity on top of an already 70,000-page tax administration system. Simplification should precede or replace revenue-raising reform.

**The evidence-based response**: The complexity objection has genuine merit — the CAMT's near-zero revenue performance ($572 million against $35 billion projected) is a real example of a revenue-raising instrument defeated by complexity in its own design. The IRS waived CAMT penalties in 2023 because the rules were too complex to comply with.

However, the complexity argument is selectively applied. The most administratively complex parts of the US tax code are the result of successful lobbying: $1.8 trillion in annual tax expenditures, each with its own eligibility criteria, phase-outs, and interaction effects. §199A's pass-through rules require 23 pages of proposed regulations for a single deduction. The corporate alternative minimum tax's book-income AFSI calculation requires reconciling GAAP and tax accounting across complex multinational corporate structures. This complexity was created by concentrated interests and serves them; it should not be used as a shield against revenue-raising reform that affects them.

The appropriate response is correct sequencing: start with administratively simple instruments (mark-to-market on publicly traded securities, stepped-up basis elimination, carried interest reclassification) and build administrative capacity for more complex ones (wealth tax on illiquid assets, comprehensive offshore enforcement). This is the sequencing described in the reform proposals section of domain-05-fiscal-reform.md. Complexity is an argument for good design and sequencing, not an argument against reform.

The Direct File termination is instructive in the opposite direction: the IRS demonstrated in 2024–2025 that it can run a simple, accurate, free filing system. The complexity that serves the tax preparation industry was preserved not because it is inherent to the tax system but because it serves a concentrated interest. Simplification and revenue-raising reform are complementary, not competing objectives.

### Flat Tax / Single-Rate Arguments

The flat tax proposal — a single rate on all income above a personal exemption — resurfaces regularly (Forbes 1996; various Republican budget proposals). Its claims:

**The fairness argument**: Everyone pays the "same" percentage. This conflates horizontal equity (equal treatment of equals) with vertical equity (proportional sacrifice). A 20% rate takes $2,000 from a $10,000 income and $200,000 from a $1 million income — the same percentage but not the same marginal sacrifice, given declining marginal utility of income.

**The efficiency argument**: Eliminating bracket variation reduces the ETI for high earners by reducing avoidance incentives. This is partially correct but overstated: most ETI avoidance occurs at the top through complex sheltering and income shifting, and a flat tax eliminates brackets without eliminating the deductions and exclusions that drive most avoidance. Base-broadening matters more than rate-flattening for reducing avoidance.

**Distributional reality**: Every serious analysis of flat tax proposals finds they reduce taxes for the wealthy and raise them for the middle class. The Tax Policy Center consistently finds the top quintile receives the majority of the benefit from flat tax proposals. A pure flat tax without a substantial personal exemption is regressive by construction. Even with a generous exemption, the elimination of the EITC — incompatible with a flat tax structure — would substantially harm working families.

**The 1986 precedent as a better model**: The 1986 Tax Reform Act achieved genuine simplification — collapsing multiple brackets to two rates (15% and 28%), eliminating many shelters and loopholes, and broadening the base while lowering rates. It was broadly revenue-neutral, had bipartisan support, and reduced avoidance substantially. It demonstrates that base-broadening-with-rate-reduction is achievable; it also demonstrates that the political gains from simplification are temporary — the code re-complexified rapidly as concentrated interests lobbied for restored preferences.

Sources: [Tax Policy Center: Flat Tax Distributional Analysis](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/what-are-arguments-and-against-flat-tax); [Saez (2001): Using Elasticities to Derive Optimal Income Tax Rates](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/derive.pdf); [Saez, Slemrod, Giertz (2012): ETI — A Critical Review](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-slemrod-giertzJEL12.pdf)

### "Progressive Taxation Punishes Success and Discourages Entrepreneurship"

**The claim**: High marginal rates reduce the reward from additional effort and investment, discouraging the risk-taking that drives entrepreneurship and innovation. If successful founders keep less of their upside, fewer people will attempt high-risk ventures.

**The evidence-based response**: The empirical literature on entrepreneurship and taxation does not support a strong deterrence effect at the tax rates under discussion. First, most entrepreneurial income at the founding stage comes in the form of equity appreciation — unrealized gains that are not taxed at all under current or proposed law until realization. A founder who builds a company from zero to $1 billion faces zero income tax on $999 million in wealth creation during the growth phase; the reforms discussed here address taxation at realization and death, not during the accumulation phase. A progressive income tax on high annual incomes does not directly affect the dominant form of entrepreneurial return.

Second, entrepreneurship in the US flourished during decades of high marginal rates — the 1950s (91% top rate), 1960s (70%), and 1970s (70%). Silicon Valley's foundational companies (Intel, Apple, Oracle) were founded during periods of high marginal rates. The claim that lower top marginal rates are responsible for US entrepreneurial dynamism is not empirically supported by the historical record. The countries with the highest startup rates per capita relative to population include Denmark, Sweden, and Canada — all of which have substantially higher total tax burdens than the US.

Third, the carried interest loophole, stepped-up basis, and unrealized gains deferral primarily benefit established asset managers and investors, not active entrepreneurs building companies. The distinction between earned income from building new enterprises and unearned advantage from holding accumulated capital is precisely the distinction that a well-designed progressive tax system should maintain — taxing the former more lightly (through EITC-style incentives for working founders, lower rates on business income reinvested in growth) and the latter more heavily (through mark-to-market on liquid assets and estate tax on inherited wealth).

## Section 16: Fiscal Policy and Democratic Legitimacy — The Political Economy Connection

### The Gilens/Page Evidence Applied to Tax Policy

The framing of tax reform as a democracy issue — not merely an economic policy choice — is supported by the most systematic study of policy responsiveness in the American political science literature.

Gilens and Page (2014, *Perspectives on Politics*) analyzed 1,779 policy outcomes from 1981 to 2002 against the preferences of three groups: average-income citizens, economic elites, and organized interest groups. Finding: policy outcomes correlate strongly with economic elite preferences and with the positions of organized business interest groups. They show no statistically significant correlation with the preferences of average-income citizens when elite preferences are controlled for.

Specifically: a proposed policy change with low support among economically elite Americans is adopted only about 18% of the time even when supported by a majority of average citizens. A change with high elite support is adopted roughly 45% of the time regardless of public opinion. "Mass-based interest groups and average citizens have little or no independent influence" on policy outcomes.

Tax policy is precisely the domain where this asymmetry is most consequential: polling consistently shows majority or strong-majority public support for higher taxes on the wealthy and corporations. A 2024 Gallup poll found 52% of Americans believe corporations pay too little in taxes; 60% believe upper-income individuals pay too little. These preferences have not translated into legislative outcomes — TCJA passed without a single Democratic vote, and the OBBBA made its provisions permanent, in both cases against majority public opinion on the distributional direction.

### The Fiscal Feedback Loop — Why This Domain Is Different

Tax policy is particularly susceptible to elite capture because:

1. **Complexity is intentionally opaque**: Most voters cannot identify the carried interest loophole, the stepped-up basis, or §199A in isolation. The benefits of complexity — loopholes, preferences — are concentrated and visible to their recipients; the costs — revenue foregone, distributional distortion — are diffuse and invisible.

2. **Lobbying ROI is exceptionally high**: H&R Block and Intuit/TurboTax spent approximately $39 million lobbying against IRS free filing between 2006 and 2023, preserving an industry that captures approximately $10–20 billion annually from Americans who would file for free under a pre-populated return system. Return on lobbying investment: roughly 5,000:1. The private equity industry spent tens of millions lobbying against carried interest reform; the loophole survived Trump's own stated preference. The 2025 OECD side-by-side deal exempting US multinationals from Pillar Two top-up taxes was secured through intensive corporate lobbying of the Treasury Department and the trade representative's office, with minimal congressional oversight.

3. **The IRS as uniquely vulnerable**: The IRS is the only major federal agency whose budget is directly cut by the constituencies it regulates. No other federal agency faces the same dynamic. The 2022–2025 funding cycle — $80 billion appropriated by IRA, $41.8 billion rescinded by three successive legislative acts, another $11.7 billion cut in the OBBBA plus $2.5 billion in FY2026 budget request cuts — is possible because high-income tax enforcement affects precisely the donors who fund the campaigns of legislators who control the IRS appropriation.

### The 1986 Precedent: What Made Reform Possible

The Tax Reform Act of 1986 — which broadened the base substantially while reducing the statutory top rate from 50% to 28% — passed with bipartisan support, 97–3 in the Senate, combining rate reductions with substantial base-broadening against intense lobbying by affected industries. It demonstrates that comprehensive reform is politically achievable when the distributional argument is made clearly.

The conditions that made 1986 possible and that are absent today: (a) it was explicitly revenue-neutral — no net tax increase, which neutralized anti-tax Republican opposition; (b) it eliminated loopholes used by the wealthy and corporations while lowering rates — giving each side a political win; (c) Reagan provided sustained personal leadership; (d) the reform occurred before the post-Reagan re-concentration of lobbying power and before Citizens United (2010) opened unlimited corporate political spending.

The 1986 lesson for today: base-broadening creates coalition opportunities that rate-increase-alone does not. Eliminating §199A's high-income windfall, closing the stepped-up basis, and closing the carried interest loophole can be framed as base-broadening in the 1986 tradition — closing loopholes that distort capital allocation — even when the net revenue effect is large. This framing is more durable politically than an explicit "tax increase on the wealthy" frame, though the distributive effect is identical.

**> Evidence callout**: The OBBBA's preservation of the carried interest loophole despite the president's stated support for closing it is the clearest contemporary demonstration of the Gilens/Page mechanism. Average citizens, organized labor, and the president all preferred eliminating the loophole. Private equity's lobbying overcame all three. This is not a failure of the democratic process — it is the democratic process operating exactly as Gilens and Page's research predicts it operates under current campaign finance and lobbying conditions.

### The IRS Enforcement Crisis: Near-Term Actions

The enforcement crisis has two components requiring different responses:

**Congressional appropriations**: The $41.8 billion in IRA rescissions are locked in through the OBBBA. Restoration requires: (a) a future Congress with different composition to appropriate enforcement funding; (b) in the interim, legislative riders preventing further DOGE-driven workforce reductions. The 130+ House Democrats who have written letters opposing IRS enforcement cuts are the current vehicle for public accountability.

**Litigation**: Multiple legal challenges to DOGE-directed workforce reductions are underway, including court orders temporarily blocking some IRS terminations. The legal framework is unsettled; however, the IRS has lost 31% of its revenue agents regardless of litigation outcomes because many terminations and voluntary departures are not easily reversed.

**Public accountability**: The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration (TIGTA) continues to publish reports on IRS capacity and IRA spending. TIGTA's March 2025 snapshot confirmed the 31% revenue agent loss. These reports provide primary-source documentation for advocacy.

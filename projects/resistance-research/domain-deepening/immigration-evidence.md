# Immigration Policy — Evidence Deepening

*Prepared April 2026. Supplements Domain 16 in the Democratic Renewal Proposal. Does not restate the reform architecture — deepens the evidentiary foundation with specific data, incident-level documentation, the international benchmarks that reform arguments depend on, and the fiscal arithmetic that should anchor any policy debate.*

## Gaps This Document Fills

The Immigration and Citizenship domain correctly identifies the structural failures: enforcement without accountability, courts without independence, a legal system that manufactures the undocumented population it then punishes, and a detention apparatus that enriches private industry through human captivity. What it is thinner on:

- The precise per-day and per-deportation fiscal arithmetic — the actual numbers that make alternatives-to-detention cost comparisons concrete
- The economic contribution data — GDP, tax, Social Security, entrepreneurship — that answers the "fiscal burden" framing dominant in public debate
- Granular documentation of 2025–2026 enforcement actions, including wrongful deportation cases involving US citizens and legal permanent residents with federal court orders
- Family separation: the updated reunification status, including how many children remain separated six years later
- ICE detention death data for 2024–2025, with the documented decline in oversight inspections
- The Card-Borjas wage debate — what the research actually shows vs. how it is misrepresented
- Visa overstay data — the overlooked fact that 40%+ of the undocumented population entered legally
- International benchmarks with outcome numbers, not just system descriptions: Canada's Express Entry employment rates, Germany's first-year Skilled Immigration Act figures, Australia's reform arithmetic
- The mass deportation fiscal case — Penn Wharton, American Immigration Council, and CBO projections from 2025

## Section 1: The Fiscal Arithmetic of the Current System

### 1.1 ICE Detention: Costs Per Day, Per Year, Per Deportation

The proposal states that detention costs $150–$300/day versus $4.50/day for community-based alternatives. The specific verified figures:

**Per-day detention cost**: ICE's own FY2025 bed rate is **$164.65/day per detainee**. The 2024 average across facility types was approximately **$152/day**, producing an annual detention bill of roughly **$3.4 billion** for a daily average population of approximately 41,500.

**All-in deportation cost**: The complete lifecycle cost — from arrest through detention to removal — averages **$17,121 per person**, according to the American Immigration Council's October 2024 analysis of ICE operational data.

**Annual ICE budget trajectory**: The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, enacted July 2025, includes **$45 billion** for expanding detention capacity over four years and approved **$29.9 billion** toward ICE enforcement and deportation operations — tripling ICE's annual budget. ICE's pre-OBBBA budget was approximately $8 billion/year.

**Alternative to detention**: ICE's Intensive Supervision Appearance Program (ISAP) costs **$4.20/day per participant**. Community-based case management through nonprofit organizations costs approximately **$14/day per family member** based on 2018 pilot data. The ratio is not 1/30th (as the proposal states) — it is more conservatively 1/10th to 1/40th depending on the specific program, but compliance outcomes are equivalent or superior.

Sources: [American Immigration Council: Cost of Immigration Enforcement Fact Sheet 2024](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/cost_of_immigration_enforcement_factsheet_2024.pdf) | [National Immigration Forum: Immigration Detention Costs in a Time of Mass Deportation](https://forumtogether.org/article/immigration-detention-costs-in-a-time-of-mass-deportation/) | [American Immigration Council: Congress Approves Unprecedented Funding for Mass Detention/Deportation](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/press-release/congress-approves-unprecedented-funding-mass-detention-deportation-2025/)

### 1.2 Alternatives to Detention: Compliance Rate Evidence

The 99% court appearance rate stated in the proposal requires the specific data behind it:

- ICE's own ISAP contractor (BI, Inc.) reported a **99.6% appearance rate** for participants in its "Full Service" program (Contract Year 2013 data, the most granular public figure available).
- Two national alternatives-to-detention programs more recently reported **97% appearance rates**.
- The FEMA/CRCL Case Management Pilot Program received **$15 million** in FY2024 funding and was operating in five cities before being terminated in January 2025.

The 99%+ appearance rate for ATD participants compares to lower rates for detained persons released on bond without case management support — the relevant comparison is not detention vs. no supervision but detention vs. supervised community alternatives. The evidence supports supervised community alternatives as equally or more effective at ensuring court appearances at a fraction of the cost.

### 1.3 The Mass Deportation Fiscal Case

**American Immigration Council (October 2024)**: A one-time mass deportation of the estimated 11+ million undocumented population would cost at minimum **$315 billion**, broken down as:
- $89.3 billion for arrests
- $167.8 billion for detention
- $34.1 billion for legal processing
- $24.1 billion for removals

Sustaining one million deportations per year would cost **$88 billion annually**, the majority in detention infrastructure.

**Penn Wharton Budget Model (July 2025)**: The most rigorous independent fiscal modeling found:
- A 4-year mass deportation policy reduces GDP by **1.0% by 2034** and requires $82.7 billion in additional outlays over the 10-year budget window
- A 10-year sustained policy reduces GDP by **3.3% by 2034** and **4.9% by 2054**, with total fiscal cost of **$987 billion** over ten years after economic feedback effects
- Per-capita wages fall **0.5% (4-year) to 1.7% (10-year)** — meaning mass deportation makes the average American worker poorer, not richer
- The federal deficit **increases** under mass deportation, directly contradicting the fiscal argument made for it

**American Immigration Council extended analysis**: Long-term mass deportation (until no undocumented population remains) would produce a **4.2% to 6.8% GDP loss**, totaling $1.1 to $1.7 trillion in lost output.

## Section 2: The Economic Contribution of Immigrants

### 2.1 Tax Contributions: Undocumented Immigrants

The "fiscal burden" framing in public debate systematically omits the tax contribution side of the ledger. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP) data:

**Undocumented immigrants pay significant taxes**:
- In 2022, the 10.9 million undocumented immigrants in the United States paid **$96.7 billion in total taxes**, including $19.5 billion in federal income taxes and $32.3 billion in federal payroll taxes.
- In 2023, undocumented immigrant households paid **$89.8 billion** in combined federal, state, and local taxes.
- Undocumented immigrants pay **$32.1 billion into Social Security and Medicare annually** — programs from which they are categorically excluded from receiving benefits.
- More specifically: **$25.6 billion to Social Security** and **$6.4 billion to Medicare** per year, contributing to programs that pay retirement and health benefits to American citizens, not to themselves.

**The Social Security subsidy**: The Congressional Budget Office's 2024 analysis of the immigration surge found that the surge population is expected to contribute **$348 billion in Social Security revenues** between 2024 and 2034, while collecting only approximately **$1 billion in Social Security benefits** over the same period. This is not a burden — it is a structural subsidy to the retirement security of native-born Americans.

### 2.2 GDP and Labor Market Contributions

**CBO 10-year projection**: The congressional budget office projects that immigration — including the recent surge — will boost total nominal GDP by **$1.3 trillion (3.2%) in 2034** and **$8.9 trillion over the 2024–2034 period**. Federal tax revenue increases by **$1.2 trillion** over the same period. The federal deficit decreases by **$900 billion** over the 10-year window. These are not advocacy estimates — they are from the non-partisan budget scorer used by Congress for all fiscal legislation.

**All immigrant households**: Immigrant households (legal and undocumented combined) pay **$16.80 of every $100 in federal, state, and local taxes collected** — roughly in proportion to their population share, and in some categories above it.

**Entrepreneurship**: In 2025, the American Immigration Council documented that **46% of Fortune 500 companies** (230 companies) were founded by immigrants or their children — the highest share in the report's history. These companies collectively generated **$8.6 trillion in revenue in FY2023** and employ more than **15.5 million people worldwide**. The manufacturing sector has the highest concentration: more than two-thirds of Fortune 500 manufacturers were founded by immigrants or their children.

**Agricultural dependency**: Approximately **35% of hired agricultural workers** — an estimated 680,000 of 1.93 million workers — lack legal work authorization. This is not a policy failure that enforcement can solve: the legal pathway (H-2A visa) is chronically under-resourced relative to demand, and the agricultural sector's structural dependence on unauthorized labor is the direct consequence of a legal system that has never provided sufficient supply for actual demand.

### 2.3 The Card-Borjas Wage Debate — What the Research Actually Shows

Immigration's effect on native worker wages is the most contested empirical question in immigration economics. The debate is frequently misrepresented in both directions.

**The Card finding (1990, updated)**: David Card's foundational Mariel Boatlift study found that a 7% increase in Miami's labor force from Cuban immigrants produced no measurable wage effect on native workers. Card's subsequent work consistently found that immigration's wage effects on native workers are negligible to slightly positive, because immigrants and native workers are imperfect substitutes — they do not directly compete for the same jobs.

**The Borjas finding**: Harvard economist George Borjas, analyzing the 1980–2000 period using a "skill-cell" methodology, found that immigration reduced the average wage of similarly-educated native workers by 3–4%, and by 9% for workers without a high school diploma. Borjas's work is the most-cited empirical case that immigration reduces wages for low-wage native workers.

**The methodological disagreement**: The substantial difference in findings turns on methodology. The "spatial" approach (Card) compares cities with different immigrant concentrations and finds minimal effects. The "skill-cell" approach (Borjas) compares education-experience cohorts nationally and finds larger negative effects. A 2025 meta-analysis in the Journal of Development Economics reviewing multiple studies concluded that "the consensus is very strong that there are not significant costs to U.S.-born workers from immigration" at current levels — but that low-wage native workers without high school diplomas face the most direct competition in Borjas-framework analyses.

**The practical takeaway for reform design**: The research supports a focus on labor protections for low-wage workers — including both immigrants and native workers — rather than restriction. When all workers have enforceable rights (Domain 17), the wage suppression mechanism that Borjas identifies (exploitable undocumented workers bidding down wages) disappears. Labor reform and immigration reform are functionally interdependent.

Sources: [EPI: Immigration and Wages — Methodological advancements confirm modest gains for native workers](https://www.epi.org/publication/bp255/) | [CNBC: Are immigrants taking jobs from 'native' U.S. workers? Here's what economists say (September 2024)](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/2024/09/28/are-immigrants-taking-jobs-from-us-workers-heres-what-economists-say.html) | [Cato Institute: Does Immigration Reduce Wages?](https://www.cato.org/cato-journal/fall-2017/does-immigration-reduce-wages)

## Section 3: Who the Undocumented Population Actually Is

### 3.1 Visa Overstays: The Border-Centric Frame Is Wrong

Public debate treats the undocumented population as primarily composed of unauthorized border crossers. The data contradicts this.

**40%+ entered legally**: The DHS FY2024 Entry/Exit Overstay Report documents that in FY2023, approximately **510,000 people** overstayed their visas — representing nearly **40% of new undocumented arrivals** that year, alongside an estimated 860,000 unauthorized border crossings. Over the accumulated undocumented population (built up over decades), more than **40% are individuals who entered legally** and remained beyond their authorized period.

**The implication**: Border wall and enforcement-at-the-border spending — which constitutes the bulk of enforcement expenditure — addresses at most 60% of the mechanism creating the undocumented population. A policy framework that treats all undocumented presence as a border security problem misdiagnoses 40% of its own subject.

**Overstay rates are low by percentage**: The FY2024 overall suspected in-country overstay rate was **0.92%** — meaning over 99% of nonimmigrant visa holders departed or adjusted status as required. The problem is not a dysfunctional overstay detection system; it is the absence of a legal mechanism for people who were here, integrated, and did not leave.

Sources: [DHS: Entry/Exit Overstay Report Fiscal Year 2024](https://www.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/2025-09/25_0912_cbp_entry-exit-overstay-report-fiscal-year-2024.pdf) | [Statista: Visa Overstays Make up Almost 40 Percent of Illegal U.S. Entries](https://www.statista.com/chart/16701/visa-overstays-outnumber-illegal-crossings/)

### 3.2 DACA Recipients: The Specific Profile

**Active recipients**: As of 2024, approximately **525,210 active DACA recipients** live in the United States. More than half are female; 81% were born in Mexico, 4% in El Salvador, 3% in Guatemala.

**Average time in the US**: DACA recipients arrived at an average age of **6.6 years old** and have spent an average of **25.2 years** in the country. They are Americans in every practical sense — educated in American schools, speaking English as a primary language, with family, careers, and community ties in the United States.

**US citizen family members**: If DACA were terminated, approximately **254,000 US-born children** with at least one DACA parent would face family separation. An additional **110,000 US citizen spouses** and **300,000 other US citizen family members** would be affected.

**Economic contribution**: 92.4% of DACA recipients are currently employed. Their average hourly wage has increased from **$11.88 before DACA** to **$33.55 in 2024** — a 182% increase. They contribute collectively **$2.1 billion annually to Social Security and Medicare**. After receiving DACA, 60% moved to better-paying jobs and 48.7% to jobs with better working conditions.

**The wage growth tells a specific story**: DACA recipients earning $33.55/hour pay substantially more in taxes than they would have at $11.88/hour. The wage increase is not a gift — it reflects the economic value unlocked when a person can legally work in the job for which they are actually qualified, rather than the underpaid shadow-economy job they could access without documentation.

## Section 4: The Asylum System Dysfunction — Numbers

### 4.1 The Backlog

**3.8 million pending cases**: As of Q3 2025, the US immigration court system has a backlog of approximately **3.8 million pending cases** — up from 3.7 million at the end of 2024. Of these, approximately **2.4 million** are asylum seekers' cases.

**Average wait time**: For all cases, the average pending case has been waiting approximately **636 days** as of early 2026. For asylum cases specifically, the National Immigration Forum reported an estimated wait of **4.3 years** in 2024. These waits are not a feature — they are a system failure with direct human consequences: people cannot work legally, cannot make permanent life plans, and are subject to removal at any time for years while awaiting a hearing.

**Caseload per judge**: By 2024, the average immigration judge carried **5,286 pending cases** — double the caseload of a decade prior. By March 2026, some measures show approximately 3,300+ cases per judge as early terminations and dismissals have reduced the count, but the NAIJ's recommended maximum caseload is approximately 700 cases per judge. The system is running at 5–7x recommended capacity.

**Judge hiring has collapsed**: New judge hires fell to **46 in 2024**, the lowest since 2015, and through Q3 of 2025, only **14 new judges** have been hired. The administration has simultaneously dismissed large numbers of cases categorically — a maneuver that reduces the backlog count without adjudicating claims on the merits.

**The backlog as a structural tool**: The 3.8 million case backlog is not an administrative failure — it is the predictable output of a system chronically underfunded relative to its caseload, subject to constant political interference in case categories and priority, and structured so that immigration judges answer to the Attorney General (who is simultaneously prosecuting the cases they hear).

Sources: [TRAC Immigration: Immigration Court Backlog](https://tracreports.org/phptools/immigration/backlog/) | [National Immigration Forum: Immigration Detention Costs](https://forumtogether.org/article/immigration-detention-costs-in-a-time-of-mass-deportation/) | [GAO: U.S. Immigration Courts See a Significant and Growing Backlog](https://www.gao.gov/blog/u.s.-immigration-courts-see-significant-and-growing-backlog)

## Section 5: Detention — Deaths, Oversight Collapse, and Specific Cases

### 5.1 Deaths in Custody: 2024–2025 Data

**2024 baseline**: 11 deaths in ICE detention were reported for 2024.

**2025 — the deadliest year in two decades**: **32 people died** in ICE custody in 2025 — nearly **three times the 2024 rate** and the highest annual total since 2004. NPR's October 2025 reporting documented 2025 as "the deadliest year to be in ICE custody in decades."

**Oversight inspections collapsed**: ICE detention facility inspections declined by **36.25%** in 2025 even as the detained population surged to record highs. The Project on Government Oversight documented this inverse relationship: more detainees, fewer inspections, more deaths.

**Preventability finding**: A June 2024 ACLU report found that the **overwhelming majority** of deaths in ICE detention could have been prevented if ICE had provided clinically appropriate medical care. The DHS Office of Inspector General found in 2023 that **at least 1 in every 5 deaths** at ICE and CBP facilities in FY2021 occurred because detainees were not given timely or adequate medical care.

**The structural cause**: The DHS OIG's 2021 report identified the specific mechanism — "remote locations, a cumbersome hiring process, and competing opportunities" — that prevents adequate medical staffing at private detention facilities. Private facilities have a financial interest in minimizing operating costs (including medical staff costs) while maximizing bed occupancy. This is not a bug in the private detention model — it is a predictable output of aligning financial incentives with captivity rather than care.

## Section 6: Family Separation — The Documented Record

### 6.1 Zero Tolerance: Scale and Ongoing Failure

**The program**: The "Zero Tolerance" policy was officially adopted across the entire US-Mexico border from April to June 2018, prosecuting all adults crossing without authorization and automatically separating accompanying children.

**Total separated**: More than **5,500 children**, including infants, were separated from their families. The practice continued for at least 18 months after the policy's nominal end — an estimated **1,100 additional families** were separated between June 2018 and the end of 2019.

**Reunification as of 2025–2026**: Six years after the policy ended:
- Approximately **1,360 children** — nearly **30%** of all known separated children — may remain separated from their parents as of 2024.
- There is no current plan or initiative for reunification of the remaining separated families.
- Human Rights Watch's December 2024 report, titled "We Need to Take Away Children: Zero Accountability Six Years After Zero Tolerance," documented that no federal official has faced criminal accountability for the policy and that the reunification effort has effectively been abandoned.

**The Inspector General findings**: DHS OIG documented systemic record-keeping failures that made tracking and reuniting families difficult by design — the government separated children from parents without creating adequate records linking them. This is not administrative negligence; court-filed documents from the ACLU's Ms. L. v. ICE litigation revealed that the administration anticipated the reunification difficulty and proceeded anyway.

**Second-term acceleration**: The Trump administration's second term has initiated new family separation through structural means — detaining parents while removing children (or vice versa) through operations that avoid the specific "Zero Tolerance" label while producing equivalent results. The February 2025 case of a **2-year-old US citizen deported to Honduras** with her pregnant mother "with no meaningful process" represents a direct continuation of the separation paradigm.

Sources: [HRW: "We Need to Take Away Children": Zero Accountability Six Years After Zero Tolerance (December 2024)](https://www.hrw.org/report/2024/12/16/we-need-take-away-children/zero-accountability-six-years-after-zero-tolerance) | [University of Cincinnati IHRLR: Forcible Family Separation at the U.S.-Mexico Border (March 2025)](https://lawblogs.uc.edu/ihrlr/2025/03/14/forcible-family-separation-at-the-u-s-mexico-border-continued-impact-years-after-zero-tolerance/) | [American Immigration Council: A Look Back at the Family Separation Policy](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/report/family-separation-policy/)

## Section 7: 2025–2026 Specific Documented Enforcement Actions

### 7.1 Wrongful Deportation: Documented Cases

The Trump administration's second term has produced a documented pattern of deportations in violation of federal court orders and the removal of individuals with legal status, including US citizens and legal residents. Courts have ruled more than **4,400 times** that the Trump administration is detaining people illegally.

**Kilmar Abrego Garcia (Maryland)**: A 29-year-old with a court-issued "withholding of removal" order — which legally barred deportation to El Salvador — was arrested and deported to CECOT in March 2025. The US Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Judge Xinis "properly requires the Government to 'facilitate' Abrego Garcia's release." The administration refused to comply. As of April 2026, Garcia remains imprisoned at CECOT in violation of Supreme Court order.

**Chanthila Souvannarath (Louisiana)**: ICE deported a man with a 20-year substantive claim to US citizenship to Laos in direct violation of a federal court ruling recognizing his citizenship claim.

**Jordin Melgar-Salmeron (Virginia)**: Deported to El Salvador on May 7, 2025, **thirty minutes** after a federal court issued a stay of removal. Courts directed the administration to facilitate his return.

**2-year-old US citizen**: Deported to Honduras in April 2025 "with no meaningful process" while her pregnant mother was removed. A US citizen child was removed from the country of her citizenship without due process.

**Britania Uriostegui Rios**: A Mexican transgender woman deported to Mexico in November 2025 in violation of an immigration court order, discovered only after her attorneys made inquiry.

These are not isolated errors — they represent a systemic pattern of non-compliance with judicial orders. Federal courts have directed the Trump administration to return wrongfully deported individuals in at least **four documented cases** in the first six months of the second term, with compliance consistently resisted.

### 7.2 Alien Enemies Act Invocation: The 1798 Statute in 2025

In March 2025, the Trump administration invoked the **Alien Enemies Act of 1798** — a wartime statute last used to intern Japanese Americans in World War II — to deport **238 Venezuelans** to El Salvador, including **137 under the statute** itself and 101 under regular immigration law. They were transferred to CECOT, El Salvador's maximum-security terrorism prison, under an agreement paying El Salvador to hold US deportees.

**The judicial confrontation**: District Judge James Boasberg imposed a temporary restraining order. The planes flew anyway. The Justice Department argued Boasberg had no jurisdiction over foreign policy. The Supreme Court ruled on April 8, 2025 that Boasberg was without jurisdiction to issue his order — but in December 2025, a federal judge ruled that the deportations violated due process, were conducted in defiance of court orders, and ordered the administration to provide due process to the deported class.

The September 2025 federal appeals court ruling **blocked further use** of the Alien Enemies Act for deportations, finding it was being applied outside any recognized wartime context.

**The historical precedent being invoked**: The Alien Enemies Act was used to intern 120,000 Japanese Americans (70,000 of whom were US citizens) during World War II. Its invocation in peacetime against a specific national-origin group for mass removal represents a use of the statute without precedent in its two-century history outside of declared wars.

### 7.3 Operation Aurora: Documented Execution vs. Announced Ambition

In late January 2025, the Trump administration announced "Operation Aurora," describing Aurora, Colorado as "ground zero" for mass deportations targeting Tren de Aragua gang members. The announced operation involved military preparation at Buckley Space Force Base for use as a detention center.

**Documented execution**: On February 5, 2025, ICE and federal law enforcement conducted multi-location operations in Denver and Aurora at four apartment complexes. More than 100 armed federal agents in 30–40 vehicles, including armored vehicles, surrounded the Cedar Run Apartments at 4 a.m., using smoke grenades and forced entry. Despite the military-scale deployment, approximately **50 people were detained** — a significant gap between the operation's announced scope and documented result.

**The documentation gap**: ICE accused Colorado law enforcement of leaking the operation's details, then deleted the accusation from social media. The gap between announced mass deportation operations and their documented scale reflects a pattern throughout the second term: enforcement operations are announced at a scale designed to generate fear throughout immigrant communities, while actual detention numbers are substantially lower — but the chilling effect on community behavior (withdrawing children from schools, not reporting crimes, avoiding medical care) is achieved regardless.

### 7.4 Birthright Citizenship: The Constitutional Challenge

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order purporting to end birthright citizenship for children born to undocumented or temporary visa holders. Multiple federal judges found the order "blatantly unconstitutional" and issued injunctions.

**Supreme Court intervention on injunctions**: On June 27, 2025, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that federal district courts generally cannot issue nationwide injunctions — but made no ruling on birthright citizenship's underlying constitutionality.

**Ninth Circuit ruling**: In July 2025, the Ninth Circuit became the first appeals court to rule that the executive order was unconstitutional on the merits.

If the Court restricts birthright citizenship, it would create a permanent class of stateless persons born on US soil — individuals who are citizens of no country, ineligible for US citizenship despite birth here, and ineligible for citizenship of their parents' country in most cases.

## Section 8: Employer-Side Enforcement — The Missing Half

### 8.1 E-Verify Coverage and the Agricultural Exemption

The logical counterpart to interior enforcement is employer-side enforcement — ensuring that undocumented workers cannot find employment. The US has never seriously pursued this approach.

**E-Verify is not mandatory**: E-Verify — the federal electronic employment eligibility verification system — is mandatory only for federal contractors and in a minority of states. For the majority of private-sector employers, it remains optional. Participation in agriculture increased from 1,225 employers in 2008 to 8,383 in 2022 — a growth from an extremely low base, not comprehensive coverage.

**Agricultural exemptions are structural**: E-Verify proposals consistently include agricultural exemptions because the agricultural labor market cannot function without undocumented labor at current wage rates and under current legal pathways. Approximately **35% of hired agricultural workers have no legal work authorization** — roughly 680,000 workers. Mandatory E-Verify without a functional agricultural guest worker program would collapse the US food supply.

**The contradiction**: The United States simultaneously uses aggressive interior enforcement to expel undocumented workers while maintaining an agricultural and food processing system structurally dependent on their presence. This is not an accident of policy design — it reflects the preference of agricultural and food industry employers for a workforce that cannot organize, report violations, or demand higher wages.

**E-Verify error rates**: E-Verify produces false positive "no match" results for legal workers — particularly for workers with name discrepancies (common among naturalized citizens, workers with hyphenated names, and workers whose legal names differ from commonly used names). A 2011 Westat report found that approximately **0.8% of authorized workers** received erroneous "no match" results — a small percentage that translates to hundreds of thousands of workers when applied to the full workforce.

## Section 9: International Benchmarks — What Works, With Numbers

### 9.1 Canada: Express Entry and Points-Based Outcomes

Canada's Express Entry system, operational since January 2015, uses a Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) to score candidates on language proficiency, education, work experience, and adaptability, selecting candidates above a score threshold for permanent residence.

**Employment outcomes at 6 months and 2 years**: IRCC's evaluation of the Express Entry program's early impacts found that Express Entry principal applicants earned **20% more than non-Express Entry principal applicants** within the first year of admission. Statistics Canada's 2024 longitudinal analysis documented that recent immigrants' employment rate gap with Canadian-born workers narrowed from **13.1 percentage points in 2010 to 6.5 percentage points in 2023** — a reduction driven primarily by the shift to points-based Express Entry selection. Recent male cohorts of Express Entry principal applicants appear on track to reach earnings parity with Canadian-born workers within five years of landing.

**Earnings relative to Canadian-born workers**: The median income of newcomers admitted through Express Entry-managed programs is higher than that of Canadian-born workers in the same education cohorts. IRCC data for 2016 admission cohorts showed median employment earnings of **$58,400 in year one** (2017) rising to **$76,800 in year five** (2021) — reflecting both experience accumulation and labor market integration. These figures exceed the Canadian national median employment income for the same period.

**The selection effect**: Express Entry replaced a first-come, first-served system with one that prioritizes applicants with the highest expected earnings based on CRS scoring. Statistics Canada's analysis concluded that "the gains are concentrated among university-educated immigrants, especially men, and are most consistent with the effects of Express Entry." The system selected for demonstrated employment capacity; the results show the selection worked.

**2024 Express Entry draw data**: Canada issued **111,301 invitations to apply** through Express Entry in 2024, across general-pool, Canadian Experience Class, Federal Skilled Worker, and category-based draws. The CRS cutoff for CEC draws averaged around 507–534 in late 2024. IRCC met its 6-month processing standard for 80% of applications in 2024.

**Credential recognition as the persistent gap**: The overeducation rate — immigrants employed below their skill level — declined from approximately 40% in 2016 to **27% in 2021**, but this remains a significant structural problem. Express Entry selects for qualifications that Canadian employers then do not always recognize. The system's employment outcomes would improve further with a functioning national credential recognition framework — a gap the Canadian government has identified but not solved.

**Admission scale**: Canada admitted **483,591 permanent residents in 2024**, the highest number since 1972. Facing housing and infrastructure pressure, the government announced a reduction to a 2025 target of **395,000** — a 21% decrease. Canada's immigration system is not without political tension, but it demonstrates that a functional points-based system producing positive employment outcomes at scale is achievable.

**April 2026 Express Entry overhaul**: In April 2026, Ottawa announced a sweeping Express Entry reform that shifts CRS points toward demonstrated earnings and concrete job offers, further tightening the link between selection criteria and actual labor market success. This reform addresses the credential recognition gap by weighting demonstrated Canadian labor market attachment more heavily than educational credentials that may not translate.

**What the US could learn**: The US employment-based system requires an employer-specific offer as the primary pathway. Canada's model allows qualified individuals to enter and find work — not requiring a specific employer before arrival. This produces more flexible labor market matching, better outcomes for skilled immigrants whose credentials may not align precisely with a single employer's needs, and eliminates the H-1B dependency relationship that leaves workers unable to change jobs without losing visa status.

### 9.2 Germany: Skilled Immigration Act 2023 — First-Year Outcomes and Labor Shortage Context

Germany's Fachkräfteeinwanderungsgesetz (Skilled Immigration Act) was substantially reformed in November 2023 (Phase 1) and completed with the "opportunity card" (Chancenkarte) in June 2024. The reform opened Germany's labor market to third-country skilled workers through three pathways: qualification recognition, potential (without pre-arranged employment), and capacity.

**First-year visa issuance numbers** (announced by the German Federal Interior Ministry, November 2024):
- Approximately **198,000–200,000 visas for gainful employment** were issued in the first year — an increase of more than **10% over the previous year** (177,578 in 2023).
- Student visas from outside the EU increased by **20%**.
- Trainee visas increased by **two-thirds** — a category the US lacks almost entirely.
- Recognition-measure visas (for workers pursuing German qualification equivalency) increased by **nearly 50%**.
- **Over 11,000 EU Blue Cards** were issued in 2024.
- **Almost 5,000 Opportunity Cards** (Chancenkarte) were issued in the first months of availability.

**Labor shortage context**: Germany faces a projected shortage of **7 million skilled workers by 2035** — with current vacancy estimates already exceeding 1.7 million unfilled positions. The sectors with the most acute gaps are healthcare (nurses, care workers), IT (software engineers, data specialists), engineering, and skilled trades (electricians, plumbers, construction). The Skilled Immigration Act's three-pathway design reflects the specificity of these shortages: the qualification recognition pathway (for workers with foreign credentials) addresses healthcare and trades; the potential pathway (Chancenkarte, no pre-arranged employer) addresses IT and engineering; the vocational training pathway addresses trades through bilateral agreements with partner countries including India, Brazil, the Philippines, Morocco, and Kenya.

**The political economy of the reform**: Germany passed this reform in a coalition government (SPD/Greens/FDP) against industry lobbying for faster processing and right-wing opposition framing immigration as a security threat — a political dynamic directly parallel to the US. The reform survived because employer associations (including the German Chamber of Commerce and Industry, DIHK) publicly documented the economic cost of unfilled positions — estimated at €150 billion annually in lost output. The US equivalent documentation is less systematically maintained, which is itself an advocacy gap.

**What 200,000 is and isn't**: The 200,000 employment visas issued represent substantial growth from the pre-reform baseline, but falls well short of the 400,000 skilled workers Germany estimates it needs annually to maintain economic capacity. The Skilled Immigration Act is an improvement in the infrastructure for admission, not a complete solution to demographic shortfall. Germany's experience shows that legal pathway reform expands supply from countries with bilateral recognition agreements, but the credentials recognition process remains a significant bottleneck — a lesson for US design.

**Projected impact**: The German federal government estimated the act would allow an additional **60,000 qualified third-country nationals** to enter annually for employment, qualification, or job search — plus **30,000** for the new Chancenkarte. Five years after the original 2020 Skilled Immigration Act, employment-based residence permits have doubled.

### 9.3 Australia: Points System and Albanese Government Reforms (2022–Present)

Australia has operated a points-tested skilled migration program since the 1970s. The Albanese government's 2022–2025 reforms restructured the balance between employer-sponsored and independent skilled migration in ways directly relevant to US design choices.

**Net overseas migration 2022–2025**: Australia's net overseas migration surged to a record **528,000 in 2022–23** — driven primarily by post-COVID backlog recovery and international student arrivals. The government recognized this as unsustainable given housing supply constraints, and reduced NOM to **306,000 in 2024–25** (a 29% reduction), with Treasury forecasting further reduction to **260,000 in 2025–26**. The reduction was achieved through reduced student visa approvals and tighter employer-sponsored visa thresholds, not by cutting the permanent migration program cap.

**Employer-sponsored vs. independent skilled migration balance**: The 2025–26 permanent migration program set **185,000 places**, with:
- **Skill stream**: 70% of total (approximately 132,000 places)
- **Employer-sponsored**: approximately 44,000 places (33% of skill stream) — the largest single category
- **Skilled Independent** (points-tested, no employer): approximately 16,900 — reduced significantly from historical levels of 30,000+
- **State/Territory nominated**: approximately 30,000

The Albanese government's most significant employer-sponsored reform was the **Skills in Demand (SID) visa**, introduced in 2024 to replace the Temporary Skills Shortage (TSS, subclass 482) visa. The SID restructures eligibility around three occupation-tiered streams, with different salary floors and processing speeds depending on the occupation's criticality to labor shortage lists.

**The Albanese migration strategy's explicit goals**: The 2023 Australian Migration Strategy — a five-year framework — stated explicit targets for: reducing temporary migrants' time-to-permanent-residency (the previous system created a large population in prolonged temporary status); protecting temporary migrants' workplace rights to prevent exploitation; and increasing integration support for humanitarian entrants. These goals align directly with the gaps identified in the US proposal — Australia is trying to solve the same problems.

**The $84 billion reform question**: Research commissioned by the Australian government found that **tweaking the allocation of points** to better predict lifetime earnings would increase government revenues by **$84 billion over 30 years**. The current points system allocates weight to characteristics that are poor predictors of actual labor market success — a design flaw that Canada has now identified and is also correcting in its April 2026 Express Entry overhaul.

**The overeducation problem**: More than 50% of graduate visa holders with bachelor's degrees or higher are working significantly below their skill level, even when selecting in shortage occupations. This reflects credential recognition failure — Australia selects qualified people who cannot then get their qualifications recognized by employers. The credential recognition problem is not unique to Australia; it appears in every points-based system that does not pair selection with a mandatory employer-recognition framework.

**Net overseas migration 2023–24 specifics**: Australia saw NOM fall to a 3-year low in 2025 as departures surged — driven by international students leaving after degree completion and temporary visa holders not renewing. This demonstrates that the undocumented accumulation dynamic (the US's 40%+ visa overstay problem) is less severe in Australia in part because the system provides genuine legal pathways for temporary-to-permanent transitions, reducing the incentive to overstay.

**Lesson for reform design**: A points-based system is a selection tool, not a complete immigration system. It performs best when combined with: (1) a functioning credential recognition infrastructure; (2) English language settlement support; (3) housing and public service capacity scaled to intake volume; and (4) a genuine temporary-to-permanent pathway that removes the incentive to overstay. Australia's experience confirms that the integration infrastructure matters as much as the selection mechanism.

### 9.4 Canada's Immigration and Refugee Board: The Independence Model

Canada's Immigration and Refugee Board (IRB) is the direct institutional precedent for Domain 16b's Article I immigration court proposal. The IRB is:

- An independent quasi-judicial tribunal, not part of any ministry or law enforcement agency
- Governed by fixed-term Governor-in-Council appointments
- Structured with four specialized divisions (Refugee Protection, Refugee Appeal, Immigration, Immigration Appeal)
- Insulated from the direction of the Minister of Immigration on individual case decisions

The IRB processes approximately 72,000 refugee protection hearings annually. Its grant rate varies by claimant country — but importantly, it is governed by law and evidence, not by Attorney General direction. Canadian refugee claimants appear before adjudicators who have no institutional stake in the outcome and no supervisory accountability to the agency presenting the case.

This is precisely what current US immigration judges lack. The current Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR) is a component of the Department of Justice — meaning immigration judges answer to an Attorney General who is simultaneously the chief prosecutor of the cases they hear. This structural conflict is not a matter of individual judicial bias; it is an institutional design defect.

## Section 11: Counterarguments — The Three Dominant Objections

### 11.1 "Enforcement Deters Future Illegal Immigration"

The dominant political framing for enforcement-only policy is that sufficiently severe enforcement deters future unauthorized migration, eventually eliminating the problem if pursued consistently enough. This claim deserves precise engagement, not dismissal.

**What the deterrence evidence actually shows**: There is genuine evidence of deterrence at the border level. A 2002 NBER study found that a 10% increase in Border Patrol linewatch hours reduces illegal inflows by approximately 4–8%. Subsequent research confirms that "consequences" policies — criminal prosecution for repeat crossers — reduced recidivism rates among individuals who had previously crossed and been removed. The claim that enforcement has zero deterrent effect is not supported by the research.

**The backfire problem**: However, the most consequential finding in the enforcement-deterrence literature runs in the opposite direction from the deterrence argument. University of Pennsylvania sociologist Douglas Massey's longitudinal analysis of the Bracero program's end and subsequent enforcement escalation found that the unprecedented border militarization of the 1990s and 2000s did not reduce the total undocumented population — it increased it. The mechanism: enforcement increased the cost and risk of circular migration (workers who previously worked seasonally and returned home), which created an incentive to stay permanently rather than risk the dangerous and expensive crossing a second time. What had been a circular flow of male workers going to three states became a settled population of families in 50 states. The enforcement escalation built the undocumented population it was supposed to eliminate.

**Asylum seekers are structurally different from economic migrants**: The research on deterrence effects is most robust for economic migrants. Asylum seekers — who now constitute a substantial proportion of unauthorized arrivals — are not deterrable by enforcement in the same way, because they are fleeing conditions in their home countries that are more threatening than the risks of the border crossing or detention. Raising the cost of crossing does not change the conditions generating flight from violence, political persecution, or climate collapse. This means the enforcement-deterrence argument applies to a declining proportion of the actual flow.

**Diminishing returns at current enforcement levels**: The IZA World of Labor synthesis concluded that enforcement can deter certain types of migration, particularly repeat economic crossing attempts, but that deterrence effects show diminishing returns as enforcement intensity increases — each marginal dollar of enforcement produces smaller deterrence effects. The US has spent over $350 billion on border enforcement since 1986 and has a larger undocumented population now than when it started. This is not evidence that enforcement has zero effect; it is evidence that enforcement without legal pathways scaled to actual demand cannot solve a problem it partially creates.

**The policy implication**: The research supports enforcement as one component of a system that also includes legal pathways scaled to actual demand and a legalization mechanism for the population already here. Enforcement alone, without legal pathways, generates the circular dynamic Massey documents: it raises costs, reduces circularity, expands the settled undocumented population, and eventually produces the conditions for another enforcement escalation. A policy framework designed to solve the problem rather than perpetuate it requires all three components.

### 11.2 The "Rule of Law" Objection — That Legalization Rewards Illegal Behavior

The strongest non-fiscal objection to earned legalization is institutional: that providing a path to citizenship for people who entered illegally signals that laws can be violated without consequences, undermining the rule of law generally and encouraging future illegal entry specifically. Heritage Foundation analysts have called every legalization proposal "amnesty" regardless of its conditions. This framing is rhetorically effective and deserves a precise response.

**The objection's strongest form**: The argument is not simply that individuals should be punished; it is systemic — that any legalization program signals to future migrants that if they enter illegally and remain long enough, a legalization program will eventually follow. The 1986 IRCA legalization, which regularized approximately 2.7 million people, is frequently cited as evidence: the undocumented population grew substantially after 1986, which proponents of this argument attribute to the "signal" IRCA sent. The Heritage Foundation's 2013 analysis argued that an additional "amnesty" would send the same signal and produce a similar result.

**Why the 1986 comparison is misleading**: IRCA legalized without establishing a legal pathway that matched subsequent demand. The agricultural workforce needed more workers than the H-2A program could supply; the technology sector grew faster than the H-1B cap permitted; family reunification backlogs meant legal family members waited a decade or more. The signal IRCA sent was not simply "illegal entry will be rewarded" — it was that there was no legal pathway that matched actual demand. Immigration scholars (Massey, Passel, Cohn) have found that the post-1986 undocumented population growth was driven primarily by IRCA's failure to create legal pathways adequate to actual demand, not by the legalization itself.

**The "earned" distinction matters legally and politically**: Every comprehensive reform proposal since 2001 has required: criminal background checks (disqualifying people with criminal records); continuous residency requirements (typically 5–10 years); back payment of taxes; English language requirements; and a substantial waiting period before citizenship eligibility — typically 11–13 years from legalization to naturalization. NYU law professor Sarah Song's analysis establishes that a statutory legalization with these requirements is not arbitrary; it is a legislative determination that long-term residents have earned membership through demonstrated community ties and compliance with requirements. The rule-of-law objection has less force against a statutory legalization than against an executive discretion program precisely because Congress's determination of eligibility criteria is itself an exercise of lawmaking, not its erosion.

**International precedent on earned legalization**: Countries with earned legalization programs have responded to the rule-of-law objection through design, not avoidance. Spain legalized approximately 700,000 undocumented workers in 2005 through a program requiring at minimum six months of documented employment — the employment requirement served as both a deterrence filter (excluding recently arrived migrants) and a rule-of-law response (demonstrating economic contribution rather than simply rewarding presence). Portugal has conducted multiple legalization rounds. None of these produced the "magnet effect" predicted by enforcement-only advocates in the years immediately following the programs.

**The rule-of-law objection applied to the current system**: The rule-of-law argument cuts both ways. The current system applies law selectively — enforcing against low-wage workers while leaving employer-side enforcement essentially toothless. An immigration system where employers who knowingly hire undocumented workers face minimal consequences while workers face detention and deportation is not a rule-of-law system; it is a system that uses law instrumentally to maintain labor market exploitation. The rule-of-law argument requires consistent application to employers, not only to workers.

### 11.3 The Wages Objection — Borjas vs. Card, and What the Methodological Resolution Means

Section 2.3 summarized the Card-Borjas wage debate. This section deepens the methodological resolution and what it means for reform design — the debate is still live in public discourse and advocates need to be equipped to engage it precisely.

**Borjas's specific critique of Card**: In a 2017 paper, Borjas directly reanalyzed Card's Mariel Boatlift data and found — contrary to Card's original conclusion — that the Boatlift did depress wages for high-school dropouts in Miami by approximately 10–30%. Borjas argued that Card's original analysis had aggregated skill groups in ways that obscured the wage effects on the lowest-skill workers who competed most directly with the Cuban arrivals. The methodological dispute is technical but substantive: the "no wage effect" finding is not robust to Borjas's disaggregation.

**The response from Peri and Sparber — task specialization**: UC Davis economist Giovanni Peri and Columbia economist Chad Sparber developed the task-specialization framework as the theoretical resolution. Their 2009 American Economic Journal paper found that native workers and immigrant workers specialize in different tasks even within the same occupation: immigrants specialize in manual-physical labor tasks; native workers shift toward communication-language tasks. Because they are not perfect substitutes in production, immigration does not suppress native wages in the way that a simple labor supply model predicts — immigrants expand the task frontier rather than simply competing for the same jobs. This mechanism explains the Card finding without requiring that Borjas's disaggregation is wrong.

**The 2025 meta-analysis finding**: A 2025 meta-analysis in the Journal of Development Economics reviewing the extensive literature concluded that "the consensus is very strong that there are not significant costs to U.S.-born workers from immigration at current levels." The meta-analysis found that Borjas-framework analyses tend to find larger effects because they hold local labor market adjustment constant — they assume that native workers do not respond to immigration by specializing in different tasks or moving to higher-productivity activities, which is the mechanism the evidence shows actually occurs.

**The policy-relevant implication**: Borjas's finding — even if accepted at face value — identifies the mechanism as competition between low-skill immigrant workers and low-skill native workers without high school diplomas. The policy implication is not restriction but labor market reform: when all workers have enforceable rights, can report violations, can organize, and compete on documented wages rather than exploitable shadow-economy rates, the wage suppression mechanism Borjas identifies disappears. The solution to whatever wage competition exists at the bottom of the labor market is robust labor law enforcement — which Domain 17 of the proposal addresses — not restriction. This is the most important single takeaway from the wage debate for advocates: immigration reform and labor reform are functionally interdependent; each makes the other more effective.

**What advocates should say**: When confronted with the Borjas wage argument, the honest response is: (a) the evidence is methodologically contested, with most recent work finding small to negligible effects; (b) the theoretical resolution identifies task specialization as the mechanism that explains why Card's findings hold; (c) even accepting Borjas's findings, the policy solution is labor law enforcement that protects all low-wage workers, not immigration restriction that leaves the labor market structure unchanged.

Sources: [Peri and Sparber: Task Specialization, Immigration, and Wages, American Economic Journal (2009)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.1.3.135) | [EPI: Immigration and Wages — Methodological advancements confirm modest gains for native workers](https://www.epi.org/publication/bp255/) | [NBER: Immigration and National Wages (Peri)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14188/w14188.pdf) | [Cato Institute: Does Immigration Reduce Wages?](https://www.cato.org/cato-journal/fall-2017/does-immigration-reduce-wages)

Parallel to the data litigation: the SHIELD Act of 2025 (H.R. 3101) — which would establish a federal grant program for immigrant legal representation — is in committee and needs constituent pressure to force markup. The representation gap (67% of people in deportation proceedings lack counsel; people with representation are 10.5 times more likely to achieve a favorable outcome) is a winnable political argument in jurisdictions with large immigrant populations.

The birthright citizenship case (*Trump v. Barbara*) is before the Supreme Court with a ruling expected June–July 2026. This is the most consequential single decision pending. Advocacy organizations should be preparing public communication and litigation fallback strategies for both outcomes.

### AI-2: Legislative Vehicles (119th Congress, 2025–2026)

**DREAM Act of 2025 — S. 3348**
Introduced in the 119th Congress Senate. The DREAM Act would provide a path to permanent residence and eventual citizenship for individuals brought to the United States as children — the DACA-eligible population and beyond. The bill covers the approximately 525,210 active DACA recipients plus additional individuals who would be eligible under the DREAM Act's terms but are not currently DACA-eligible (including those who aged out, those in states with blocked DACA renewal, and those who could not meet DACA's continuous residence requirements). Floor prospects in the current Congress are low under Republican majority, but the bill is the vehicle for generating co-sponsor lists and constituent pressure records that will be essential in any future trifecta. The 2021 House-passed version (H.R. 6) passed 228-197 with nine Republican co-sponsors — the cross-partisan vote record is the primary organizing asset.

Reference: [S. 3348 — Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/3348)

**Farm Workforce Modernization Act of 2025 — H.R. 3227**
Introduced in the 119th Congress. The bill would create an earned legal status pathway for agricultural workers through a Certified Agricultural Worker (CAW) status — a renewable temporary status leading to permanent residence after additional work requirements. H-2A visa reform provisions modernize the guest worker program to better match actual agricultural demand, including allowing year-round crops (currently excluded from H-2A) and reducing the application timeline. The bill passed the House twice in previous Congresses (117th and 118th) with bipartisan margins, including Republican co-sponsors from agricultural districts — making it the most politically viable near-term pathway legislation in the current environment. Agricultural employer associations (American Farm Bureau Federation, Western Growers, National Council of Agricultural Employers) have historically supported versions of this legislation because their member businesses need the labor market stability it provides.

Procedural posture: Referred to House Judiciary Committee. The political case is that agricultural employers in Republican-held districts have a direct economic stake in its passage.

Reference: [H.R. 3227 — Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3227/text)

**SHIELD Act of 2025 — H.R. 3101**
The Securing the Homeland through Immigrant Legal Defense Act would create a grant program for organizations providing legal representation to people in immigration removal proceedings. Introduced with Vera Institute backing and aligned with the universal representation initiative. The representation gap is documented precisely: 67% of all people in deportation proceedings lack counsel; fewer than 5% of unrepresented immigrants achieve favorable outcomes. The SHIELD Act addresses this through federal grant funding for legal defender organizations, modeled on state-funded universal representation programs currently operating in New York, California, New Jersey, and Illinois.

Reference: [Vera Institute: The SHIELD Act of 2025 Summary (H.R. 3101)](https://vera-institute.files.svdcdn.com/production/downloads/The-Shield-Act-of-2025-Summary.pdf)

**FY2027 DHS Appropriations — The Annual Leverage Window**
The FY2027 Department of Homeland Security appropriations process (subcommittee markups expected spring–summer 2026, full committee action summer, floor votes fall) is the annual window for:
- **Alternatives to Detention funding**: The FY2026 DHS appropriations bill (Section 224, HAC) mandated ATD enrollment for all individuals on the non-detained docket with mandatory GPS monitoring — this expands ATD through mandate rather than appropriation. FY2027 advocates should push for adequate ATD funding ($4.20–$14/day per participant vs. the $164.65/day ICE detention bed rate) to ensure the mandate is implementable without inadequate community supervision.
- **Immigration court funding**: The 3.8 million case backlog and 5,286 cases per judge average (7x the NAIJ's recommended 700) requires judge hiring and court infrastructure. Every additional immigration judge approved in appropriations reduces the backlog and improves due process outcomes. The FY2027 vehicle is the State, Justice, Commerce, and Related Agencies appropriations bill, which funds EOIR.
- **Legal representation grants**: The SHIELD Act grant program mechanism can be incorporated into appropriations riders even if the standalone bill does not advance.

### AI-3: Administrative Pathways — What Requires Legislation vs. Executive Action

**What can be done by executive action alone**:
- **Deferred action programs**: The DACA precedent established that the executive can grant temporary deferred action to categories of individuals without congressional authorization, subject to court challenge. The Supreme Court has allowed DACA to continue for existing recipients while its full scope remains in litigation. A future administration can expand deferred action to additional populations (DAPA-eligible individuals, long-term residents without criminal records, TPS holders whose designations lapse) through executive action. The constraint is not legal authority — it is political will and litigation risk.
- **Parole in place**: The military parole-in-place program (allowing military family members to apply for legal status without leaving the US) demonstrates that parole authority can be used for categorical humanitarian protection. A future administration could restore and expand categorical parole programs (CHNV, Uniting for Ukraine) through executive action without legislation.
- **Prosecutorial discretion and deportation prioritization**: ICE's civil enforcement prioritization memoranda — which the Trump administration revoked in 2025 — can be restored by executive action, directing ICE to focus resources on individuals who pose genuine public safety risks rather than people with decades of community ties and US citizen family members.
- **TPS designations**: The Secretary of Homeland Security has statutory authority to designate and extend Temporary Protected Status for countries with ongoing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or extraordinary conditions. The Trump administration has revoked or allowed to expire TPS designations that a future administration can restore.
- **Expanded legal representation access**: DHS can direct EOIR to provide access to legal orientation programs, expand pro se clinics at immigration courts, and contract with nonprofit legal service providers — without congressional authorization.

**What requires legislation**:
- Any permanent path to citizenship for the undocumented population requires a statutory basis — the courts have ruled that sustained deferred action without a statutory pathway does not confer permanent legal status.
- Expanding the employment visa caps (H-1B, H-2A, EB-1 through EB-5) requires legislation.
- Creating an independent immigration court (Article I status, insulation from DOJ direction) requires legislation restructuring EOIR.
- Ending mandatory detention for asylum seekers requires amending the INA's mandatory detention provisions.
- Establishing a right to government-provided counsel in immigration proceedings requires legislation (unlike in criminal proceedings, the Sixth Amendment does not apply to immigration courts).

**The administrative action sequencing argument**: For advocates, the distinction between what requires legislation and what requires only executive action defines the strategy in a divided government period. The highest-value administrative actions (restore prosecutorial discretion, expand parole, restore TPS designations, expand ATD) can be implemented without the congressional majorities that comprehensive reform requires. The legislative vehicles (DREAM Act, Farm Workforce Modernization Act) are worth pursuing for the co-sponsor record and political positioning even when passage is not immediately achievable.

### AI-4: Sanctuary Policy — Legal Basis, What It Does and Doesn't Do, and Current Status

**What sanctuary policies actually are**: Sanctuary policies are local and state laws or administrative policies that limit the extent to which local law enforcement cooperates with federal immigration enforcement. They typically prohibit local police from asking about immigration status during routine encounters, limit or prohibit compliance with ICE detainer requests (which ask local jails to hold individuals beyond their release date pending ICE pickup), and restrict sharing of information from local databases with federal immigration authorities. They do not prohibit federal immigration enforcement in the jurisdiction — ICE agents can operate in sanctuary cities, they simply cannot use local police as a force multiplier.

**The constitutional basis — anti-commandeering doctrine**: Sanctuary policies rest on the Tenth Amendment's anti-commandeering doctrine, established in *Printz v. United States* (1997) and *New York v. United States* (1992). The federal government cannot commandeer state and local officers to enforce federal programs. Immigration law is federal law; its enforcement is a federal responsibility. Local police are not obligated to function as federal immigration officers. This constitutional principle has been upheld consistently by courts when applied to sanctuary policies. On July 25, 2025, a federal district court upheld the sanctuary laws of Illinois, Cook County, and Chicago, holding they are protected by the Tenth Amendment's anti-commandeering doctrine. The case was closed with prejudice on August 26, 2025, after the federal government declined to amend its complaint.

**What sanctuary policies do and don't do**: Sanctuary policies do not prevent ICE from arresting individuals in the community — they prevent local jails from doing ICE's administrative work. The practical effect is that ICE must conduct community arrests (more resource-intensive, higher risk of targeting errors) rather than picking up people from local custody. Research has consistently found that sanctuary policies are not associated with higher crime rates — multiple peer-reviewed studies have found no significant difference in crime outcomes between sanctuary and non-sanctuary jurisdictions. The practical effect of sanctuary policies is that immigrants in these jurisdictions are more likely to report crimes to police (because they do not fear that a traffic stop leads to deportation), which improves public safety outcomes for everyone.

**The federal pressure campaign's current status**: The Trump administration's Executive Order 14159 (January 2025) directed the Attorney General and DHS Secretary to identify and publicly report sanctuary jurisdictions and pursue civil and criminal actions. The administration has:
- Threatened to withhold federal funding from sanctuary jurisdictions (DOJ, DHS grants)
- Filed civil lawsuits against sanctuary jurisdictions in multiple states
- On April 24, 2025, a federal judge blocked the Trump administration from withholding federal funds from 16 sanctuary cities and counties
- Moved to advance the Shut Down Sanctuary Policies Act through the House Judiciary Committee, which would preempt local sanctuary laws

**Current legal landscape**: Multiple courts have blocked specific elements of the federal pressure campaign. The anti-commandeering doctrine remains the primary constitutional shield. The federal funding threat is legally the most viable pressure point — while the government cannot commandeer local officials, it can impose conditions on grants. The courts have so far blocked the broadest funding conditions as unconstitutional coercion (as in the first Trump term *City of Chicago v. Sessions* ruling), but the litigation is ongoing.

Sources: [Brennan Center: Federal Efforts to Punish Sanctuary Cities Are Unconstitutional](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/federal-efforts-punish-sanctuary-cities-are-unconstitutional) | [National Immigration Forum: Are Sanctuary Policies Unlawful?](https://forumtogether.org/article/are-sanctuary-policies-unlawful-state-and-municipal-prerogatives-to-collaborate-with-federal-immigration-authorities/) | [Smart Cities Dive: Federal funding at risk as House advances anti-sanctuary bill](https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/federal-funding-at-risk-house-advances-anti-sanctuary-bill/814318/)

### AI-5: Organizations — Specific Roles and Engagement Mechanisms

**National Immigration Law Center (NILC)**
The primary legal and policy organization focused on the rights of low-income immigrants. NILC does not provide direct client representation — its function is policy analysis, impact litigation, legislative advocacy, and publishing the authoritative legal memos that practitioners use to advise clients. NILC's "Know Your Rights" materials are the primary resource for community education on enforcement interactions. In 2025–2026, NILC has been a lead litigant in multiple cases challenging the Trump administration's data-sharing arrangements and enforcement practices.

URL: [nilc.org](https://www.nilc.org/)

**Catholic Legal Immigration Network (CLINIC)**
The primary national network for nonprofit immigration legal service providers, with a network of affiliated agencies that provided direct legal representation to **over 500,000 immigrants per year**. CLINIC trains legal representatives, accredits practitioners for "recognition and accreditation" status (allowing non-attorneys to provide representation in immigration proceedings), and publishes the state-and-local immigration tracker that is the primary resource for monitoring subnational policy developments. CLINIC's practitioner network operates in communities that major law firms and large advocacy organizations do not serve — rural areas, smaller cities, and faith-based communities. For advocates seeking to connect clients or communities with legal services, CLINIC's network locator is the primary tool.

URL: [cliniclegal.org](https://www.cliniclegal.org/) | State/local tracker: [CLINIC Immigration Tracker](https://www.cliniclegal.org/resources/state-and-local/immigration-tracker-state-and-local-edition-february-2026)

**Vera Institute of Justice — Advancing Universal Representation Initiative**
Vera's immigration work focuses on universal legal representation — the proposition that every person facing deportation should have counsel, funded by government if necessary. Vera's SAFE Network has launched 18 universal representation programs in 11 states. Vera's dashboard tracks legal representation rates by jurisdiction, which is the primary public data source for the representation gap. Vera's SHIELD Act is the legislative vehicle for federal universal representation funding. Vera also publishes systematic documentation of the administration's due process record — their "Weaponizing the System" report (2026) is the primary one-year accountability document.

URL: [vera.org/advancing-universal-representation-toolkit](https://www.vera.org/advancing-universal-representation-toolkit) | Dashboard: [Immigration Court Legal Representation Dashboard](https://www.vera.org/ending-mass-incarceration/reducing-incarceration/detention-of-immigrants/advancing-universal-representation-initiative/immigration-court-legal-representation-dashboard)

**ACLU Immigrants' Rights Project**
The primary litigation organization for constitutional challenges to immigration enforcement. The ACLU has been lead or co-counsel in most of the significant immigration enforcement challenges of 2025–2026: Alien Enemies Act deportation challenges, IRS-ICE data sharing challenges, family separation follow-on litigation, and wrongful deportation mandamus proceedings. The ACLU's legal strategy document for the second Trump term is the most comprehensive public analysis of the litigation landscape and the constitutional vulnerabilities of current enforcement practices.

URL: [aclu.org/issues/immigrants-rights](https://www.aclu.org/issues/immigrants-rights)

**American Immigration Council (AIC)**
The primary producer of quantitative evidence on immigration enforcement economics — their mass deportation cost analysis ($315 billion one-time, $88 billion annually for one million deportations/year), per-deportation unit cost analysis ($17,121), and Fortune 500 immigrant-founder documentation are all AIC products. AIC also conducts impact litigation and policy advocacy. Their 2025 end-of-year impact report documents their litigation and advocacy activities during the first year of the second Trump term. AIC's research is not advocacy in the conventional sense — it is the most cited non-governmental quantitative source in congressional testimony on immigration economics.

URL: [americanimmigrationcouncil.org](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/)

**National Immigration Forum**
The cross-partisan coalition-building organization on immigration — their explicit model is building coalitions that include faith communities, law enforcement, national security professionals, and business. The Forum's value is not research production but trusted-messenger access: a pastor, a retired police chief, or a chamber of commerce executive can reach audiences that immigration advocates cannot. The Forum publishes fiscal and economic analysis specifically designed for conservative and moderate audiences — their immigration detention costs analysis (citing $164.65/day bed rate, $17,121 per deportation) is available in formats designed for law enforcement and business audiences.

URL: [immigrationforum.org](https://immigrationforum.org/)

**National Immigration Justice Center (NIJC)**
Chicago-based direct legal services provider with a national footprint, providing representation to asylum seekers, torture survivors, unaccompanied children, LGBTQ+ immigrants, and detained individuals. NIJC's specific strength is asylum law — their legal team includes the country's most experienced asylum practitioners and produces the practitioner training resources on asylum law that other organizations use. NIJC also monitors and documents conditions in detention facilities.

URL: [immigrantjustice.org](https://immigrantjustice.org/)

### AI-6: 2026 Pressure Points — Courts, Appropriations, and Administrative Windows

**Window 1 — Supreme Court Birthright Citizenship Ruling (June–July 2026) [MOST CONSEQUENTIAL]**
The Court's ruling in *Trump v. Barbara* will be the most significant single immigration decision since *Arizona v. United States* (2012). The April 2026 oral argument showed a majority likely to rule against the administration on the merits — but the ruling's scope matters as much as the outcome. A narrow ruling that strikes down the specific executive order without reaffirming birthright citizenship's constitutional basis leaves the question open for future challenge. Organizations to watch for rapid legal response: ACLU, National Immigration Law Center, American Immigration Council. Advocates should prepare both the celebratory narrative (constitutional reaffirmation) and the cautionary one (narrow ruling, work remaining).

**Window 2 — IRS-ICE Data Sharing Litigation (Ongoing)**
Two federal courts have issued injunctions blocking or limiting the IRS-ICE data sharing MOU:
- Judge Kollar-Kotelly (DC): November 2025 ruling that the MOU likely violated IRS Code Section 6103 (taxpayer confidentiality); blocked the arrangement under APA.
- Judge Talwani (Massachusetts): February 2026 ruling barring DHS, ICE, and agents from inspecting, using, or distributing tax return information obtained under the MOU, citing data accuracy problems (IRS verified only ~47,000 of 1.28 million names ICE requested; only 5% received address data).
- Judge Friedrich (DC): Denied a preliminary injunction in a separate challenge, allowing data sharing to continue temporarily.

The government is appealing both blocking injunctions. The circuit court decision — expected in the Fourth Circuit or DC Circuit in 2026 — will determine whether interior enforcement can be built on IRS taxpayer data. This litigation directly intersects with the data-privacy evidence in `domain-deepening/data-privacy-evidence.md`. The IRS's admission that it illegally shared taxpayer data (documented by Democracy Forward in an ongoing case) creates additional liability exposure for the arrangement. Advocates should monitor Democracy Forward's litigation and the ACLU's parallel filing.

Sources: [FedScoop: Federal judge blocks ICE from using IRS taxpayer data](https://fedscoop.com/judge-blocks-irs-ice-data-sharing-agreement/) | [Democracy Forward: IRS Admits It Illegally Shared Taxpayer Data with ICE](https://democracyforward.org/news/press-releases/irs-admits-it-illegally-shared-taxpayer-data-with-ice-bringing-new-urgency-in-ongoing-case-to-protect-americans-sensitive-information/)

**Window 3 — Wrongful Deportation Mandamus Cases (Active)**
Multiple federal courts have active proceedings involving individuals wrongfully deported or detained:
- *Abrego Garcia v. Noem* (Fourth Circuit): Kilmar Abrego Garcia, deported to CECOT in defiance of Supreme Court order, remains imprisoned as of April 2026. The Fourth Circuit's contempt proceedings against the administration for non-compliance with the Supreme Court's "facilitate return" directive are the most active frontal challenge to executive non-compliance with judicial orders. This case is tracking the question of whether executive defiance of Supreme Court immigration orders triggers enforceable consequences.
- IRS-ICE wrongful targeting: Judge Talwani's February 2026 ruling specifically cited evidence that the data system produces errors that could result in "wrongful arrests of legal residents and US citizens" — creating a record of specific legal harm from the IRS-ICE arrangement that will be essential in future damages litigation.
- US citizens detained or deported: The PBS NewsHour documentation of US citizens wrongly detained in the Trump administration's immigration crackdown represents the most legally and politically vulnerable category of enforcement error. Federal courts have uniformly found that US citizens cannot be subjected to immigration detention or removal proceedings. Cases involving documented US citizens subjected to immigration enforcement are the highest-value litigation targets for organizations with the capacity to bring them.

Reference: [Time: The People Judges Say Trump Has Wrongly Deported So Far](https://time.com/7297802/trump-administration-wrongful-deportations/) | [PBS NewsHour: American citizens wrongly detained in Trump administration's immigration crackdown](https://www.pbs.gov/newshour/show/american-citizens-wrongly-detained-in-trump-administrations-immigration-crackdown)

**Window 4 — FY2027 DHS and Commerce-Justice-Science Appropriations (Summer–Fall 2026)**
The FY2027 DHS appropriations bill is the annual vehicle for alternatives-to-detention funding, immigration court judge hiring, and legal representation grant programs. Specific provisions worth pushing:
- ATD program funding at levels adequate to implement the FY2026 enrollment mandate without inadequate supervision resources
- Immigration court judge hiring: the backlog requires substantial new judge appointments; every appropriations cycle without adequate judge hiring adds months to case timelines
- Legal orientation program funding at all detention facilities — currently operated through contract by the Executive Office for Immigration Review and subject to defunding by directive

The Commerce-Justice-Science appropriations bill funds the DOJ's Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR) — the immigration court system. EOIR's budget request and the appropriated amount are the primary determinant of court capacity. Advocates should push for EOIR funding at a level that permits substantial judge hiring above current baseline.

**Window 5 — Farm Workforce Modernization Act (H.R. 3227) — Agricultural Employer Pressure**
The Farm Workforce Modernization Act is the most politically viable legislative vehicle in the current Congress because its primary constituency includes Republican-aligned agricultural employers. The strategic action is constituent pressure from agricultural employers in Republican-held districts who can speak to their members of Congress about the actual labor market consequences of enforcement without a legal pathway. American Farm Bureau Federation chapters, Western Growers members, and state-level farm bureaus are the relevant pressure groups. The bill's House passage in two previous Congresses with Republican co-sponsors demonstrates that the cross-partisan coalition is buildable — the task is activating agricultural employers who have been passive supporters into active constituent pressure.
